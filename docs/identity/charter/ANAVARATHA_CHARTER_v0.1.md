# Anavaratha

## Project Charter

**Version 0.1 — 27 August 2026 — Personal foundational reference**

---

## Preamble

The name **Anavaratha** draws from Sinhala, carrying a resonance of continuity — unbroken, ongoing, ceaseless. What the project takes from the name is that working resonance: this is not a single intervention or a finished object, but an ongoing practice of clearing, restoring, cultivating, and caring — continuity itself as the discipline.

> **Anavaratha is a Sri Lanka–focused material-cycle and ecological-continuity project with three linked domains: Freedom from Waste, Material Afterlives, and Ecological Flourishing. It studies how materials, institutions, people, and ecosystems preserve or lose capability through cycles of use, disuse, circulation, transformation, residuality, and regeneration — and it does so through disciplined desk-first research before any claim of readiness to act.**

This charter is the ratifying document for that identity. It exists to hold the project steady across time — so that whichever domain is being worked on in a given month, the whole shape of the thing stays legible.

---

## 1. Purpose

Anavaratha holds three necessary movements together, from the start, as one. One is the removal of what restricts or damages life: unmanaged waste, pollution, contamination, ecological neglect. One is the recognition of what still has use in the interval before a thing is discarded — the relationships, repairs, and exchanges that keep material meaningfully alive before it becomes waste. One is the active cultivation of what allows life to flourish: soil, water, trees, biodiversity, habitat, knowledge, stewardship. None of the three precedes the others; each is a full direction of attention in its own right.

> **Freedom from ecological harm, the circulation of what remains useful, and freedom for ecological flourishing are three faces of the same continuity — and none is reached by ignoring the other two.**

Sri Lanka is not a site where a universal model gets applied. It is the living context the project has to learn from: distinct ecological zones, watersheds, settlements, institutions, histories, and unevenly distributed environmental burdens.

The project is not exempt from what it studies. Holding three domains of research together — coherent, evidence-graded, connected without collapsing into each other — is itself a thermodynamically costly act: an effort to maintain organization, memory, and differentiation against the ordinary pull toward scatteredness, abandonment, and half-finished work that this project elsewhere finds to be the default condition. Anavaratha is, in that sense, a small negentropic structure in its own right, and its three domains are not separate projects but the three subdomains through which that single sustained effort is currently being focused.

## 2. Inspirations

Anavaratha did not begin from an abstract theory. It draws, in different ways, from a small constellation of references, each contributing something distinct rather than being treated as the same kind of evidence.

Frédéric Back's animated film *The Man Who Planted Trees*, adapted from Jean Giono's story, gives the project an ethic of slow, unseen, cumulative care — the idea that a landscape can be transformed not by spectacle but by patient repetition over decades. Jadav Payeng's real forest at Molai, grown from years of solitary planting on a river island in Assam, offers the same patience as lived fact rather than fiction — and a caution alongside it: his restored habitat also created real friction and cost for the people living near it, a reminder that ecological work must be answerable to the communities around it, not only to the forest itself.

Biomimicry contributes a method more than an aesthetic: asking how a healthy soil community builds fertility, how a forest holds water, how diversity creates resilience — before reaching for a technical answer. Vandana Shiva's work through Navdanya keeps the project from reducing ecology to landscaping or efficiency, insisting on seed sovereignty, living soil, and the political question of who actually controls land, seed, and knowledge. Ecosia offers a smaller, stranger lesson: an ordinary repeated action, connected transparently to a larger purpose, can accumulate into real ecological leverage at scale.

Charlie Kaufman's *Adaptation.* (2002) sits apart from the others — not ecological evidence but a methodological discipline borrowed from its form. Its self-aware, uncertain, endlessly revising relationship to its own subject is the posture Anavaratha tries to hold toward its own maps: provisional, humble about what representation can capture, willing to be wrong and to say so.

> **The map is not the living world, but the map changes the conditions under which the living world is seen.**

None of these references carries the same evidentiary weight. A film is not a data source, and a data source is not a movement. What they share is a role: each keeps some part of Anavaratha honest — about patience, about consequences, about method, about politics, about scale, about its own uncertainty — that the domains' evidence tables alone cannot hold.

## 3. Foundational Discipline

Every domain begins with the same sequence, applied without exception:

> **Desk research → sense-making → knowledge-building → relationship mapping → uncertainty review → proportionate decision about action**

This is not a rigid pipeline. New evidence, a contradicted source, a seasonal observation, or an ethical concern can send any domain back to an earlier stage. No domain is permitted to skip ahead to intervention because it feels urgent or visible.

**Shared grammar** — every domain uses these terms, weighted differently by its own concerns:

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Material | Matter with a source, composition, quality, location, history, possible use or burden |
| Flow | Movement of material, energy, money, labour, information, responsibility, or ecological effect |
| Stock | Material, value, burden, knowledge, or ecological condition accumulated over time |
| Boundary | The spatial, temporal, institutional, and material limits of an analysis |
| Capacity | What a functioning arrangement can actually receive, transform, contain, or sustain — not what it was designed to |
| Dissipation | Specified loss of quality, potential, information, or containment — never a single universal score |
| Afterlife | A subsequent phase of use, repair, exchange, recovery, transformation, or residuality |
| Receiver | A person, community, worker, institution, ecosystem, or future generation experiencing a flow or burden |
| Evidence status | Observed, measured, estimated, reported, planned, historical, disputed, inaccessible, or unknown |

**Maturity model** — shared across domains, filled with different evidence in each:

| Level | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Mapped | The relevant system, geography, actors, and broad conditions are identified |
| Characterised | Components, histories, relationships, capacities, and pressures are reasonably described |
| Connected | Dependencies, feedbacks, and unintended effects are understood across scales |
| Testable | A proportionate hypothesis and learning design can be stated responsibly |
| Actionable | Authority, capacity, partners, safeguards, and resources are sufficient for a defined next step |

"Actionable" never means merely urgent. It means the knowledge and conditions are adequate for a responsible action of a stated scale.

## 4. Thermodynamic Grounding

Anavaratha treats Sri Lankan settlements and facilities as open systems — not as a metaphor but as their literal shape: material, energy, labour, information, and ecological services flow in; degraded materials, residuals, emissions, burdens, and information gaps flow out. Thermodynamics contributes discipline in five areas — conservation, boundary definition, quality, irreversibility, and unavoidable throughput — but it does not supply a theory of what is good. That judgment stays with the project's other frames: material-flow analysis, industrial ecology, political ecology, environmental justice, and the capability approach.

Each domain inherits this grounding differently:

| Domain | Where dissipation shows up |
|---|---|
| Freedom from Waste | Uncontrolled or harmful dissipation — mixing, leakage, degradation, residual formation, exposure, burden export |
| Material Afterlives | Loss of function, separability, information, and repairability before quality is dissipated |
| Ecological Flourishing | Whether living organization is maintained and regenerated under material and energy flow, or degrades under it |

"Negentropy" is used in this project only as a cautious metaphor — for maintained differentiation, information, memory, feedback, repair, and organization. It is not a literal substance sitting in a database, an object, an institution, or a forest. Thermodynamic entropy, information-theoretic entropy, material dispersal, organizational dissipation, and social injustice are related concerns, not identical quantities, and the charter keeps them distinguishable rather than collapsing them into a single score.

## 5. The Three Domains

The three domains describe three directions within the same underlying movement of ecological resources — through use, circulation, residuality, and regeneration. They are three layers of one lifecycle rather than three separate projects, and each is held as fully co-equal. Their current evidence bases differ, as described below, but that reflects where research attention currently sits — not a hierarchy among them.

### Domain I — Freedom from Waste

**Primary question:** How can involuntary exposure to destructive residual burdens be reduced without simply transferring those burdens to less visible people, places, or ecosystems?

**Objects of study:** waste generation and collection, transfer, treatment, residuals, disposal, informal dumping, open burning, leachate, unsafe work, compliance-reality gaps, burden transfer.

**Current evidence status.** It carries real Sri Lankan data: the IWMI 20-city waste inventory (2020, city-level service baselines) and a 2025 peer-reviewed study of 22 Western Province compost stations with matched design-versus-actual figures — 2,775 tons/month aggregate design capacity against only 157 tons/month actual production. The capacity-chain method (generation → capture → transfer → reception → conversion → product quality → market → residuals → institutional persistence) is fully specified and has already surfaced a structural finding: Sri Lanka's core problem is not simply insufficient infrastructure but the absence of a public, current, comparable evidence system for what installed facilities actually do. That gap — the CEA's Pollution Source Inventory currently redirects to a login page — is itself now a documented finding rather than an obstacle. Next priority: a national facility-capacity reality audit, built as an event table rather than a static list.

### Domain II — Material Afterlives

**Primary question:** When does "no longer needed by me" become "available for another," and what allows or prevents that value from circulating?

**Objects of study:** used goods, repairable objects, surplus institutional assets, informal exchange, repair economies, reuse depots, matching infrastructures — everything with latent utility before it becomes waste.

**Current evidence status.** The domain's scaffolding is conceptual: eight circulation models compared (direct reuse, resale, sharing, repair, remanufacture, institutional redistribution, reuse centres, product-as-service), positioned against established frameworks (the EPA's waste hierarchy, the Ellen MacArthur Foundation's circular economy model, Singapore NEA's donation/resale/repair gateway). Sri Lanka-specific evidence is still to be gathered — the research treats this as leads requiring verification, not established fact. Ten provisional operating principles guide the domain: disuse is not uselessness; circulation is not automatically good; trust is infrastructure; labour must be visible; the boundary with waste must stay visible. It stands as a full domain within Anavaratha, on equal footing with the other two.

### Domain III — Ecological Flourishing

**Primary question:** What material, institutional, and social conditions allow ecological systems to regenerate, adapt, and sustain the capabilities of living communities?

**Objects of study:** forests, wetlands, waterways, soils, habitats, ecological corridors, restoration sites, biodiversity, environmental stewardship — and, within it, the Osu Uyana inquiry into Sri Lankan medicinal-garden traditions.

**Current evidence status.** It carries a wide research infrastructure: eight domain papers (ecological regions, forests, soil/water, biodiversity, wetlands, agroecology, urban cultivation, stewardship), a living-sector taxonomy descending from landscape to organism to relationship to care, a biocultural priority framework, and a source-map/crosswalk system connecting existing Sri Lankan catalogues rather than duplicating them. Cultivation Atlas Release 01 demonstrates the method at small scale: 11 controlled records, including a well-evidenced Yellow-eared Bulbul frugivory relationship in Horton Plains and a deliberately preserved discrepancy in a 2025 medicinal-plant checklist (1,476 reported vs. 1,349 conservatively extracted). The Osu Uyana inquiry keeps a careful three-way separation: the contemporary Sinhala usage of the term is confirmed; Sri Lanka's broader history of managed medicinal and monastic gardens is well supported; but a direct institutional continuity from an ancient named "Osu Uyana" to any present garden remains unverified and is not asserted. The domain is mapped and characterised, partially connected — not yet testable at any named site.

## 6. How the Domains Relate

The three domains form a field, not a ladder:

```text
                    ECOLOGICAL FLOURISHING
               regeneration / living capability
                              ▲
                              │
                care / restoration / recovery
                              │
    MATERIAL AFTERLIVES ◄──► FREEDOM FROM WASTE
   reuse / repair / exchange     residual burdens / exposure
        before disposal          and failed containment
```

A reusable object may stay in Material Afterlives, become waste, or return to a new afterlife through repair. Composting reduces a waste burden while opening a possible nutrient relation to ecological systems. Restoration itself requires materials and labour that carry their own afterlives and residuals. The bridge between domains is not "circularity" as a slogan — it is **accountable transition**:

> **A material transition is beneficial only when it preserves or creates use, reduces burdens, protects ecological capability, and does not conceal or export new residuals.**

**Boundary rules**, applied to every record, source, or proposed action:

- Every record states a single primary domain.
- A cross-link to another domain is permitted, but explicit: it must name the relationship, its evidence, its geography, its time horizon, and its confidence.
- A connection is never assumed simply because two things both touch "the environment."
- Each domain keeps its own material, capability, burden, knowledge, time, and persistence ledgers. A combined view may connect them only through documented flows.

---

## 7. What Anavaratha Does Not Claim

This charter does not claim, and no domain within it should be read as claiming: a complete Sri Lankan waste, species, or medicinal-plant catalogue; medical advice; a species-selection or garden-design prescription; a marketplace design; a restoration plan; proof that any intervention, in any domain, is ready to begin. Its purpose is to preserve a careful, honestly-graded foundation from which later research and, eventually, proportionate action can proceed.

---

## 8. Operating Principle

> **Understand before acting. Reduce the burdens that obstruct life. Cultivate the conditions where life can continue. Recognise the value still latent before something becomes waste. Keep the three domains distinct in their questions and evidence, and connect them only where the evidence itself shows they meet.**

## 9. Status and Provenance

This is version 0.1 of the charter, dated 27 August 2026 — the date this document was written, not the date the project began. The ideas underlying Anavaratha took shape over at least the year before this charter; this document ratifies a shape that had already been forming, rather than founding it. It formally establishes the three-domain architecture — Freedom from Waste, Material Afterlives, Ecological Flourishing — as Anavaratha's governing structure, each domain co-equal as a direction within the project's resource-flow and lifecycle model. The underlying research across all three domains was produced through AI-directed desk research (Manus AI) under Neev's direction as architect and product owner. This charter itself does not add new evidence; it fixes the identity, boundaries, and honest maturity state of the project as a personal foundational reference, to be revised as a new version when the domains' evidentiary balance materially changes.
