# Anavaratha Desk Review Round 1 Baseline

## Executive synthesis

The first desk review suggests that Sri Lanka’s waste problem should be understood as a **system-fit problem** rather than a single failure of household behavior, collection, technology, or policy. Across the reviewed sources, waste pressure interacts with uneven physical, operational, financial, human, institutional, political, market, and informational capacity. The binding constraint is likely to vary by local authority, population scale, settlement pattern, waste stream, time period, and receiving location.

The central working proposition is:

> **Waste-system performance depends on the fit between material pressure, usable capacity, institutional coordination, and pathway reliability across time and space.**

The current evidence is sufficient to design the next research cycle, but not sufficient to publish a single current national mass balance. Existing values differ in year, geography, definition, method, and reporting purpose. Anavaratha should preserve these differences rather than averaging them into false precision.

## 1. What the first round has established

Sri Lankan policy and institutional documents frame waste management increasingly through resource circulation, 3R principles, circular economy, prevention, public health, ecosystem protection, and stakeholder responsibility.[1] [2] [3] The National Action Plan on Plastic Waste Management covers the lifecycle from importation and processing through use, collection, recovery, recycling, and disposal, and includes targets related to source separation, collection, recycling, health and safety, community participation, and monitoring.[3]

The reviewed research repeatedly identifies structural constraints: inadequate or poorly allocated finance, insufficient land, limited vehicles and facilities, inadequate technical expertise, weak regulation, low staffing or labor productivity, limited political or social support, irregular collection, and weak source separation.[4] [5] [6] [7] These findings should not be interpreted as a universal checklist. They are candidate mechanisms whose importance may change by local-authority scale and context.

The plastic action plan adds an important chain perspective. Unsorted and soiled post-consumer plastic may lose resource value unless it is cleaned and graded, and recycling depends on collection, quality, facilities, markets, and downstream demand.[3] Earlier initiatives, including the Pilisaru programme, are described as falling short partly because the approach was not sufficiently holistic or inclusive, separated collection was unreliable, final treatment and disposal facilities were inadequate, and some facilities were not operated effectively.[3]

The evidence also indicates that waste burdens and recovery value are spatially distributed. Separated materials may move to other regions where markets or processing capacity exist.[6] A local authority can therefore appear cleaner while transferring residuals, costs, risks, or value to another location. Anavaratha must map origins, pathways, destinations, and receiving burdens rather than mapping only collection or disposal sites.

## 2. The layered waste-system model

| Layer | Main analytical question |
|---|---|
| Material pressure | What materials enter, circulate, become waste, or accumulate? |
| Generator and activity | Which households, businesses, markets, institutions, or visitors create the pressure? |
| Population and denominator | What population or activity unit should normalize the pressure? |
| Service capacity | What collection, transport, and access capacity exists and is actually usable? |
| Facility capacity | What can recovery, composting, transfer, landfill, or disposal facilities receive and process? |
| Institutional capacity | Who has the mandate, finance, skills, data, and authority to act? |
| Social and political conditions | What shapes trust, participation, leadership, conflict, and enforcement? |
| Market capacity | Are recovered materials wanted, affordable to transport, and accepted at required quality? |
| Flow and stock | What moves through the system, and what remains accumulated? |
| Spatial distribution | Who receives the burden or benefit, and where? |
| Time | How do daily, seasonal, project, political, and long-term changes affect the system? |
| Outcome | What changes in service, exposure, recovery, cost, health, ecology, and trust? |

This structure makes the project proportional to real conditions. The relevant question is not only “How many tonnes?” but “How much pressure exists relative to usable capacity, for whom, where, and during what period?”

## 3. Failure-mechanism taxonomy

| Mechanism | Desk interpretation |
|---|---|
| Design mismatch | National or provincial models do not fit the size, density, land, finance, or route geometry of a local authority |
| Coordination failure | Policy, operations, enforcement, facility management, and data remain disconnected |
| Physical capacity gap | Land, vehicles, bins, facilities, equipment, or roads are insufficient or unavailable |
| Recurrent-finance gap | Capital or project investment exists without reliable operation and maintenance finance |
| Human and technical gap | Staff numbers, skills, training, safety, or data competence are insufficient |
| Collection-pathway failure | Irregular schedules, missed service, mixed loads, or poor route design weaken later recovery |
| Separation and quality failure | Materials are not separated, are remixed or contaminated, or cannot meet buyer requirements |
| Market and destination failure | Recovery lacks viable buyers, prices, aggregation, processing, or transport |
| Political and regulatory failure | Leadership, enforcement, incentives, or political intervention undermine implementation |
| Social connection failure | Awareness, trust, convenience, participation, and community contribution are weak or poorly designed |
| Spatial transfer failure | Burden is shifted to disposal edges, neighboring authorities, waterways, workers, or future periods |
| Temporal persistence failure | Outcomes weaken after funding, attention, staffing, or maintenance changes |
| Data-system failure | Definitions, periods, units, boundaries, and reporting are not compatible or continuously maintained |

## 4. What can be compared now

The first round supports qualitative comparison across source contexts. Older studies can reveal recurring mechanisms. Recent policies can reveal intended goals and institutional assumptions. Provincial and local studies can show how those assumptions meet capacity conditions. Database studies can reveal whether knowledge infrastructure is itself persistent and usable. International tools can provide a measurement architecture.

The first round does not yet support directly combining all reported waste quantities, collection rates, or plastic-flow estimates. Values must be harmonized by reference period, boundary, waste definition, denominator, and method before ratios are calculated. The National Action Plan baseline, for example, is valuable for reconstructing the policy evidence base, but its estimates must retain the plan’s historical period and definitions.[3]

## 5. Priority desk hypotheses

The following hypotheses should guide the next desk cycle:

1. **Policy–scale fit:** local-authority performance varies partly because standardized approaches do not match population, density, geography, land, and finance.
2. **Capacity mismatch:** collection and disposal problems occur where material pressure exceeds usable vehicles, staff, routes, facilities, finance, or land.
3. **Pathway reliability:** source separation produces value only when collection, sorting, quality control, destinations, markets, and residual management work together.
4. **Spatial transfer:** improvements at the point of generation can transfer burdens to disposal sites, neighboring authorities, waterways, informal workers, or future time periods.
5. **Temporal persistence:** interventions that work during externally supported project periods may not persist under ordinary operating conditions.
6. **Informal visibility:** formal records undercount recovery performed by informal actors and may misclassify where materials actually go.
7. **Data as capacity:** poor definitions, missing updates, and fragmented records limit not only analysis but also institutional ability to manage waste.
8. **Organic recovery conditions:** organic diversion depends on contamination, processing, land, transport, output quality, demand, and maintenance—not merely on the existence of composting technology.

## 6. The next desk-review work package

The next round should focus on extracting page-level claims and comparable variables from the National Plastic Action Plan, the IWMI 20-city report, the Western Province database evaluation, the CEA policy material, and selected local-authority studies. Each claim should be coded by location, scale, population/activity denominator, period, waste stream, capacity, constraint, actor, failure mechanism, outcome, evidence grade, and transferability.

The output should be a **Desk Baseline v0.2** containing a source-comparison table, a candidate-location profile, a provisional flow map, a capacity-pressure matrix, an uncertainty register, and a list of field observations that could change a real decision. Fieldwork should remain deferred until the desk analysis identifies specific high-value unknowns.
