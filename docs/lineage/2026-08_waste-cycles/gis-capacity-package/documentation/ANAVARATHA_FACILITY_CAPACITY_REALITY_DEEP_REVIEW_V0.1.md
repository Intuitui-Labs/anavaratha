# Anavaratha Facility-Capacity Reality in Sri Lanka — Deep Review v0.1

**Status:** Desk-based research synthesis; current as of 22 August 2026. **Author:** Manus AI.

## Executive finding

Sri Lanka has many waste-management assets and projects, but the available evidence does not support treating installed facilities as equivalent to functioning capacity. The strongest recent operational evidence is a 2025 open-access study of 22 local-authority compost stations in Western Province using January–December 2022 data. It reports 2,775.0 tons/month of aggregate design capacity and 2,391.3 tons/month of running capacity, but only 157.0 tons/month of actual compost production and 142.4 tons/month of sales.[1] The resulting evidence pattern is not simply “there is insufficient infrastructure.” It is that **capacity is lost, distorted, or transferred across the chain** through mixed feedstock, weak weighing and records, long processing times, absenteeism, space constraints, odour and leachate, weak markets, limited quality testing, maintenance gaps, social opposition, and residual disposal.

This review therefore defines facility capacity as a chain of linked realities rather than a single number.

> **A facility exists physically. Capacity exists operationally only when the facility can reliably receive a defined material stream, process it to a usable quality, move or sell the output, contain the residuals, protect workers and receiving environments, and sustain the pathway over time.**

## Evidence hierarchy and currentness

| Evidence class | What it can establish | What it cannot establish alone |
|---|---|---|
| Current weighbridge, operating log, utility, maintenance, inspection, and financial records | Measured operations at a defined facility and period | National prevalence without comparable coverage |
| Dated government inspection/licence/project records | Formal status, approvals, observed deficiencies, planned/design values | Actual daily throughput if not measured |
| Independent field survey with site observations | Physical and operational conditions at survey time | Current condition after the survey date |
| Peer-reviewed multi-facility survey | Comparable operational patterns and variables | Current status of unsurveyed or changed facilities |
| Government master plan or project document | Intended network, design assumptions, targets, project capacity | Delivered, sustained, or safe operation |
| Media or NGO case report | Leads, events, reported failures, public concerns | Legal conclusion or national prevalence without primary records |
| Anecdote | Verification lead | Fact about location, date, legality, or present condition |

The newest directly relevant operational study located in this review was published in September 2025 but used 2022 station averages. The 2026 Pivithuru Purawara announcement is newer but is programme and investment evidence, not an operational inventory.[5] No publicly downloadable, current, national, comparable solid-waste facility register with actual throughput was located through the CEA public portal; the CEA-linked Pollution Source Inventory redirects to a login page.[6]

## The capacity chain

| Chain node | Reality test | Failure signature |
|---|---|---|
| Generation and composition | Is the incoming quantity and stream defined and measured? | Estimated generation, mixed waste, no weighing, incompatible denominators |
| Capture and collection | Does material enter the intended service pathway? | Partial coverage, missed routes, seasonal surges, difficult roads |
| Transfer and transport | Can material reach the facility at required frequency and cost? | Vehicle downtime, fuel shortage, long distance, transfer bottlenecks |
| Reception and sorting | Can the facility accept and separate its feed? | Contamination, manual sorting burden, no source separation |
| Process conversion | Is the treatment process controlled? | Long cycles, poor aeration, leachate, odour, low conversion |
| Product quality | Is the output safe and marketable? | No laboratory testing, weak standards, buyer distrust |
| Market and cost recovery | Can output be sold or otherwise valued? | Unsold stock, low demand, no sales strategy, weak revenue |
| Residuals and receiving environment | Are rejects, ash, leachate, and non-recoverables safely contained? | Open dumping, ash uncertainty, water/soil/forest exposure |
| Institutional persistence | Does the system continue after launch or funding? | Expired maintenance, absent staff, idle equipment, unimplemented recommendations |

## Latest operational evidence: 22 Western Province compost stations

The 2025 study reports that all 22 stations operated 25 days per month. The reproduced station table is supplied as `ANAVARATHA_MANIPURA_2025_WESTERN_PROVINCE_22_STATIONS.csv`. It contains design capacity, running capacity, facility area, actual production, employees, sales, and derived diagnostics.

| Aggregate indicator | Reported value | Interpretation |
|---|---:|---|
| Design capacity | 2,775.0 tons/month | Nominal station design values |
| Running capacity | 2,391.3 tons/month | Reported running capacity, not actual output |
| Running/design | 86.17% | Aggregate source-table comparison |
| Actual compost production | 157.0 tons/month | Reported product output |
| Production/running | 6.57% | Very large gap; may reflect boundary, measurement, feedstock, process, and data-quality issues |
| Compost sales | 142.4 tons/month | May include previously unsold stock |
| Sales/production | 90.70% | Not a simple same-month production-to-sales ratio |
| Employees | 254 | Reported total, not attendance or skill capacity |
| Stations below running design | 14 of 22 | Source-table classification |
| Stations above running design | 7 of 22 | May indicate overloading or differing definitions, not success |
| Stations equal to design | 1 of 22 | Does not establish quality or persistence |

![Capacity reality diagnostics](ANAVARATHA_MANIPURA_2025_CAPACITY_REALITY_DIAGNOSTICS.png)

The aggregate numbers reveal a critical measurement distinction. Running capacity is close to design capacity, but actual compost production is much smaller. A facility may therefore be “running” in the sense of having a nominal processing potential while producing little saleable compost. The study reports organic-waste conversion between 1% and 15%, process completion of 2.5–4.5 months, and no observed standard operating procedure. It reports excess leachate and odour at 59% of stations and poor data management that prevents meaningful benchmarking.[1]

The study also reports the following constraints: low perceived compost demand at 82% of stations, high absenteeism at 82%, space constraints at 77%, sorting time from poor household segregation at 36%, and limited funding at 32%. More than 80% of stations reported high absenteeism. Although personal protective equipment was reported as available, it was not always used; health checks and OHS training were not evident in many stations. Only one station had weighing facilities for daily incoming waste, and seven of 22 local authorities maintained sales outlets.[1]

These are not marginal administrative details. They are mechanisms that convert a nominal asset into under-utilized, unsafe, unmeasured, or financially fragile capacity.

## Station-level diagnostic patterns

The station-level data show that “over design” and “high output” are not equivalent. Minuwangoda, Mathugama, and Millaniya report running capacity above design but actual production below 2% of running capacity. By contrast, Ja Ela UC and Divulapitiya report lower running capacity relative to design but higher actual production relative to running capacity. These contrasts should be investigated through feedstock definitions, measurement practices, cycle time, staffing, storage, and market conditions rather than ranked as good or bad facilities.

| Diagnostic question | Example result | Required verification |
|---|---|---|
| Is running capacity realized as product? | Seventeen stations fall below 10% of running capacity in the extracted table | Weigh incoming organic material, measure product and rejects, reconcile mass balance |
| Does exceeding design mean better performance? | Seven stations exceed reported design running capacity | Check overloading, design definition, actual operating days, safety, quality, and stock accumulation |
| Does sales mean current production? | Some stations sell more than they produce in the table | Track opening stock, production, sales, closing stock, and unsold accumulation |
| Does staffing equal labour capacity? | Employees range from 4 to 38 across stations | Record attendance, skills, training, task assignment, absenteeism, and contractor status |
| Does equipment presence equal usable capacity? | Equipment is reportedly available, but maintenance, weighing, and process controls vary | Record uptime, MTBF, MTTR, fuel, spare parts, operator hours, and actual use |

## Countrywide evidence beyond Western Province

Western Province is the best-documented recent operational case, but it is not the only evidence base. The 2016 JICA national survey contains local-authority and facility observations from multiple provinces, including Anuradhapura, Badulla, Batticaloa, Colombo, Galle, Jaffna, Kandy/Kurunegala, Kilinochchi, Mannar, Matale, Matara, Mullaitivu, Negombo, Nuwara Eliya, Puttalam, Ratnapura, Trincomalee, and Vavuniya. The archived IWMI 20-city inventory provides a separate multi-province service and facility dataset. These records are retained as `operating_historical`, not silently merged with the 2022 Western Province study.[8] [9]

The World Bank Emergency Solid Waste Management Project framework provides a national-period context from 2018. It reports government estimates of 6,000–7,000 tons/day of national solid-waste generation and approximately 250 operational open-dump sites, including 25 in Western Province at that time. It identifies historically reported facility roles including the WMA’s 500 tons/day controlled dumpsite at Karadiyana, CEA’s Dompe sanitary landfill, and a planned 500 tons/day Kerawalapitiya composting facility. These are dated project-period records and do not establish current operation.[10]

The JICA/CEA ReEB Waste project provides further multi-province evidence from 2017–2019. Its documented scope included disposal-site rehabilitation, compost-plant improvement, leachate management, source separation, water-quality sampling, and pilot monitoring in local authorities including Kurunegala, Ratnapura, Kataragama, and Western Province sites. It is especially useful for studying whether improvements persisted after a project ended.[11]

## Historical, closed, inactive, and transition records

Historical status is not a reason to discard a facility. It is evidence about persistence. The JICA 2016 survey records that the Trincomalee main dumping area was about 1 km inside forest, with waste also dumped along the access road during rainy seasons when vehicles could not use the unpaved muddy road. From 1 October 2015, management and maintenance had reportedly been handed to a private company for preliminary rehabilitation and proposed recycling/waste-to-energy development. The source records the transition and early works but does not establish the later outcome.[8]

The same survey records a composting facility at Sundarapola associated with Kurunegala Municipal Council, designed for up to 25 tons/day. Its sorting conveyor had not been installed; private operation was discontinued on 18 June 2014; and KMC was gradually restarting the plant from October 2014. This is a `discontinued_then_restarted` historical status event, not current operation.[8]

The Auditor General’s report provides a later official example in clinical waste. It records idle and repeatedly failing Meta Mizer treatment equipment, expired maintenance contracts, accumulated treated waste, unresolved transport, weak pathogen verification, incomplete licensing, and recommendations that remained unimplemented through October 2024.[12] These records demonstrate why the national register must retain `idle_or_suspended`, `closed`, `abandoned_or_failed`, and `rehabilitation_or_upgrade` events alongside operating records.

No record has been coded `closed` merely because it is old or absent from a newer source. A closure label requires an explicit closure source or a clearly documented cessation event. Where no later evidence exists, the correct label is `operating_historical` or `status_unknown`.

## Design, operational, and market capacity are different variables

The 2025 study’s supplementary questionnaire is a near-complete template for Anavaratha’s current facility survey. It asks for design and running capacity, capital investment, funding, operating days, population, collection coverage, segregation, ward-level tonnage and distance, vehicle payloads, labour, feedstocks, tipping fees, functional areas, roof and concrete floor, machinery hours and power, leachate containment, process monitoring, cycle time, quality testing, failure factors, monthly income and expenditure, training and OHS, marketing, customer demand, and digital complaints/payments.[2]

Its proposed KPI framework includes a General Facility Management Index, Composting Process Management Index, Compost Quality Index, and Cost Recovery Index. The framework is analytically useful but should not be applied as a national score until the underlying measurements are standardized. Some proposed formulas depend on guidelines and benchmarks that the authors say still need to be developed. Anavaratha should therefore store the raw indicators first and calculate indices only after testing reliability, comparability, and sensitivity.

## Planning and project-capacity reality

The CEA’s review of the Western Province Solid Waste Master Plan provides an important design-side perspective. It records a proposed increase in waste capacity from 750 tons/day to 1,500 tons by 2028 and a proposed increase in Gampaha waste-collection coverage from approximately 43–45% to 70%, with more vehicles and service expansion to underserved areas.[3] These are plan targets, not delivered outcomes.

The CEA review-response also records that Aruwakkalu sanitary-landfill Phase I and II design life was 12.5 years at full capacity, whereas an earlier report had used 20 years. It reports that the Western Power waste-to-energy project was planned around 800 tons/day for 20 years but, in the context described, received approximately 700 tons/day, affecting electricity generation. The ash-disposal pathway for fly ash and bottom ash was flagged as a concern.[3]

The CEA recommendations are unusually important because they criticize the plan’s analytical basis. They state that the SEA largely justified the plan rather than assessing it nationally, did not adequately analyse alternatives or existing projects, lacked a proper situation/SWOT/PESTLE analysis, focused heavily on thermal recovery, and did not sufficiently address fly-ash and bottom-ash disposal. They also warn that abundant thermal-recovery capacity could discourage waste reduction and source separation if mixed waste is sent directly to the facilities.[4]

The planning lesson is clear: **a large design number can create new system dependencies and residual burdens if feedstock, ash, maintenance, finance, and alternative pathways are not demonstrated.**

## Official audit evidence: installed clinical-waste equipment did not equal capacity

The Auditor General’s Triennial Environmental and Performance Audit 2021–2023, reporting implementation status up to October 2024, extends the facility-reality analysis beyond municipal composting.[7] It found that a national clinical-waste plan and a 48-hour disinfection mechanism had not been implemented, and that annual environmental-protection licensing by hospital authorities had not achieved satisfactory progress.

In a sample of nine hospitals, the cluster system had not been implemented in eight hospitals. Sterilized/incinerated waste accumulated on hospital grounds because transport arrangements had not been prepared. Maintenance contracts for Meta Mizer machines had expired and the machines were not being used at the time, while hospitals used alternatives. The Ampara machine had been idle since establishment, and machines elsewhere were repeatedly out of service. At Chilaw, an oil shortage caused frequent stoppages and an operator disconnected a hydraulic sensor to continue operation. At Marawila, sharp waste was burned on hospital premises and unburned syringe and plastic fragments remained on hospital grounds.

The audit also found that treated material was not independently confirmed as pathogen-free, local authorities sometimes refused treated waste, and material accumulated on hospital grounds. Recommendations remained unimplemented as of October 2024. This is a direct public-audit demonstration that **procured equipment, funded projects, and installed machinery do not equal safe, maintained, available, or accepted capacity**.

## 2026 programme: latest but still planned

The Pivithuru Purawara National Waste Management Programme was reported on 6 August 2026 as a 2026–2029, three-phase national programme coordinated through the Ministry of Public Administration, Provincial Councils and Local Government and the Clean Sri Lanka National Programme. The report states that LKR 30 billion had been allocated and that the programme would address collection, disposal, recycling, household composting, legal action, dumping reports, coastal waste, medical waste, and port waste.[5]

This should enter Anavaratha as a **planned-programme record** with fields for announced budget, responsible actor, planned activities, procurement, delivery, commissioning, operating budget, measured output, outcome, and persistence. It must not be added to existing capacity totals until project-level evidence shows what was built, where, when, at what capacity, and with what operating record.

## Analytical rules

Do not create one national “capacity score” by adding compost, landfill, waste-to-energy, clinical-waste, recycling, and collection values. Their units, streams, periods, and system boundaries differ. Do not compare design capacity with product output unless the input/output boundary and unit are compatible. Do not interpret sales as production without stock reconciliation. Do not interpret equipment presence as uptime. Do not interpret a project budget as delivered capacity. Do not interpret a licence as compliance in practice. Do not interpret a court or audit finding as national prevalence.

The first useful national outputs should therefore be: a facility currentness matrix; a design-versus-running-versus-output table; a service/catchment table; a maintenance and uptime table; a finance-and-market table; a legal/reality status table; and a data-completeness map. Only after these are populated should facility records be joined to population, roads, hydrology, forest, protected-area, and waste-flow layers.

## Source documentation and access barriers

The machine-readable catalogue `ANAVARATHA_NATIONAL_FACILITY_CAPACITY_SOURCE_CATALOGUE.csv` contains 20 source records with provider, publication date, data period, geographic scope, facility domain, status role, evidence grade, public URL, local archive, what the source establishes, what it cannot establish, and access date. The review package also retains downloaded source PDFs, supplementary documents, extracted text, and derived tables where available.

The CEA public portal currently exposes a Solid Waste Management Unit, guidelines, annual-report links, GIS/wetland acquisition leads, and a Pollution Source Inventory link.[6] [13] The Pollution Source Inventory redirects to a login page, and the CEA 2023 annual-report link failed to retrieve from the linked legacy endpoint during this review. These are documented access barriers, not evidence that the underlying records do not exist. A formal information request is required for current national facility, licence, inspection, throughput, maintenance, finance, and status tables.

## Current conclusion

The best-supported conclusion is not that Sri Lanka lacks waste facilities. It is that **Sri Lanka lacks a publicly accessible, current, comparable evidence system showing which facilities are usable, how much they actually process, how reliably they operate, whether outputs are safe and marketable, and where residual burdens go**.

That evidence gap is itself part of the capacity problem. If incoming waste is not weighed, if output is not tested, if downtime is not recorded, if stock is not reconciled, if licenses and inspections are not linked to coordinates, and if projects are not tracked after commissioning, then the system cannot know its own usable capacity. Anavaratha’s next stage should therefore be a **national facility-capacity reality audit**, not a simple facility map.
