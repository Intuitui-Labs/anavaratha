# Anavaratha Desk Review Round 1: Source Library

## Reading purpose

This is a starting corpus for waste-focused sense-making in Sri Lanka. It is not intended to be an exhaustive bibliography. Sources are selected because they help explain system structure, failure mechanisms, capacity mismatches, spatial transfer, time dependence, data quality, or intervention design.

## Initial failure mechanisms extracted

The first reading round identifies the following mechanisms as recurring candidates rather than settled conclusions:

| Mechanism | How it appears in the corpus |
|---|---|
| Policy–scale mismatch | National or provincial models do not fit different local-authority sizes, densities, land conditions, or finances |
| Fragmented responsibility | Policy, operations, enforcement, and data are separated across institutions without sufficient coordination |
| Physical capacity gap | Insufficient land, vehicles, bins, facilities, equipment, or usable processing capacity |
| Recurrent-finance gap | Capital or project support exists without sustainable operating and maintenance finance |
| Human and technical gap | Insufficient staff, skills, training, safety, or data competence |
| Collection-system weakness | Irregular schedules, missed areas, mixed loads, or poor route/service design undermine later recovery |
| Source-separation breakdown | Separation is weak, remixed, contaminated, or not supported by a trustworthy downstream pathway |
| Facility and market mismatch | Recovery facilities or outputs are not matched to quality requirements, buyers, or transport economics |
| Political and governance interference | Political leadership, public commitment, enforcement, or allocation decisions affect implementation |
| Social and behavioral disconnection | Awareness, trust, convenience, participation, and business/community contribution are treated as secondary |
| Spatial burden transfer | Visible cleanliness or local improvement shifts residual burden to another facility, community, waterway, or future period |
| Temporal non-persistence | Improvements weaken after project funding, attention, staffing, or maintenance changes |
| Data-system failure | Definitions, periods, units, boundaries, and reporting practices prevent reliable comparison or accountability |

## First-round synthesis

The evidence does not support a single-cause explanation of Sri Lanka’s waste problems. The repeated pattern is a **chain failure**: pressure grows; local capacity is uneven; policy design is insufficiently differentiated; collection and separation are unreliable; recovery markets and facilities are incomplete; residuals are transferred or accumulated; and data systems do not consistently reveal the full pathway.

The main desk-review task is therefore to reconstruct the chain for different local-authority scales and contexts. The key question is not simply “How much waste is there?” It is:

> **Where does pressure exceed usable capacity, which link fails first, who controls it, who receives the burden, and how does that relationship change across time and space?**
