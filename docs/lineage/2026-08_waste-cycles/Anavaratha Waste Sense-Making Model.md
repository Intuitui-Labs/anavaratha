# Anavaratha Waste Sense-Making Model

## 1. The object of analysis

Anavaratha should analyze waste as a **dynamic system under conditions**, not as a single quantity. The same amount of waste can produce different outcomes depending on population density, land availability, collection capacity, route geometry, institutional arrangements, market access, seasonality, and the location of receiving burdens.

A useful analytical statement is:

> **Waste outcome = material pressure × system conditions × time × spatial arrangement × institutional response.**

This is not intended as a literal predictive equation at the beginning. It is a discipline for asking what variables must be considered before interpreting an outcome.

## 2. Recommended layers

| Layer | Unit of analysis | Core question | Example variables |
|---|---|---|---|
| Material | Material stream | What is flowing? | Organics, plastics, paper, glass, metals, residuals |
| Generator | Source type | Who or what creates the flow? | Households, shops, markets, institutions, tourism, industry |
| Population and activity | People and activity units | How many people or activities create demand? | Population, households, establishments, visitors, seasonal activity |
| Service | Route and service area | What service reaches whom, when, and how? | Coverage, frequency, vehicle capacity, missed service, cost |
| Facility | Node | What can the facility receive, process, recover, or contain? | Capacity, inflow, technology, staffing, maintenance, control level |
| Actor and institution | Organization or group | Who has responsibility, power, knowledge, cost, or exposure? | Authority, contractor, worker, recycler, resident, regulator |
| Capacity | Capability of the system | What can the system actually do? | Land, finance, staff, equipment, data, governance, market |
| Constraint | Limiting condition | What prevents performance or creates trade-offs? | Land, funding, politics, behavior, distance, contamination, law |
| Flow and stock | Movement and accumulation | What moves, what is recovered, and what remains? | Inflow, outflow, residual, stock, leakage, accumulation |
| Space | Location and relation | Where are burdens and benefits distributed? | Density, distance, drainage, waterways, roads, receptors |
| Time | Period and sequence | How does the system change? | Daily, seasonal, project, election, economic, long-term trend |
| Outcome | System effect | What changed or persisted? | Cleanliness, exposure, cost, recovery, pollution, trust, resilience |

## 3. Capacity should be multidimensional

Do not reduce capacity to the number of trucks or the size of a budget. A system can have vehicles but no fuel, land but no social license, staff but no data, or a facility but no market for its outputs.

| Capacity type | Meaning | Desk evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Physical | Space, vehicles, bins, roads, facilities, equipment | Asset lists, maps, facility reports |
| Operational | Ability to schedule, collect, maintain, sort, process, and monitor | Route plans, schedules, downtime, staffing records |
| Financial | Ability to fund recurrent service and maintenance | Budgets, fees, contracts, cost records |
| Human and technical | Skills, staffing, safety, engineering, data competence | Staff structures, training, technical plans |
| Institutional | Clear mandates, coordination, accountability, decision authority | Laws, plans, organizational charts, meeting records |
| Political and social | Leadership support, legitimacy, participation, conflict resolution | Policy continuity, public engagement, complaints, political decisions |
| Market | Demand and viable destinations for recovered materials | Buyer records, prices, quality requirements, transport |
| Informational | Timely, compatible, trusted data | Databases, reporting frequency, metadata, audit trails |
| Ecological receiving capacity | Ability of land and water systems to absorb or recover without harmful overload | Wetland, waterway, soil, flood, and land-use evidence |

## 4. Constraints are relational

A constraint is not simply a missing resource. It is a relationship between demand and available capacity under a particular context. A vehicle shortage is more severe on long dispersed routes than on compact routes. A disposal site can be adequate for a small flow but inadequate after population growth or seasonal inflows. A recycling market can be viable near a city and uneconomic from a remote settlement.

Represent constraints as:

> **Constraint = demand or pressure − usable capacity, under a defined time and spatial condition.**

The word “usable” matters. A nominal facility capacity may not be usable if the facility is closed, inaccessible, contaminated, politically contested, or unable to process the relevant material.

## 5. Population and proportionality

Use both absolute and normalized measures. Absolute quantities are necessary for infrastructure and total burden. Proportional measures help compare places of different size. However, denominators must match the activity that creates the waste.

| Context | Preferable denominator examples |
|---|---|
| Residential waste | Person, household, dwelling, or served population |
| Commercial waste | Establishment, employee, floor area, sales proxy, or operating day |
| Market waste | Stall, vendor, market day, visitor, or tonne handled |
| Tourism-related waste | Guest-night, visitor, occupied room, or peak-day activity |
| Collection service | Served household, route kilometre, stop, tonne, or population |
| Facility operation | Tonne received, operating day, staff member, or capacity |
| Hotspot risk | Area, road length, drainage catchment, nearby population, or receptor |

Per-capita indicators should not be used to imply that residents are solely responsible for waste. They are analytical denominators, not moral judgments.

## 6. Time and accumulation

Waste is both a flow and a stock. Daily generation is a flow. A waste mound, landfill cell, clogged drain, or contaminated site is a stock that persists and may grow. A desk model should therefore distinguish:

- **Flow variables:** tonnes per day, collection trips, recovered material, burning events, leakage events.
- **Stock variables:** accumulated volume, remaining landfill capacity, persistent hotspot extent, stored recyclables, contaminated soil or water.
- **Transition variables:** opening, closure, maintenance, policy change, seasonal shift, disaster, price change, or project end.

At minimum, each source should be assigned to a snapshot, seasonal period, intervention cycle, or structural trend. The model should ask whether a project’s outcome persisted after funding, staffing, or political attention changed.

## 7. Spatial reasoning

Map origin, pathway, destination, and receiving burden. Do not map only facilities. A waste-management system can appear efficient at the collection point while transferring burden to a landfill edge, downstream waterway, neighboring authority, informal worker community, or future generation.

Use nested geographies: national, province, district, local authority, settlement, route, facility, hotspot, and environmental receptor. When exact coordinates are sensitive, use generalized grids or administrative units.

## 8. Multi-perspective reading

Every major claim should be tested through at least five perspectives:

| Perspective | What it reveals |
|---|---|
| Household or generator | Convenience, cost, information, trust, choices, and behavior |
| Worker or informal actor | Hidden recovery, risk, livelihood, dignity, and practical knowledge |
| Operator | Routes, equipment, staffing, safety, schedules, and maintenance |
| Authority and regulator | Mandate, finance, land, enforcement, politics, and accountability |
| Receiving community and ecology | Exposure, inequality, drainage, soil, water, habitat, and long-term burden |

A sixth perspective can be added for markets and downstream users, especially where recovery depends on quality, prices, and transport.

## 9. Sense-making questions

For every location and period, ask: What is the pressure? What capacity is available? What constraint binds first? Which actor controls the relevant decision? What pathway does the material take? Where does the burden or value arrive? What changes with population, season, distance, or policy? What is measured, inferred, contested, or unknown?

## 10. Practical desk output

The first desk analysis should not produce a single national score. It should produce a **layered system profile** for each candidate location: material pressure, population/activity denominator, service and facility capacity, binding constraints, flow and stock conditions, spatial burden distribution, time pattern, actor perspectives, evidence quality, and unresolved hypotheses. These profiles can later guide proportional comparison and targeted field validation.
