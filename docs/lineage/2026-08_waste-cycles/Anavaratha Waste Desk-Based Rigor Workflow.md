# Anavaratha Waste Desk-Based Rigor Workflow

## Operating principle

The first round is a **sense-making cycle**, not a data-collection race. The aim is to understand the existing knowledge landscape, identify recurring mechanisms, compare how they vary by place and scale, and determine what evidence is strong enough to guide the next question.

## Round 1: Build the evidence universe

Create a source library containing policy documents, laws, national and provincial plans, local-authority plans, project reports, academic papers, books, datasets, evaluations, documented failures, public complaints, and relevant media investigations. Do not treat all sources as equal. Record author, institution, year, location, method, intended audience, and likely incentives.

## Round 2: Extract claims, not just summaries

For each source, extract specific claims such as “collection is irregular,” “separation is not sustained,” “facility capacity is insufficient,” or “the intervention failed after project support.” Each claim should be tied to a page, section, table, or direct source location where possible.

## Round 3: Code the system conditions

Code each claim against the following dimensions: waste stream; location; spatial scale; population or activity denominator; time period; actor; capacity type; constraint; intervention; failure mechanism; outcome; evidence type; evidence strength; and transferability.

## Round 4: Compare proportionally

Compare absolute and normalized measures together. A larger local authority may collect more tonnes but have lower collection per person. A smaller authority may have fewer vehicles but shorter routes. A facility may receive less waste but still be overloaded relative to its usable capacity. Proportional comparison must preserve the denominator and activity context.

## Round 5: Identify patterns and exceptions

Look for recurring patterns across sources, but actively search for exceptions. If several studies say awareness is the main issue, check whether collection reliability, downstream recovery, or trust explains the behavior. If a technology is described as successful, check maintenance, financing, input quality, staffing, and persistence after the pilot.

## Round 6: Build the causal map

Represent each important claim as a condition–mechanism–outcome chain. Add actor control, time dependency, spatial transfer, and alternative explanations. The causal map should show where evidence is direct and where it is assumed.

## Round 7: Build the uncertainty register

Classify each conclusion as established, supported, plausible, contested, or unknown. Record what kind of uncertainty exists: measurement, definition, spatial coverage, time coverage, sampling, reporting incentive, model assumption, or causal ambiguity.

## Round 8: Prioritize future evidence

Rank unknowns by decision value. A high-priority unknown is both important to a potential intervention and feasible to validate. For example, whether a collection gap is caused by vehicle capacity may be more actionable than a national estimate that cannot change a near-term decision.

## Round 9: Design the field phase only afterward

Convert the highest-value unknowns into field protocols. Each protocol must state the hypothesis, population or location, sampling unit, time period, measurement method, safety and consent requirements, expected result, and decision that the result could change.

## Staged outputs

| Stage | Output |
|---|---|
| Initial desk round | Coded source library and failure taxonomy |
| Sense-making round | Layered comparison matrix and causal map |
| Pre-field round | Uncertainty register and priority hypotheses |
| Targeted field round | Validation dataset and discrepancy report |
| Post-validation round | Revised model and intervention options |

## Definition of rigor

For Anavaratha, rigor does not mean pretending to have perfect data. It means making the reasoning chain visible, preserving disagreement, separating fact from inference, comparing like with like, acknowledging who is missing from the data, and designing the next observation to resolve a consequential uncertainty.
