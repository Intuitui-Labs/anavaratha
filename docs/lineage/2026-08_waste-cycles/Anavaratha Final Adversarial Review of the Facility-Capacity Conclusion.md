# Anavaratha Final Adversarial Review of the Facility-Capacity Conclusion

**Review date:** 22 August 2026. **Scope:** Sri Lanka-wide facility-capacity evidence. **Purpose:** Test rather than defend the conclusion that Sri Lanka lacks a publicly accessible, recent, nationally comparable facility-capacity evidence system.

## The original conclusion under test

> Sri Lanka has many documented facilities and projects, but no publicly accessible, recent, nationally comparable system showing which facilities currently operate, what they actually process, how reliably they work, whether outputs are safe and marketable, and where residual burdens go.

This statement contains several separate claims. They must be tested separately: whether data systems exist; whether they are recent; whether they cover the country; whether they are public; whether they are comparable; and whether they describe operational reality rather than plans or installed assets.

## Strongest counterevidence found

The strongest counterevidence is real and materially important. A 2019–2023 JICA project for formulating the Western Province Solid Waste Management Master Plan developed a municipal solid-waste database reportedly introduced to 49 local authorities. A 2024 peer-reviewed account says local officers entered collection, intermediate treatment such as composting and waste-to-energy, transfer, final-disposal, and recycling amounts on daily, weekly, and monthly bases. It describes five modules including data input, collection, analysis, and publication.[1]

A SACEP/PLEASE project page dated 16 April 2025 says JICA was funding a pilot solid-waste-management database for Western Province intended to collect monthly local-authority information in a real-time database, with possible replication across the country.[2] The Japanese Embassy reported a July 2024 handover of a JICA pilot project at Karadiyana, including trommels for compost screening and tractors for waste transportation.[3] The Western Province WMA publicly provides downloads for the provincial master plan and a Karadiyana waste-to-energy project.[4]

These findings disprove any absolute version of the claim that Sri Lanka has no recent data-system activity, no public project architecture, or no facility intervention. There is a significant Western Province information and implementation architecture, and it continued to receive attention after the 2023 project completion.

## What the counterevidence does and does not prove

| Test | Counterevidence | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Does a waste-management database exist? | JICA/Sato describe a database introduced to 49 Western Province local authorities | **Yes, at least regionally, by report** |
| Is there recent continuation? | SACEP/PLEASE describes a 2025 pilot real-time database initiative | **Yes, as a reported pilot initiative** |
| Are recent facility interventions documented? | 2024 Karadiyana handover included screening and transport equipment | **Yes** |
| Are current operating outputs publicly available? | No downloadable daily/monthly database extracts, validation reports, or facility-level public throughput table were located | **Not established** |
| Is coverage national? | The database evidence identifies Western Province; replication across the country is described as an intention or possibility | **Not established** |
| Is the data independently audited? | No public audit or reconciliation against weighbridges, maintenance, finance, quality, licences, and residuals was located | **Not established** |
| Does a database measure usable capacity? | It reportedly records flows, but equipment uptime, staffing, quality, residual containment, and persistence are not demonstrated by the public descriptions | **Not by itself** |
| Does project delivery prove sustained operation? | Equipment handover and pilot completion are documented | **No** |
| Does current project planning prove delivered capacity? | 2026 Pivithuru Purawara is a planned national programme | **No** |

## Revised conclusion

The original conclusion should be **qualified, not rejected**.

The over-strong version—“Sri Lanka has no recent waste-management data systems or facility-capacity initiatives”—is false. There is documented recent regional database development, public planning material, project continuation, and facility-equipment handover in Western Province.[1] [2] [3] [4]

The defensible version is narrower:

> **Sri Lanka has a documented Western Province waste-management database and project architecture, alongside historical multi-province facility records and recent national programme announcements. However, the public evidence reviewed does not yet establish a nationally accessible, independently validated, recent, comparable facility-capacity system that links all local authorities and facility types to current operation, measured throughput, downtime, finance, workforce, quality, residual pathways, licences, inspections, and persistence.**

This revised conclusion is stronger because it acknowledges the real counterevidence rather than treating the evidence gap as total. It also identifies the precise unresolved question: not whether data collection has ever occurred, but whether the country can demonstrate a current, comparable, auditable chain from facility identity to actual material throughput and safe residual destination.

## Why the conclusion still survives in qualified form

First, **geographic coverage remains unresolved**. The database is described as covering 49 Western Province local authorities, while Sri Lanka has local authorities across all nine provinces. The 2024 Sato article itself presents sharply different provincial collection estimates: for 2020, reported collection coverage ranged from 13.7% in North Central Province and 18.0% in Sabaragamuwa to 54.0% in Eastern Province and 62.1% in Western Province.[5] Such differences make it unsafe to assume that Western Province data represent national facility reality.

Second, **public accessibility remains unresolved**. The public WMA page exposes plan downloads, and the CEA exposes institutional pages and publication links, but the JICA/Sato database itself was not located as an open national download with facility-level records. The CEA-linked Pollution Source Inventory redirects to a login page. The public existence of a database is therefore not equivalent to public data access.

Third, **comparability remains unresolved**. The 2025 operational study demonstrates that even within Western Province, design capacity, running capacity, actual production, and sales can diverge dramatically. Across the 22 stations, aggregate running capacity was 86.17% of design capacity, but actual compost production was only 6.57% of running capacity under the extracted 2022 table.[6] A national database that records “intermediate treatment amount” without standardized definitions, weighing methods, stock reconciliation, downtime, contamination, accepted feedstock, rejects, and quality cannot by itself provide comparable usable capacity.

Fourth, **operational validation remains unresolved**. The Auditor General’s clinical-waste audit shows why projects and assets require follow-up. It found idle or repeatedly failing Meta Mizer equipment, expired maintenance contracts, accumulated treated waste, weak pathogen verification, incomplete licensing, and unimplemented recommendations through October 2024.[7] This does not prove all municipal facilities fail; it proves that commissioning and equipment presence cannot substitute for operation, maintenance, safety, and persistence evidence.

Fifth, **national capacity must include residual and environmental pathways**. The CEA’s Western Province review raised unresolved ash-management concerns for waste-to-energy projects and criticized planning that focused heavily on thermal recovery without sufficiently analysing alternatives, baseline conditions, or existing project performance.[8] A facility-capacity system that counts treatment but cannot trace ash, rejects, leachate, and final disposal is incomplete for Anavaratha’s Freedom from Waste question.

## The adversarial evidence matrix

The accompanying file `ANAVARATHA_CONCLUSION_ADVERSARIAL_EVIDENCE_LOG.csv` records ten falsification tests. Its verdicts are deliberately mixed. Some claims are qualified; some are rejected; some survive; and some are confirmed as unresolved. This is the correct structure for an adversarial review.

| Evidence status | What belongs here |
|---|---|
| **Counterevidence accepted** | Regional database development, 2024 equipment handover, public WMA project downloads, nine additional mechanized compost plants reported by Sato et al. |
| **Original claim rejected** | “There are no recent data-system or project activities.” |
| **Original claim retained in qualified form** | “There is no publicly demonstrated, national, independently validated, comparable operational-capacity system.” |
| **Still unknown** | Database uptime, public access permissions, field completeness, national replication, current facility throughput, current status of closed/idle sites, maintenance and quality records, residual destinations |

## Required next test

The decisive next step is not another general literature search. It is a **data-retrieval and validation test** directed to the database owners and responsible agencies. The request should ask for: the database name and access route; list of all local authorities enrolled; facility IDs and coordinates; date of last update; data dictionary; daily/monthly records from 2019–2026; collection, treatment, transfer, recycling, final-disposal, and residual fields; validation and missingness reports; user and audit logs; and status of national replication.

The same request should ask CEA, WMA, provincial councils, local authorities, and the National Solid Waste Management Support Center for facility-level design, permitted, running, actual-intake, output, residual, downtime, maintenance, finance, workforce, quality, environmental, licensing, inspection, complaint, closure, and rehabilitation records. If the database exists but these fields are absent or inaccessible, that becomes evidence of an **information-capacity limitation**. If they are available and auditable, Anavaratha’s conclusion should be revised again.

## Final verdict

**Verdict: qualified survival.** The evidence defeats a sweeping “nothing exists” narrative. It does not defeat the narrower and more useful claim that the publicly demonstrable national evidence system remains incomplete for facility-capacity reality. Anavaratha should now treat the Western Province database as a major counterevidence lead and possible gateway to better data—not as proof that the national problem has already been measured or solved.
