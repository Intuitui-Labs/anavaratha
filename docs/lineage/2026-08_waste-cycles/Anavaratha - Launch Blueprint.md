# Anavaratha: Launch Blueprint

## 1. Project definition

**Anavaratha** is a Sri Lanka–focused material-and-waste-flow intelligence project. It will document where materials enter a defined local system, where they are used and discarded, how waste moves through formal and informal channels, where it is recovered or disposed of, and where it accumulates or leaks into the environment.

The project should be designed as an **evidence system**, not merely as a map or awareness campaign. Its core output will be an auditable baseline that allows local authorities, communities, researchers, businesses, and policymakers to see the same system: sources, quantities, routes, facilities, actors, risks, and intervention opportunities.

> **Working thesis:** Waste is not an endpoint. It is a recurring flow of materials, value, labor, risk, and environmental impact. If the flow is measured, mapped, and understood, parts of it can be prevented, recovered, redirected, or converted into value.

Sri Lanka’s policy direction supports this framing. The Central Environmental Authority states that the National Environmental Policy approved in 2022 recognizes reduce–reuse–recycle and circular economy principles, while the National Policy on Waste Management approved in 2019 emphasizes resource circulation, minimizing final disposal, improving existing systems, waste prevention, and protection of public health and ecosystems.[1] [2]

## 2. What Anavaratha should and should not attempt initially

The first version must be narrow enough to measure properly. Attempting to map every material, every district, and every type of waste immediately would create a visually impressive but unreliable product.

| Initial scope | Recommended decision |
|---|---|
| Geography | One local-authority area or connected cluster, provisionally the Dompe/Gampaha-connected system, subject to access confirmation |
| Main waste domain | Municipal solid waste |
| Priority material streams | Organic waste and plastics; add paper/cardboard and metals where records permit |
| Time period | Minimum 4–6 weeks of field measurement, ideally repeated across contrasting seasons later |
| System boundary | Generators, collection, transport, transfer, recovery, informal recovery, disposal, leakage, and accumulation |
| Excluded from first baseline | Hazardous, biomedical, e-waste, industrial, and construction waste unless a justified sub-study is added |
| Main output | Auditable baseline, waste-flow diagram, accumulation-hotspot map, intervention priorities, and prototype data model |

The boundary is not a statement that excluded waste streams are unimportant. It is a quality-control decision. Each future module can be added only when the project has a responsible partner, an appropriate measurement method, and a clear use case.

## 3. The first research questions

The pilot should answer six practical questions. First, what materials and products enter the pilot system through households, shops, markets, institutions, tourism, agriculture, and other activities? Second, how much municipal waste is generated, by source and material category? Third, what happens to it after generation: separation, collection, transport, reuse, recycling, composting, informal recovery, burning, dumping, or controlled disposal? Fourth, where does waste accumulate or leak into drains, waterways, wetlands, farmland, or public space? Fifth, who performs each function, formally or informally, and what constraints or risks do they face? Sixth, which interventions can reduce final disposal, improve service, protect workers and communities, or create recovery value?

Every answer must carry four labels: **measurement status** (measured, reported, estimated, or unknown), **time period**, **geographic boundary**, and **confidence level**. This prevents figures from different years or definitions being combined as though they were comparable.

## 4. Pilot strategy

The strongest initial candidate is a **Dompe/Gampaha-connected pilot**, not because it is automatically representative of all Sri Lanka, but because the CEA explicitly identifies the Dompe Sanitary Landfill and the Gampaha District Integrated Solid Waste Management Project within its special-project responsibilities.[1] An official Cabinet Office record also describes the Pilisaru National Solid Waste Management Project and a sanitary landfill in the Dompe Divisional Secretariat area of Gampaha District.[3]

The final pilot must be selected through a short scorecard and confirmed through conversations with the relevant local authority and operators. A second candidate should be retained for comparison, such as a Colombo local-authority cluster or a coastal municipality outside the Western Province. The pilot should not be announced as final until data access and permission are secured.

| Pilot criterion | Question to test | Evidence required |
|---|---|---|
| Institutional access | Will the local authority nominate a focal person and share operational records? | Letter or meeting confirmation |
| Data availability | Are route, vehicle, facility, weighbridge, or recovery records available? | Sample records and data dictionary |
| Flow diversity | Does the area include residential, commercial, institutional, and market sources? | Land-use and generator inventory |
| Environmental relevance | Are there visible accumulation, burning, drainage, or leakage problems? | Initial hotspot reconnaissance |
| Operational feasibility | Can a field team measure the system safely for 4–6 weeks? | Fieldwork plan and risk assessment |
| Intervention potential | Are there recovery actors, markets, composting, or service improvements to test? | Stakeholder interviews |
| Replicability | Can the method be applied elsewhere after the pilot? | Standard operating procedures |

The candidate that scores well on cooperation and measurable flows should beat a larger location with weak access. **Data access is a design variable, not an administrative afterthought.**

## 5. Stakeholder architecture

Anavaratha should be co-produced with the people who generate, move, recover, regulate, finance, and live alongside waste. The CEA itself describes distinct roles for policy and technical intervention, enforcement, and special projects.[1] UN-Habitat’s Waste Wise Cities guidance similarly recommends involving local government, regulators, collection operators, disposal managers, formal and informal recyclers, manufacturers, residents, and civil society in the assessment and intervention process.[4]

| Stakeholder group | Role in Anavaratha |
|---|---|
| Central and provincial environmental authorities | Policy alignment, technical review, regulatory context |
| Local authority | Boundary definition, service records, route data, permissions, operational validation |
| Collection contractors and municipal crews | Route, load, schedule, vehicle, missed-service, and practical-constraint data |
| Landfill, transfer, composting, and recovery operators | Inflow/outflow, residual, capacity, control, and safety data |
| Informal collectors, aggregators, and buyers | Hidden recovery flows, prices, material destinations, livelihood and safety realities |
| Households, businesses, markets, and institutions | Generation, separation, service access, and behavior data |
| Universities and technical partners | Sampling design, quality control, analysis, and training |
| Community organizations and civil society | Local knowledge, hotspot validation, communication, and accountability |
| Recyclers and potential end users | Demand, quality requirements, and viable recovery pathways |

The project should create a small **pilot steering group** and a technical working group. The steering group resolves access and institutional questions. The technical group maintains definitions, sampling, quality checks, and versioned data.

## 6. Measurement methodology

The methodology should adapt the UN-Habitat Waste Wise Cities Tool (WaCT) and the complementary Waste Flow Diagram (WFD), rather than inventing a separate vocabulary. UN-Habitat describes WaCT as a seven-step approach covering preparation, household waste generation and composition, non-household waste, recovery facilities, disposal facilities, composition at disposal facilities, and calculations related to food waste, recycling, plastic leakage, greenhouse gases, and air pollution.[4] The WFD adds rapid, observation-based mapping of waste flows and plastic leakage.[4] [5]

### Phase A: Preparation

Define the boundary and inventory all generators, routes, facilities, hotspots, waterways, and institutional actors. Prepare permission letters, field safety procedures, consent language for interviews, and a common glossary. Establish the project’s coordinate reference system, unique IDs, file naming conventions, and data-provenance rules before collection begins.

### Phase B: Generator survey and waste characterization

Stratify sampling by settlement type and generator category. At minimum, include households, shops, food businesses or markets, institutions, and public spaces. Record waste mass, material categories, number of people or business activity proxies, collection access, separation behavior, and disposal behavior. Use repeat measurements rather than one-day observations. Record wet and dry conditions, date, time, and unusual events.

The initial material taxonomy should be operationally simple: food and other organics; paper and cardboard; plastics by major type where feasible; glass; metals; textiles; sanitary material; inert or fine fraction; and other or unknown. The taxonomy can become more detailed after the team demonstrates reliable sorting.

### Phase C: Collection and transport audit

For each route or collection event, record origin, destination, vehicle or crew identifier, date and time, load type, estimated or weighed mass, number of stops, missed areas, fuel or travel notes where available, and whether material is mixed or separated. Document transfer points and any informal diversion before the official facility.

### Phase D: Facility audit

Assess recovery facilities, composting sites, transfer stations, landfills, and other disposal points. Record incoming mass, outgoing recovered materials, residuals, storage, operating days, environmental controls, worker protective equipment, and known downstream destinations. Treat capacity, closure, overflow, and maintenance as time-dependent attributes.

### Phase E: Leakage and accumulation mapping

Use GPS-enabled observations to record illegal dumps, persistent roadside piles, open burning, drain entry points, stream or wetland litter, exposed waste, and accumulation near schools, residences, farms, or workplaces. Photograph only where ethical and permitted. Repeat hotspot observations so that temporary litter is not confused with persistent accumulation.

### Phase F: Reconciliation and analysis

Build a mass-balance table for each period and material stream:

> **Opening stock + inflows − measured outflows = closing stock ± uncertainty**

For a waste-generation baseline, the practical reconciliation is:

> **Generated = collected + recovered through formal channels + recovered through informal channels + treated/processed + controlled disposal + uncontrolled disposal/leakage + unobserved remainder**

The unobserved remainder must remain visible. It should not be silently allocated to disposal merely to make the equation balance.

## 7. Minimum data model

The project should begin with a relational-style data model, even if the first collection tool is a spreadsheet. Each record needs a stable ID and a source reference.

| Entity | Essential fields |
|---|---|
| Generator | generator_id, category, location, population/activity proxy, sampling stratum |
| Measurement event | event_id, date, time, weather, method, observer, source, confidence |
| Material flow | flow_id, origin_id, destination_id, material, mass, unit, status, event_id |
| Collection route | route_id, operator, vehicle, origin zone, destination, schedule, service notes |
| Facility | facility_id, type, operator, coordinates, operating status, control level |
| Hotspot | hotspot_id, coordinates, type, estimated area/volume, repeat count, receptor proximity |
| Stakeholder | stakeholder_id, organization/type, role, consent status, contact permission |
| Intervention | intervention_id, problem addressed, mechanism, owner, cost class, expected result |
| Source/provenance | source_id, document/interview/measurement, date, URL or file, verification status |

Every mass record should also include **mass_basis** (weighed, counted and converted, surveyed, facility-reported, or modelled), **coverage_period**, **geographic_scope**, and **uncertainty_note**.

## 8. Prototype specification

The first digital product should be a small **Anavaratha Atlas**, not a national command center. Version 0.1 should provide four views: a map of generators, routes, facilities, and hotspots; a material-flow diagram for the pilot; a time view showing accumulation and flow changes; and an intervention view connecting problems to possible actions.

The prototype should support CSV or spreadsheet import, manual correction with an audit log, source links, confidence filters, and export of maps and summary tables. It should display a clear distinction between **observed**, **reported**, **estimated**, and **unknown** data. Public users should see aggregated data only. Sensitive facility details, worker information, household-level information, and potentially stigmatizing locations should be access-controlled or generalized.

No predictive model should be built until the pilot has a sufficiently long, quality-controlled time series. In the first stage, descriptive mapping and mass-balance reconciliation are more valuable than a machine-learning forecast based on sparse or inconsistent records.

## 9. Ninety-day starting plan

| Period | Main work | Deliverable |
|---|---|---|
| Days 1–14 | Confirm problem statement, shortlist pilots, contact authorities, define scope and glossary | Signed project charter and pilot scorecard |
| Days 15–30 | Secure permissions, form steering and technical groups, inventory sources/facilities/routes, prepare forms | Field protocol, stakeholder register, data dictionary |
| Days 31–50 | Conduct reconnaissance, generator sampling, route audits, facility assessments, hotspot mapping | Raw measurement dataset and field notes |
| Days 51–65 | Repeat selected measurements, interview recovery actors, validate destinations, reconcile flows | Cleaned baseline and uncertainty register |
| Days 66–80 | Produce flow diagrams, maps, hotspot ranking, and intervention workshop materials | Pilot Atlas v0.1 and workshop pack |
| Days 81–90 | Hold stakeholder workshop, agree priority interventions, document lessons, plan next season | Pilot report, scale-up decision, funding concept note |

The first success criterion is not a polished dashboard. It is a **trusted baseline that local actors recognize as operationally credible**.

## 10. Initial success measures

Anavaratha should measure its own performance. By the end of the first pilot, the team should be able to state the proportion of flow records with a named source, the share of mass estimates supported by direct measurement, the number of routes and facilities validated, the number of hotspots revisited, the number of formal and informal actors represented, and the number of intervention decisions supported by the evidence.

Environmental and social indicators should be added only when a baseline and responsible measurement partner exist. Possible later indicators include reduction in uncontrolled disposal, increased source separation, recovery volume, avoided transport or disposal cost, worker-safety improvements, plastic leakage reduction, compost output, and livelihood effects.

## 11. Risks and safeguards

The largest risk is false precision. Different studies and agencies may define generation, collection, disposal, and municipal waste differently. Anavaratha should preserve original definitions and show ranges when necessary. A second risk is invisibility of informal workers. The project should treat informal recovery as a real system component, obtain informed consent, avoid exposing personal identities without permission, and consider safety and livelihood impacts before recommending formalization.

A third risk is building a technology product before establishing operational trust. The pilot should use simple forms and transparent calculations first. A fourth risk is stigmatizing communities or facilities. Public outputs should emphasize system conditions and intervention opportunities rather than labeling neighborhoods as “dirty.” A fifth risk is assuming that all recovery is environmentally beneficial. Each proposed pathway should be assessed for contamination, worker safety, transport, market demand, and residual disposal.

## 12. Immediate next actions

The correct first move is a **two-week inception sprint**. During that sprint, the team should finalize the one-sentence problem statement, prepare a two-page project concept note, shortlist three pilot areas, request introductory meetings with the relevant local authorities and CEA-linked actors, create the data dictionary and field forms, and conduct a desk inventory of existing records.

The first decision gate is simple: proceed to fieldwork only if one pilot has a defined boundary, a local focal point, permission to observe or request records, an identified disposal or recovery endpoint, and a safe plan for measurement. If those conditions are not met, choose the next candidate before collecting data.
