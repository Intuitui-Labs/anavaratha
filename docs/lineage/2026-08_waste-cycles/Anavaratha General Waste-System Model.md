# Anavaratha General Waste-System Model

## 1. Waste is not one object

At the highest level, waste is a set of material, biological, social, economic, institutional, and ecological transformations. A material becomes “waste” when it loses its intended use for a generator or when the system lacks a viable pathway to keep it in use. The same object may be waste to one actor, a tradable input to another, a hazard to a community, or a stock that accumulates in land or water.

Therefore, Anavaratha should not begin with the question “How much waste exists?” It should begin with several linked questions: what material is moving; who defines it as waste; where does that definition change; what capacity exists to recover or contain it; and where do value and burden end up?

## 2. The general system boundary

A complete waste-system model should include upstream material inflows, production and commerce, consumption and use, discard, separation, storage, collection, transport, sorting, repair, reuse, recycling, biological treatment, thermal treatment, disposal, leakage, environmental transformation, and accumulated stocks. It should also include actors, rules, finance, infrastructure, labor, markets, knowledge, and ecological receptors.

The boundary can be narrowed for a particular research question, but every narrowing must state what is excluded and what may therefore be hidden.

## 3. Flows, stocks, and transformations

| Element | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Inflow | Material, product, packaging, food, fuel, construction input, or other item entering a system |
| Use | The period and activity in which the material provides a service or value |
| Discard flow | Material leaving intended use and entering a waste or recovery pathway |
| Recovery flow | Reuse, repair, recycling, composting, reprocessing, or other pathway that retains material or value |
| Residual flow | Material that remains after sorting or treatment and requires disposal or further management |
| Leakage flow | Material escaping intended management into air, soil, water, streets, drains, or ecosystems |
| Stock | Material accumulated in households, facilities, landfills, dumps, waterways, soils, or long-lived products |
| Transformation | Change in material, quality, toxicity, energy content, biological state, or social meaning |
| Receptor | Person, worker group, community, ecosystem, water body, soil, atmosphere, or future population receiving burden |

A flow is not automatically a success or a failure. Recovery can produce contamination or unsafe work. Disposal can be controlled or uncontrolled. A material can leave a city and remain a burden elsewhere. Every flow needs a destination and a consequence.

## 4. The waste hierarchy as a direction, not a complete model

The waste hierarchy generally places prevention and reduction above reuse, recycling, recovery, and disposal. This is useful as a normative ordering, but it does not by itself explain whether a pathway is feasible, safe, affordable, equitable, or durable. Anavaratha should use the hierarchy together with material-flow analysis, life-cycle reasoning, institutional analysis, and social and ecological perspectives.

## 5. The system’s key relationships

| Relationship | Question |
|---|---|
| Pressure–capacity | Does material or activity pressure exceed usable collection, treatment, market, or ecological capacity? |
| Flow–stock | Is the system reducing a flow or merely moving the stock to another place or future period? |
| Value–burden | Who gains from recovered value and who receives cost, risk, exposure, or inconvenience? |
| Policy–practice | How do laws and plans become routes, contracts, facilities, behavior, and enforcement? |
| Formal–informal | Which flows and actors are counted formally and which remain invisible? |
| Local–regional | Does management depend on destinations, markets, or facilities outside the originating boundary? |
| Present–future | Does a current solution reduce future burden or defer it? |
| Data–governance | Does better information change decisions, or merely describe a system without accountability? |

## 6. General failure theory

Waste systems fail when a pathway is designed without the capacities needed to operate it, when responsibilities are unclear, when finance does not persist, when material quality is incompatible with recovery, when social participation is assumed rather than organized, when burdens are transferred outside the measurement boundary, or when data are too inconsistent to reveal change.

A visible failure is therefore often downstream of a deeper mismatch. For example, open dumping may be associated with behavior, but it may also result from irregular collection, inaccessible routes, unavailable land, facility closure, costs, weak enforcement, or seasonal flooding. A successful analysis reconstructs the chain instead of assigning blame to the last visible actor.

## 7. General analytical lenses

The eagle-view model should be read through at least eight lenses: material and energy; population and activity; infrastructure and operations; institutions and law; finance and markets; labor and social relations; space and ecology; and time and historical change. No single lens should be allowed to define the system in isolation.

## 8. What Anavaratha adds

Anavaratha can contribute a **cycle-and-burden perspective**. It should follow not just whether waste is collected, but how materials and burdens circulate, accumulate, transform, disappear from records, or reappear in another location or period. It should map freedom from waste burden alongside the conditions that enable communities, workers, and ecosystems to flourish, while keeping the waste-focused empirical model precise.
