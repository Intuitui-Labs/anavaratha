# Anavaratha Desk Baseline v0.2

## Status

This is a **desk-based working baseline**, not a current national waste inventory and not a field-validated diagnosis. It synthesizes reviewed Sri Lankan policy documents, research studies, planning records, database-system evidence, and international measurement guidance. Historical figures and targets remain source-specific unless their definitions, periods, and boundaries are harmonized.

## 1. Main finding

Sri Lanka’s waste-management challenge is best understood as a **capacity–pressure–pathway problem**. Material pressure is generated by households, businesses, markets, institutions, tourism, and other activities. Usable capacity varies by place and time. The system fails when one or more links—policy fit, coordination, collection, separation, transport, recovery, markets, treatment, disposal, monitoring, or maintenance—cannot carry the pressure.

This means that the same visible symptom can have different causes in different places. A dumping hotspot may reflect irregular collection, insufficient vehicles, a commercial activity cluster, lack of land access, weak enforcement, flooding, downstream facility closure, or a combination. The desk model should therefore resist one-cause explanations.

## 2. What the reviewed evidence contributes

The Central Environmental Authority identifies policy and technical intervention, implementation and enforcement, and special-project functions within its Solid Waste Management Unit, including work connected to Dompe and Gampaha.[1] CEA policy recommendations emphasize resource circulation, reduce–reuse–recycle, circular economy, waste prevention, ecosystem and public-health protection, and caution against treating thermal recovery as a substitute for source separation and broader waste-hierarchy measures.[2]

The National Action Plan on Plastic Waste Management 2021–2030 provides the strongest lifecycle framework in the reviewed corpus. It covers plastic inflows, processing, use, collection, recovery, recycling, disposal, health and safety, community participation, monitoring, and stakeholder coordination.[3] It also identifies the importance of clean and graded material, downstream markets, facility audits, local-authority waste profiles, and a system to track plastic flows into and within the country.[3]

The policy’s retrospective discussion of Pilisaru is particularly valuable for Anavaratha because it describes failure as a chain problem: an approach that was not sufficiently holistic or inclusive; unreliable collection of separated plastics; inadequate final treatment and disposal; and ineffective operation of some facilities provided to local authorities.[3]

The academic studies reinforce this chain interpretation. The Southern Province study links poor services to policy and legislative weakness, political and public commitment, technical expertise, finance, land, and a failure to differentiate between larger and smaller local authorities.[4] The Western Province implementation study emphasizes staff conditions, commitment, motivation, political leadership, social and business participation, land, vehicles, instruments, technology, integrated programming, regulation, labor productivity, awareness, and political interference.[5] The southeastern coastal-city study emphasizes source non-segregation, irregular collection, limited resources, weak regulation, and the movement of separated materials to other regions because local recycling capacity is limited.[6]

The small-local-authority case underlines the need for area-specific plans, separation, education, follow-up, and community participation alongside technical treatment.[7] The IWMI 20-city report offers a major cross-location resource for comparative work, while the Western Province database evaluation indicates that the operation and durability of data systems should itself be studied as part of waste governance.[8] [9]

## 3. Layered waste-system interpretation

| Layer | Desk interpretation for Anavaratha |
|---|---|
| Pressure | Generation from population and economic or seasonal activity |
| Capacity | Land, vehicles, staff, finance, equipment, technical skills, governance, information, and markets |
| Pathway | Separation, collection, transport, recovery, treatment, disposal, leakage, and accumulation |
| Constraint | The first condition that prevents a pathway from functioning adequately |
| Stock | Accumulated waste, landfill burden, persistent hotspot, stored material, or remaining facility capacity |
| Space | Origin, route, destination, receiving community, waterway, drain, wetland, or ecological receptor |
| Time | Daily, seasonal, project, political, crisis, and long-term change |
| Actor | Generator, worker, informal collector, operator, authority, regulator, buyer, resident, or receiving community |
| Evidence | Direct observation, administrative record, estimate, proxy, target, interpretation, or unknown |
| Outcome | Service quality, recovery, cost, exposure, pollution, trust, health, or persistence |

## 4. Capacity-pressure comparison logic

The project should compare absolute and proportional measures together. Total tonnes are necessary for infrastructure decisions, while normalized values help compare different places. The denominator should match the activity: population or household for residential waste, establishments or operating days for commercial waste, stalls or market days for markets, guest-nights for tourism, route kilometres for collection, and inflow relative to usable capacity for facilities.

The key analytical relation is:

> **System gap = pressure − usable capacity, under a defined place and time condition.**

Usable capacity is not the same as nominal capacity. A facility or vehicle may be unusable because of closure, maintenance, contamination, fuel, staffing, access, or market conditions.

## 5. Failure mechanisms

The evidence supports a provisional failure taxonomy with thirteen mechanisms: policy–scale mismatch; fragmented coordination; physical-capacity gap; recurrent-finance gap; human and technical gap; collection-pathway weakness; separation and quality failure; market and destination failure; political and regulatory failure; social connection failure; spatial transfer; temporal non-persistence; and data-system failure.

These mechanisms can combine. For example, poor separation may appear behavioral but be caused by unreliable segregated collection, lack of trust, contamination during transport, absent buyers, or a facility that does not operate. A technology intervention should therefore be assessed against the whole pathway rather than against the visible symptom.

## 6. Contradiction discipline

The reviewed sources contain different national and local waste estimates, collection rates, plastic flows, and definitions. These differences may reflect real spatial variation, different years, different boundaries, different waste categories, different denominators, or reporting purposes. The project should preserve each source’s value and context instead of creating one blended number.

At present, the responsible conclusion is not a single national quantity. It is that the evidence base is fragmented enough that **data harmonization is itself a central Anavaratha research problem**.

## 7. Ranked validation priorities

| Rank | Unknown to test later | Why it matters | Desk preparation before fieldwork |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Which pathway currently accounts for the largest unobserved or uncontrolled share in the chosen pilot? | Determines whether to prioritize collection, separation, recovery, disposal, or leakage | Reconcile all existing records and definitions |
| 2 | What is the binding capacity constraint for the pilot area? | Prevents investing in a non-binding solution | Build a capacity inventory and pressure estimate |
| 3 | Are reported collection gaps spatially aligned with persistent hotspots? | Tests whether route/service improvement could reduce uncontrolled disposal | Compare route/service records with spatial reports and maps |
| 4 | Are separated materials actually kept separate and delivered to viable destinations? | Tests the reliability of the recovery pathway | Trace records, operators, buyers, and facility claims |
| 5 | How large is informal recovery relative to formal records? | Prevents undercounting recovery and harming livelihoods | Map actors and destination chains ethically from secondary sources |
| 6 | Which outcomes persisted after previous projects ended? | Identifies maintenance and financing weaknesses | Compare project-period and post-project evidence |
| 7 | Which source definitions explain major conflicting national figures? | Prevents false baseline construction | Build a full definition and denominator register |
| 8 | Which communities, workers, and ecosystems receive transferred burdens? | Adds equity and ecological accountability | Overlay facilities, waterways, land use, population, and hazard layers |

## 8. Immediate next work package

The next desk task is to complete page-level extraction from the IWMI 20-city report and the Western Province database evaluation, then create a structured comparison of local-authority scale, population/activity denominators, service capacity, facility capacity, recovery pathways, and time persistence. The result should be a small number of defensible candidate system profiles, not a premature national map.
