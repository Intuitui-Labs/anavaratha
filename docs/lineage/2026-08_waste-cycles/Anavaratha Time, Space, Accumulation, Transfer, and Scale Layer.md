# Anavaratha Time, Space, Accumulation, Transfer, and Scale Layer

## 1. Waste is a process across time

Waste is often measured as a daily flow, but many of its most important consequences are cumulative. A short-term reduction in visible waste may coexist with a growing landfill stock, contaminated soil, clogged drainage, worker exposure, or material leakage into waterways. Anavaratha should therefore distinguish what happens during a period from what accumulates across periods.

## 2. Time horizons

| Time horizon | What to examine | Typical error if ignored |
|---|---|---|
| Daily or weekly | Collection frequency, storage time, route capacity, market days, operational disruptions | Mistaking a normal day for a system average |
| Seasonal | Wet/dry seasons, rainfall, festivals, tourism, harvests, school cycles, disease events | Missing predictable peaks, flooding, or contamination |
| Project cycle | Baseline, implementation, operation, maintenance, closure, post-project period | Calling a supported pilot a lasting success |
| Political and institutional | Leadership, elections, budgets, regulations, land decisions, contracts | Treating governance changes as random noise |
| Economic cycle | Prices, fuel, employment, consumption, construction, imports, recovery markets | Missing demand and market-driven changes |
| Long-term | Population, urbanization, land use, climate, infrastructure, material substitution | Underestimating cumulative stock and future burden |

## 3. Flows and stocks

| Type | Examples | Core question |
|---|---|---|
| Flow | Generated, collected, transported, recovered, burned, leaked, disposed per period | What is moving now? |
| Stock | Landfill mass, dump volume, stored recyclables, clogged drains, contaminated land, persistent litter | What remains and grows? |
| Rate of change | Change in stock or flow over time | Is the burden expanding, stabilizing, or declining? |
| Transition | Facility opening/closure, policy change, disaster, market shift, project end | What changed the system pathway? |

A stock is not removed simply because it disappears from an official flow record. It may have moved to another place, been burned, entered water, or become unobservable. The model must record unknown or unobserved outcomes rather than assuming disappearance.

## 4. Space as relation

Waste geography is not just a map of points. It is a network of origins, routes, nodes, destinations, and receptors. The same material can pass through several administrative units and ecological systems. A facility may be far from the generator but near a community, waterway, wetland, road, or floodplain that receives the burden.

| Spatial element | Analytical question |
|---|---|
| Origin | Where is waste generated and by which activity? |
| Storage point | Where does material wait before movement? |
| Route | What roads, waterways, drains, or informal paths carry it? |
| Node | Where is it transferred, sorted, treated, sold, or accumulated? |
| Destination | Where does the material or residual finally go? |
| Receptor | Who or what receives risk, cost, pollution, or value? |
| Boundary | Which administrative or ecological boundary hides the pathway? |

## 5. Transfer mechanisms

A waste-management intervention can transfer rather than eliminate burden. Transfer may be spatial, temporal, material, social, financial, or ecological.

| Transfer type | Example |
|---|---|
| Spatial | Waste leaves a city and accumulates at a peripheral facility or neighboring authority |
| Temporal | A project delays accumulation without changing the underlying generation or maintenance problem |
| Material | A difficult residue remains after recycling or treatment and moves to disposal |
| Social | Risk shifts from the public space to waste workers or informal actors |
| Financial | Costs shift from the authority to households, contractors, or receiving communities |
| Ecological | Pollution shifts from land to water, air, soil, or marine systems |
| Data | Unrecorded flows disappear from official performance measures |

## 6. Scale effects

A system behaves differently at different scales. National policy can set direction but cannot directly provide a route, bin, worker, market, or facility. A local authority may improve operations but depend on regional disposal or markets. A household may separate waste but lose trust if downstream collection remixes it. A facility may have nominal capacity but become constrained by transport, contamination, access, or receiving opposition.

| Scale | What becomes visible | What can remain hidden |
|---|---|---|
| National | Policy, imports, material economy, finance, institutions, overall trends | Local pathway failure and receiving burdens |
| Province/region | Inter-authority flows, shared facilities, markets, planning coordination | Household and route-level behavior |
| Local authority | Service coverage, budgets, contracts, routes, facilities, complaints | Upstream product design and downstream market dependence |
| Settlement/neighborhood | Density, activity, storage, service inequality, hotspots | Regional disposal and market chains |
| Route/facility | Operational bottlenecks, mixing, downtime, worker conditions | National policy and broader consumption drivers |
| Household/business | Discard behavior, convenience, storage, separation, costs | System-wide destination and cumulative effects |

## 7. Cumulative burden and resilience

A useful desk model should track not only current service but accumulated burden and recovery capacity. Cumulative burden can be represented through persistent stocks, repeated leakage, repeated exposure, declining facility capacity, contaminated land, or growing maintenance backlog. Resilience concerns the ability to absorb shocks—rainfall, disasters, fuel or price changes, population surges, facility closure, or political transition—without shifting uncontrolled burden elsewhere.

## 8. What this layer must deliver

Before descending to named locations, Anavaratha should produce a general time typology, flow-stock model, spatial network model, transfer taxonomy, scale matrix, cumulative-burden indicators, and resilience questions. These tools will later determine what kind of local case is needed and what data a case must contain.
