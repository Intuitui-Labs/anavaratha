# Anavaratha research notes

## Verified institutional and policy findings

### Central Environmental Authority (CEA)
Source: https://www.cea.lk/web/en/2013-05-07-07-51-07/waste-management-division-sin/solid-waste-management-unit

The CEA Solid Waste Management Unit was established on 1 June 2018 under the Waste Management Division. Its stated objective is to support effective implementation of municipal solid-waste management systems in Sri Lanka. The unit has three cells: Policy & Technical Interventions; Implementation & Enforcement; and Implementation of Special Projects. The special-project work includes operation, maintenance, and improvement of the Dompe Sanitary Landfill and the Gampaha District Integrated Solid Waste Management Project.

Implication for Anavaratha: the first version should treat CEA, provincial authorities, local authorities, and special-project operators as distinct institutional actors, rather than assuming one national waste system.

### CEA recommendations on the proposed Western Province solid-waste master plan
Source: https://www.cea.lk/web/images/pdf/2024/SEA/Recommendations_of_the_Central_Environmental_Authority.pdf

The CEA document states that Sri Lanka's National Environmental Policy approved in June 2022 recognizes the 3R concept (reduce, reuse, recycle) and circular economy as key policy principles. It states that the National Policy on Waste Management approved in October 2019 highlights effective resource circulation, minimizing final disposal while retaining resource value through the life cycle, improving existing systems, preventing/minimizing waste, and reducing risks to public health, ecosystems, and waste handlers. It also identifies polluter-pays and extended producer responsibility as market-based tools, and warns that over-reliance on waste-to-energy can weaken source separation and other environmentally preferable steps.

Implication for Anavaratha: the project should not be designed as a landfill inventory or waste-to-energy feasibility study only. It should map prevention, reuse, recycling, organic recovery, residual disposal, health/safety, and cumulative accumulation across the life cycle.

## Initial working definition
Anavaratha is a Sri Lanka-focused material-and-waste-flow intelligence project. It maps where materials enter a defined system, where waste is generated, how it is collected and transported, where it is recovered or disposed of, and where materials accumulate over time. Its purpose is to reveal intervention points for prevention, circularity, public-health protection, and better allocation of resources.

## Initial boundary recommendation
Start with municipal solid waste in one local-authority area or connected cluster, and one or two material streams (organic waste plus plastics are a strong initial combination). Exclude hazardous, biomedical, industrial, e-waste, and construction waste from the first baseline unless the pilot area has reliable data and a clear reason to include them. Keep these as later modules.

## Initial research questions
1. What materials enter the pilot area, and through which household, commercial, institutional, tourism, agricultural, or industrial activities?
2. How much waste is generated by source and material category, and how does it vary by season or location?
3. What percentage is separated, collected, transported, recovered, informally handled, openly dumped, burned, or sent to final disposal?
4. Where are the accumulation hotspots and what communities, waterways, wetlands, farms, or workers are exposed?
5. Which interventions could reduce final disposal or convert waste into useful material, compost, energy, or income?
6. What data can be measured directly, what must be estimated, and how uncertain are the estimates?

## Pilot-selection criteria
Select a pilot with an identifiable local authority, accessible operational data, a manageable geographic boundary, visible material-flow problems, a mix of urban/peri-urban or rural contexts, and a realistic pathway to cooperation. Dompe/Gampaha and a Colombo-connected local-authority cluster appear worth evaluating because CEA explicitly identifies related special projects, but this is a candidate—not a final selection.

## Next work
Compare candidate pilot locations, identify stakeholders and permissions, then design a minimum viable data schema and field protocol before building any dashboard or predictive model.

These notes are research inputs, not the final deliverable. They must be rewritten into a coherent, properly cited launch blueprint.

## Peer-reviewed findings

### Southeastern coastal cities study
Source: https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/13/8/4556

A 2021 peer-reviewed study of municipal solid-waste management in southeastern coastal cities reported that systems contained collection, transport, treatment, and disposal elements, but effectiveness was weakened by poor source segregation, limited resources, weak regulation, irregular collection schedules, and limited technical know-how. In the studied Kalmunai area, the source describes high biodegradable content and notes that some separated plastics, glass, tins, and other materials were moved to Ampara and Colombo because local recycling capacity was limited. The study also highlights insufficient final-disposal land, inefficient workforces, and weak household motivation to segregate.

Implication: Anavaratha needs to represent both formal and informal flows, destination markets outside the pilot boundary, and operational constraints—not only waste-generation estimates.

### Integrated planning study
Source: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10163-018-0815-5

A 2019 regional case study described an evidence-based informative database intended to support comprehensive integrated solid-waste management plans at local-authority, provincial, and national levels. The study reported a large gap between waste generation and collection in its referenced baseline and emphasized improving collection efficiency, safe landfill disposal, and recycling diversion.

Implication: Anavaratha should be structured as a hierarchical system: site/route records feed local-authority summaries, which can later aggregate into district, provincial, and national views. It should keep measured data separate from estimated data and retain provenance for every number.

## Candidate pilot logic
A pilot should be selected through a transparent scorecard rather than intuition. Score each candidate on: access to local-authority cooperation; existing weighbridge, route, landfill, or recycling records; representative mix of residential/commercial/institutional sources; visible accumulation or leakage problem; manageable boundary; presence of downstream recovery markets; and ability to repeat measurements over at least one seasonal cycle.

Possible candidates to assess include a Dompe/Gampaha-connected system, a Colombo local-authority cluster, and one non-Western-Province comparison such as Kalmunai or another coastal municipality. No final pilot should be declared until data access and stakeholder willingness are confirmed.

## Important methodological caution
Published Sri Lankan waste figures vary by year, geography, and definition of “generated,” “collected,” and “disposed.” Anavaratha must never combine them without recording the measurement year, boundary, source, unit, and confidence level. The initial model should therefore be an auditable baseline with explicit assumptions, not a false-precision national map.

## International methodology to adapt

### UN-Habitat Waste Wise Cities Tool and Waste Flow Diagram
Sources:
- https://unhabitat.org/waste-wise-data
- https://unhabitat.org/sites/default/files/2021-02/Waste%20wise%20cities%20tool%20-%20EN%207%20%281%29.pdf
- https://unhabitat.org/sites/default/files/2020/10/brief_note_on_waste_wise_cities_tool_for_eoi.pdf

UN-Habitat describes the Waste Wise Cities Tool (WaCT) as a seven-step process for establishing a baseline of municipal solid waste generated, collected, and managed in controlled facilities; assessing the recovery chain and facilities; and calculating indicators related to food waste, recycling, plastic leakage, greenhouse-gas emissions, and air pollution. It includes household surveys, non-household waste assessment, recovery-facility assessment, disposal-facility assessment, waste composition at disposal facilities, and calculations. The Waste Flow Diagram (WFD) is described as complementary, rapid, and observation-based, mapping waste flows and quantifying plastic leakage. The guidance also emphasizes a participatory workshop involving local government, regulators, collection operators, disposal managers, formal and informal recyclers, manufacturers, residents, and civil society.

Implication: Anavaratha should adapt WaCT/WFD rather than inventing definitions from scratch, while adding Sri Lanka-specific layers such as local-authority boundaries, monsoon/seasonality, waterways and wetlands, language accessibility, informal recovery, and cumulative accumulation over time.

## Draft field sequence

1. Preparation: define the pilot boundary, appoint a local coordinator, obtain permissions, create a stakeholder register, and agree on definitions.
2. Household and source survey: sample households, shops, institutions, markets, tourism sites, and other generators; record waste quantities, composition, behavior, service access, and seasonality.
3. Collection and transport audit: map routes, schedules, vehicle capacities, missed areas, transfer points, weighbridge records, fuel/time, and informal collection.
4. Facility audit: inspect recovery, composting, transfer, landfill, and disposal sites; record inflows, outflows, residuals, environmental controls, and worker safety.
5. Leakage and accumulation survey: map illegal dumps, roadside piles, drain/waterway entry points, burning, litter traps, and persistent accumulation zones using GPS and repeat observations.
6. Flow reconciliation: produce a mass-balance table and Sankey-style flow diagram, separating measured, reported, inferred, and unknown quantities.
7. Intervention workshop: co-design prevention, source separation, recovery, service, infrastructure, enforcement, and livelihood interventions with stakeholders.
8. Public atlas: publish only aggregated, safety-checked layers; do not expose private household data or create maps that could stigmatize communities.

## Minimum data entities
Generator; material stream; collection route; vehicle/load; transfer station; recovery facility; composting/processing site; landfill/disposal site; informal collector or buyer; leakage hotspot; waterway/drain; measurement event; intervention; stakeholder; source/provenance; confidence score.

## Conceptual framing: two directions

### Berlin’s distinction
Source: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Positive and Negative Liberty”: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/liberty-positive-negative/

The standard distinction describes negative liberty as the absence of obstacles, barriers, constraints, or interference, and positive liberty as the possibility or fact of acting in a way that enables control of one’s life and realization of fundamental purposes. The distinction is useful for Anavaratha as a metaphor, but the project must state clearly that it is extending the language beyond Berlin’s original political-philosophical context.

### Capability-oriented refinement
Source: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “The Capability Approach”: https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/capability-approach/

The capability approach distinguishes formal access from real or substantive opportunities to achieve valuable doings and beings. It also emphasizes conversion factors: personal, social, political, and environmental conditions that determine whether resources can become actual opportunities. This gives Anavaratha a stronger basis for the ecological-development direction: not merely “more trees,” but greater ecological capabilities such as the capacity of landscapes to absorb water, support biodiversity, provide shade, regenerate soil, and sustain human livelihoods.

### Recommended labels
Use the following working labels during development:

- **Freedom from Waste**: reduction of waste burdens, unmanaged accumulation, exposure, leakage, forced proximity to hazards, and system failures. This is the negative-freedom-inspired direction.
- **Freedom to Flourish**: expansion of ecological and social capabilities through trees, restored ecosystems, clean public space, soil and water health, biodiversity, shade, food systems, and resilient livelihoods. This is the positive-freedom/capability-inspired direction.

Alternative paired labels worth testing with collaborators: “Release / Regeneration,” “Decompression / Flourishing,” “Unburdening / Becoming,” “Clearance / Cultivation,” and “Repair / Renewal.” Avoid “negative / positive” as the public-facing names because they can sound morally hierarchical or politically loaded.

### Core conceptual caution
The two directions should not be treated as a simple linear scale where waste reduction automatically produces ecological flourishing. They are connected but analytically distinct. A site can have low visible waste but poor ecological condition, or tree planting can occur without addressing waste leakage or social access. Anavaratha should therefore map two linked but separate outcome layers and study the mechanisms connecting them.

## Waste-focused failure-analysis evidence

### Southern Province case study
Source: Vidanaarachchi, Yuen & Pilapitiya, “Municipal solid waste management in the Southern Province of Sri Lanka: Problems, issues and challenges,” Waste Management (2006): https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0956053X0500245X

The article preview identifies recurring causes of poor waste-management services: lack of appropriate government policy and legislation; limited political will and public commitment; inadequate technical expertise; insufficient or poorly allocated financial resources; and lack of suitable land for disposal. It also criticizes a one-size-fits-all national strategy that does not differentiate between larger and smaller local authorities. The article preview reports low household access to collection in the study context and notes that disposal sites can be under-capacity relative to increasing demand.

Interpretation for Anavaratha: failure should be analyzed as a mismatch between system scale and capacity, not only as household behavior or lack of technology. The desk model should compare local-authority size, population, service demand, land, finance, technical capacity, and policy fit.

### Western Province implementation analysis
Source: Fernando, “Solid waste management of local governments in the Western Province of Sri Lanka: An implementation analysis,” Waste Management (2019): https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0956053X18307086

The study used qualitative and quantitative methods across selected Western Province local governments and found that staff remuneration and facilities, staff commitment, motivation, political leadership support, and contributions from society and business significantly affected implementation success. It identifies administrative and system problems including insufficient land for dumping, composting and recycling; lack of vehicles, instruments, modern technology, and an integrated programme; weak regulation; low labor productivity and staffing; limited resident awareness; and inappropriate political interference.

Interpretation for Anavaratha: capacity must be disaggregated into physical, financial, human, institutional, political, social, and informational capacity. “Failure” should be coded by mechanism and actor rather than recorded as a single outcome.

## Initial failure-mechanism taxonomy

1. Policy-design failure: goals or models do not fit local-authority scale and context.
2. Institutional-coordination failure: responsibilities or incentives are fragmented.
3. Capacity failure: insufficient land, staff, vehicles, tools, finance, or technical capability.
4. Operational failure: irregular routes, weak maintenance, missing records, or poor facility operation.
5. Behavioral and social failure: weak source separation, low trust, low participation, or inadequate communication.
6. Regulatory failure: weak enforcement, unclear standards, or political interference.
7. Market failure: recovery exists but downstream demand, prices, quality, or logistics are inadequate.
8. Data failure: generation, collection, recovery, disposal, and leakage are not measured with compatible definitions.
9. Spatial failure: disposal or service systems impose unequal burdens or leave areas disconnected.
10. Temporal failure: short projects, seasonal variation, emergencies, and long-term accumulation are not accounted for.

## Desk Review Round 1: policy and provincial planning evidence

### National Action Plan on Plastic Waste Management 2021–2030
Sources:
- UNEP report page: https://www.unep.org/ietc/resources/report/national-action-plan-plastic-waste-management-2021-2030
- IGES publication page: https://www.iges.or.jp/en/pub/national-action-plan-plastic-waste-management/en

The plan was developed by Sri Lanka’s Ministry of Environment with technical assistance from IGES/UNEP-linked environmental-technology partners and financial support from Japan’s Ministry of Environment. It uses a preventative approach, the 3R waste hierarchy, circular-economy framing, and a stated aim of reducing reliance on landfill. It emphasizes collection of segregated plastic waste and recycling as a viable business that can produce quality raw material for the plastics industry. The plan describes measurable, time-bound goals developed through stakeholder consultations and workshops.

The IGES page presents a national plastic-flow estimate for the plan’s baseline, including estimated generation, collection, recycling, open burning, and disposal to land and waterways. These figures must be treated as plan-specific estimates with their own definitions and reference period—not as timeless national facts—until the PDF methodology is extracted in full.

Implication: the desk model should include the entire plastic life cycle from inflow and packaging through use, collection, recovery, burning, disposal, and environmental leakage. It should also test whether “recycling as a business” is supported by quality, price, aggregation, transport, and downstream demand.

## Verified Desk Review Round 1 source: National Plastic Action Plan

Source: Ministry of Environment Sri Lanka, “National Action Plan on Plastic Waste Management 2021–2030,” 2021. PDF via CCET/IGES: https://www.ccet.jp/sites/default/files/2021-08/srilanka_report_web_fin_pw.pdf

The plan explicitly covers the full plastic life cycle, from importation and processing through final use, collection, recycling, and disposal. It frames the response around prevention and 3R principles, gives time-bound goals, and includes cross-cutting themes of education/training/research, health and safety, and community participation. The plan also identifies the need for segregated collection, quality control, downstream demand, and viable recycling businesses.

The plan states that post-consumer plastic often arrives unsorted and soiled, making part of it unsuitable as a useful resource without cleaning and grading. It identifies the need to understand formal and informal recovery, market quality, and final treatment. It also records that earlier prevention-oriented efforts under the Pilisaru project fell short of the “Waste free Sri Lanka” goal, attributing problems to an unholistic and non-inclusive approach, failures in collection of separated plastics, lack of proper final treatment and disposal facilities, and ineffective operation of some facilities provided to local authorities.

The plan reports several national baseline estimates, including total solid-waste generation and collection, plastic flows, recycling, open burning, and disposal. For Anavaratha, these should be treated as source-specific estimates with the plan’s stated reference periods and definitions. The numbers are useful for reconstructing the historical knowledge base and identifying measurement gaps, not for presenting an unqualified current national baseline.

Analytical importance: this source connects failure to a chain of broken links—design, inclusion, collection, facility operation, treatment, quality, and market—not to one isolated cause. It is therefore a core source for the failure-mechanism taxonomy.

The downloaded 50-page Ministry of Environment report was text-extracted successfully. Relevant sections include:

## Candidate-context evidence: Colombo and coastal plastic systems

### Colombo local-authority life-cycle assessment
Source: Peiris & Dayarathne, “Application of Life Cycle Framework for Municipal Solid Waste Management: a Circular Economy Perspective from Developing Countries,” Circular Economy and Sustainability (2022): https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9360726/

This open-access study applies life-cycle assessment to a selected Colombo local authority and evaluates waste-management scenarios from generation through transport, treatment, and final disposal. It describes the value of a local-authority boundary, a defined functional unit, a life-cycle inventory, and comparison of alternative scenarios using greenhouse-gas impacts. The article emphasizes that urban waste decisions in developing countries are often shaped by cost, land availability, expertise, reliability, convenience, fragmented responsibilities, and mismatch between national policy and local action. It also argues that increasing collection fleets or disposal capacity can remain linear responses if waste minimization and circularity are not considered.

Implication: a candidate profile should include a life-cycle boundary and scenario logic, but must keep scenario outputs distinct from measured current performance.

### Colombo Municipal Council case record
Source: Urban SDG Knowledge Platform, “Sustainable Solid Waste Management in the City of Colombo”: https://www.urbansdgplatform.org/profile/profile_caseView_detail.msc?no_case=649

The case record describes a 2017 start after the Meethotamulla dump collapse, with actions including source segregation, public awareness, compost bins, recycling markets, e-waste drop-off, route mapping, GPS on collection vehicles, CCTV, enforcement, by-laws, construction-clearance requirements, and planning documents. It identifies historical challenges including a landfill reaching capacity, illegal roadside dumping, open burning, lack of land within city limits, and public protest against dumping in receiving areas. The case record reports outcomes such as reduced waste collection, reduced open dumping, and waste-to-energy operations, but these are claims in a case-study database and require independent verification before being treated as evaluated impact.

Implication: Colombo is a useful candidate for a documented intervention-and-response profile, especially for studying policy change after a crisis, spatial transfer to receiving areas, technology mix, and persistence. It should not be treated as a simple success story.

### Dehiwala-Mt. Lavinia coastal plastic case
Source: Kariyawasam, Madhuwanthi & Wilson, “The role of stakeholders in managing polythene and plastic waste in coastal cities of Sri Lanka: A case study of the Dehiwala-Mt. Lavinia municipal council region,” E3S Web of Conferences (2019): https://eprints.qut.edu.au/207950/

The repository record describes a study using 579 households, 182 commercial properties, and 103 institutions. It reports a coastal, high-density, mixed-land-use context in which limited council capacity, weak practices, poor awareness, irregular collection, weak enforcement, negative attitudes, and lack of practical and cost-effective alternatives contributed to plastic release into the environment and canal networks. The study links waste-generator reduction, private collection, and media awareness, and describes environmental disposal through landfill, burning, and water bodies.

Implication: this is a strong candidate for a coastal stakeholder and canal-leakage profile. Its household, commercial, and institutional sample structure is useful for designing proportional comparisons, but its reported totals and correlations remain context-specific.

## Eagle-view theory sources: waste as a general system

### World Bank What a Waste 3.0
Source: World Bank, “What a Waste 3.0: Global Snapshot of Solid Waste Management toward Circularity until 2050,” 2025: https://www.worldbank.org/en/publication/what-a-waste

The World Bank presents municipal solid waste as a system shaped by population growth, urbanization, income, consumption, local-system capacity, and municipal budgets. Its global reference dataset covers waste generation, composition, collection, treatment, disposal, legislation, institutional arrangements, plastics, private-sector participation, employment, environmental impacts, and costs/financing. The source is useful for defining the broad variable universe, while its global averages must not be substituted for Sri Lankan conditions.

### Re-assessing global municipal solid waste generation
Source: Maalouf & Mavropoulos, “Re-assessing global municipal solid waste generation,” Waste Management & Research (2022): https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10114251/

The study argues that waste reporting is difficult to compare because of inconsistent terminology, categories, methodologies, missing unregistered quantities, and exclusion of waste outside formal municipal systems. It uses regression analysis and material-flow analysis to improve cross-comparability. It identifies open burning, uncollected waste, informal collection, rural waste, transient populations, and trade-related waste as important sources of undercounting or boundary confusion.

Implication: Anavaratha’s eagle-view layer must begin with ontology and boundary discipline. Waste numbers are not neutral observations; they are outputs of definitions, institutions, measurement methods, and reporting systems.

### World Bank circular-economy evaluation
Source: World Bank, “Transitioning to a Circular Economy: An Evaluation of the World Bank Group’s Support for Municipal Solid Waste Management (2010–20),” 2022: https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/entities/publication/d1822780-61bc-56de-95a4-1dc8a336dd3d

The evaluation defines the waste hierarchy as prioritizing prevention, reuse, recycling, and recovery before disposal, and describes circular economy as closing loops between extraction, manufacturing, use, and end-of-life materials. It emphasizes that waste impacts cross local and regional boundaries and involve public-health, environmental, social, and economic costs. Its broad lesson for Anavaratha is that waste governance must be assessed in relation to country context, institutional coherence, financing, and implementation effectiveness rather than technology selection alone.
