# Anavaratha: Revised Pre-Field Blueprint

## Executive position

Anavaratha should begin as a **desk-based conceptual and evidence project**, not as an immediate field survey. Its purpose is to build a rigorous model of how waste burdens constrain ecological and social life, and how recovery, restoration, and better collective systems can expand the capability of places and communities to flourish.

The project has two linked directions:

> **Freedom from Waste:** reducing unmanaged waste, exposure, leakage, accumulation, unsafe work, collection failure, ecological damage, and the burden of living near hazards.

> **Freedom to Flourish:** expanding ecological and social capabilities such as healthy soil, living trees, biodiversity, water retention, shade, food production, clean public space, resilience, and meaningful community stewardship.

These directions are connected but not interchangeable. Less waste does not automatically create an ecologically flourishing place, and tree planting does not automatically solve waste-system failures. Anavaratha must measure the mechanisms between them.

## 1. Why the Berlin reference is useful—and where it must be limited

The inspiration from Isaiah Berlin is conceptually useful. Negative liberty is commonly explained as freedom from obstacles or interference, while positive liberty concerns the possibility of acting with control and realizing purposes.[1] However, Berlin’s distinction is primarily about political and social liberty. Anavaratha should therefore use it as a metaphorical starting point, not as a literal environmental theory.

The ecological-development direction is better strengthened by the **capability approach**, which focuses on real or substantive opportunities to achieve valuable doings and beings, not merely on the presence of resources or formal rights.[2] In Anavaratha, a tree-planting budget is a resource; surviving trees, shade, habitat, soil improvement, and community use are capabilities or achieved outcomes.

## 2. The research object

The unit of analysis is not “waste” alone. It is a **place-based socio-ecological system** containing material inflows, human activity, waste flows, ecological assets, institutional arrangements, and feedback loops.

| System component | Desk question |
|---|---|
| Material inflows | What products, resources, and materials enter the place? |
| Human activity | Which households, businesses, markets, institutions, and services use them? |
| Waste generation | What residuals are created, and according to which definitions? |
| Waste pathways | What is reused, recovered, collected, transported, processed, disposed, burned, dumped, or lost? |
| Accumulation | Where do material burdens persist or build up? |
| Ecological assets | Where are trees, wetlands, waterways, soils, vegetation, and habitat corridors? |
| Capabilities | What ecological and social functions can these assets support? |
| Institutions | Who regulates, operates, pays, works, maintains, benefits, or bears the risk? |
| Feedback | How do conditions change future behavior, service quality, ecological health, and choices? |

## 3. Desk-first work programme

The first stage should be completed without assuming immediate field access. It should use official documents, existing databases, statistics, maps, academic studies, administrative records, remote-sensing layers, and carefully documented secondary sources.

| Desk module | Output |
|---|---|
| Conceptual definitions | Glossary for waste burden, ecological capability, recovery, accumulation, leakage, resilience, and flourishing |
| Boundary design | Candidate local-authority or connected-system boundaries and a provisional system map |
| Source inventory | Dataset register with publisher, period, geography, unit, definition, method, licensing, and limitations |
| Waste-flow reconstruction | Provisional flow model separating measured, reported, estimated, and unknown values |
| Ecological baseline | Candidate layers for tree/vegetation cover, wetlands, waterways, land use, flood exposure, and public ecological access |
| Institutional analysis | Responsibilities, policies, operators, funding, enforcement, and missing links |
| Causal reasoning | Hypotheses linking waste burdens to ecological and social outcomes |
| Uncertainty analysis | Evidence grades, conflicts, gaps, and priority questions for later validation |

Sri Lanka already has relevant institutional and policy anchors. The Central Environmental Authority identifies separate policy/technical, enforcement, and special-project roles within its Solid Waste Management Unit, including work related to Dompe and the Gampaha District Integrated Solid Waste Management Project.[3] Sri Lanka’s policy direction also emphasizes resource circulation, waste prevention, reduction of final disposal, public health, ecosystem protection, 3R principles, and circular economy approaches.[4]

## 4. The two-direction indicator system

Do not create one combined score immediately. First, build two transparent indicator families.

| Family | Indicator groups | Early desk proxies |
|---|---|---|
| Freedom from Waste | Burden, service, exposure, leakage, accumulation, safety | Reported generation/collection; route gaps; known disposal points; hotspot reports; proximity to drains and homes |
| Freedom to Flourish | Ecological condition, function, access, resilience, stewardship | Vegetation/tree cover; wetlands and waterways; flood/heat exposure; public ecological space; restoration opportunity |

Each indicator must be labeled **direct measure**, **administrative record**, **proxy**, **modelled estimate**, or **hypothesis**. A proxy is not allowed to silently become an outcome. Tree cover is not equivalent to ecological health. A clean road is not equivalent to a functioning waste system. A compost facility is not equivalent to soil restoration.

## 5. The two-axis matrix

After the indicators are defined, the project may use a two-axis matrix:

| Waste burden | Ecological capability | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| High | Low | Burdened and degraded: containment, safety, service, and repair are urgent |
| High | High | Capable but pressured: protect ecological assets while reducing waste leakage |
| Low | Low | Clean but fragile: prevent new burdens and build ecological capability |
| Low | High | Flourishing and protected: maintain, learn, and replicate |

This matrix is for intervention logic, not for ranking or stigmatizing communities. Every classification must be traceable to underlying indicators and uncertainty.

## 6. The reasoning cycle

Anavaratha should use a repeated cycle:

> **Concept → hypothesis → evidence inventory → harmonization → provisional map → alternative explanations → priority unknowns → future field test → validation → model revision.**

A desk hypothesis should never be written only as “X causes Y.” It should specify the mechanism, expected evidence, alternative explanation, decision relevance, and minimum future validation. For example, irregular collection may be related to uncontrolled disposal, but the same hotspot may also be explained by commercial waste, construction activity, flooding, or land access. The desk phase should keep these alternatives open.

## 7. The pre-field decision gate

Do not enter the field merely because a topic is important. Begin fieldwork only when:

1. The pilot boundary is explicit.
2. The question is specific enough to change a decision.
3. The desk review has identified the relevant records and gaps.
4. The measurement method, sampling logic, and data fields are ready.
5. Permissions, safety, consent, privacy, and data stewardship are in place.
6. The field activity has a defined validation purpose.

The field phase should therefore be a targeted test of desk hypotheses, not an open-ended search for everything that might be relevant.

## 8. First practical deliverables from the desk

The first version of Anavaratha should produce a concept note, two-direction glossary, candidate-pilot comparison, dataset register, evidence-quality matrix, provisional waste-flow diagram, ecological-capability hypothesis map, causal-hypothesis log, uncertainty register, stakeholder map, and field-priority list.

A lightweight prototype can initially be built using a structured spreadsheet and GIS-ready tables. It should show sources and confidence, allow the same place to be viewed through both directions, and preserve unknowns. A dashboard is useful only after the underlying definitions and data lineage are stable.

## 9. Recommended immediate sequence

Begin with a **desk-based inception cycle**. First, agree on whether “Freedom from Waste” and “Freedom to Flourish” express the intended project. Second, choose a provisional geography without treating it as final. Third, collect and catalogue existing evidence. Fourth, write the first causal hypotheses. Fifth, produce the first conceptual map and identify contradictions. Sixth, decide what must be measured later and what can already be answered responsibly from secondary data.

The first milestone should be called **Anavaratha Desk Baseline v0.1**, not “the Sri Lankan waste map.” It should be explicitly provisional, evidence-graded, and designed to improve through later validation.
