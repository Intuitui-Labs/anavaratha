# Anavaratha Waste-Stream Cycles

## Why streams must be separated

“Waste” is not a homogeneous substance. Different streams are generated by different activities, move through different actors and markets, require different technologies, and create different forms of risk or value. A system that is adequate for organic waste may be inadequate for plastics, e-waste, construction debris, medical waste, or wastewater. Anavaratha should therefore explore the waste-stream layer before descending to locations.

## Stream taxonomy

| Stream | Main generators | Possible cycle | Main risks or burdens | Key desk variables |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Organic and food waste | Households, markets, restaurants, agriculture, institutions | Prevention, edible-food recovery, animal feed where safe, composting, anaerobic digestion, controlled disposal | Odour, vectors, methane, leachate, rapid seasonal accumulation | Moisture, contamination, seasonality, collection frequency, land, output demand |
| Plastics and polythene | Packaging, retail, households, services, industry | Reduction, reuse, refill, sorting, washing, recycling, co-processing, controlled residual disposal | Leakage, burning emissions, microplastics, long persistence, contamination | Polymer type, packaging inflow, quality, collection, price, buyer, destination, leakage |
| Paper and cardboard | Commerce, offices, households, schools, retail | Reuse, baling, pulping, recycling, residual disposal | Moisture, contamination, volume, transport burden | Source type, bulk density, price, moisture, recovery actor |
| Glass and metals | Households, hospitality, commerce, construction, industry | Reuse, return, sorting, remelting, resale | Breakage, worker safety, transport weight, resource loss | Material grade, deposit/return systems, buyer, distance, safety |
| Textiles and synthetic fibres | Households, apparel, manufacturing, retail | Reuse, repair, resale, fibre recovery, co-processing, disposal | Microfibres, dyes, mixed-material difficulty, hidden flows | Pre/post-consumer distinction, composition, reuse market, offcuts, destination |
| Construction and demolition waste | Construction, demolition, roads, infrastructure | Reuse, crushing, aggregate recovery, fill, controlled disposal | Dust, obstruction, illegal dumping, land occupation | Project pipeline, material type, truck movement, permitted destination |
| E-waste | Households, firms, schools, institutions, repair markets | Repair, refurbishment, component recovery, material recycling, hazardous disposal | Toxic substances, unsafe dismantling, data/privacy, informal exposure | Product type, lifespan, repair actors, formal/informal destination, hazardous fraction |
| Healthcare and hazardous waste | Hospitals, laboratories, clinics, industry, households | Segregated treatment, sterilization, specialist recovery/disposal | Infection, toxic exposure, chemical release, fire, worker risk | Source, hazard class, segregation, container, treatment, compliance |
| Wastewater and faecal sludge | Households, businesses, institutions, septic systems, industries | Treatment, biogas, nutrient recovery, safe reuse, controlled discharge | Pathogens, nutrients, odour, water pollution, sludge accumulation | Volume, treatment level, emptying, transport, receiving environment |
| Agricultural and biomass residues | Farms, plantations, livestock, food processing | Compost, soil amendment, bioenergy, animal feed where safe, fibre products | Burning, smoke, nutrient loss, seasonal accumulation | Crop calendar, moisture, nutrient content, collection economics |
| Street litter and marine/coastal debris | Public spaces, transport, tourism, rivers, drains, households, fishing | Prevention, capture, sorting, recovery, safe disposal | Drain blockage, flooding, marine pollution, wildlife ingestion | Hotspot, rainfall, drainage, litter type, event calendar, receptor |
| Mixed residual waste | All generators after prevention and recovery | Controlled treatment or disposal | Fire, leachate, methane, land demand, persistent stock | Composition, contamination, inflow, facility control, remaining capacity |

## Distinct cycle questions

For each stream, Anavaratha should ask six questions. What is the upstream inflow and use pattern? Where and why does the material become discard? Which actors separate, collect, buy, transport, process, or dispose of it? What capacity is needed to keep it in a beneficial cycle? What causes leakage, contamination, hazardous exposure, or accumulation? Which residuals remain after recovery and where do they go?

## Stream-specific indicators

| Analytical purpose | Example stream-sensitive indicator |
|---|---|
| Pressure | Kilograms per person, household, establishment, market day, guest-night, project, or operating day |
| Recovery | Share recovered by material grade and pathway, not only total weight |
| Quality | Contamination, moisture, polymer/material grade, usable output, rejected fraction |
| Service | Collection frequency, coverage, route distance, storage time, missed service |
| Facility | Inflow, usable capacity, downtime, residual fraction, output quality, environmental controls |
| Leakage | Items or mass per road, drain, shoreline, catchment, or observed hotspot |
| Stock | Accumulated volume, remaining capacity, persistence, fire/leachate risk, remediation need |
| Equity | Worker exposure, receiving-community burden, service inequality, and who captures recovered value |

## Cross-stream interactions

Streams often mix and change one another’s value. Organic material contaminates paper and plastics. Plastic packaging enters food and market waste. E-waste can enter mixed household waste. Construction waste can block drains and alter litter pathways. Wastewater and solid waste interact through drainage and leachate. A stream model must therefore record both separated fractions and points of mixing.

## Stream hierarchy for initial desk work

A practical first pass should begin with three broad families: **biodegradable flows**, **recoverable materials**, and **residual/hazardous flows**. The model can then disaggregate into the detailed streams above wherever evidence supports it. This avoids excessive detail before the major pathways are understood while preventing plastic, organic, hazardous, and residual waste from being treated as interchangeable.

## What this layer must deliver

Before descending to national or local applications, the waste-stream layer should provide a stream ontology, generator-to-destination pathway for each major category, stream-specific pressure and capacity indicators, mixing and contamination points, value and burden pathways, and a list of data gaps. Only then can Sri Lankan national data be interpreted responsibly.
