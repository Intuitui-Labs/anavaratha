# Anavaratha Law–Capacity–Reality Model v0.1

## Central proposition

A legal rule does not become environmental protection merely because it exists. It must pass through an institutional chain:

> **Rule → duty → responsible actor → detection capacity → evidence → enforcement action → adjudication → sanction or remedy → payment/restoration → repeated verification → persistent compliance.**

Failure at any link can produce a gap between formal compliance and lived reality.

## Three realities to keep separate

| Reality | What it records | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Legal reality | What the law prohibits, requires, empowers, or penalizes | Open burning prohibited; soil pollution offence; CEA direction power |
| Administrative reality | What institutions report, plan, license, inspect, record, or claim | Waste plan, licence, facility audit, complaint register, council report |
| Material/ecological reality | What physically happens to waste and receiving environments | Dumping, burning, leakage, accumulation, forest or wetland exposure |

A fourth layer should be added for justice:

| Justice reality | What it records | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Social/legal outcome | Who bears harm, who can complain, who receives remedy, and who is held responsible | Residents’ rights case, restoration direction, compensation, litigation costs |

## Compliance-production capacity

To show compliance to the world, an institution may need only a policy document, target, certificate, or summarized percentage. To produce actual compliance, it needs a functioning operational and evidentiary chain.

| Capacity required | Operational question | Evidence to seek |
|---|---|---|
| Rule awareness | Do generators, collectors, councils, and regulators know the applicable rule? | Guidance, training, notices, interviews |
| Detection | Can illegal dumping or burning be observed reliably? | Patrols, CCTV, complaints, sensors, inspections |
| Attribution | Can waste be linked to a person, company, vehicle, council, or contractor? | GPS, route logs, packaging, manifests, chain of custody |
| Investigation | Can evidence survive challenge? | Inspection forms, photographs, sampling, witness statements |
| Legal action | Can a notice, directive, charge, or case be initiated? | Notices, directives, court filings, licence actions |
| Adjudication | Can the court or authority determine responsibility? | Case record, judgment, sentence |
| Sanction | Is a fine, imprisonment, cost, or corrective order imposed? | Court order and payment record |
| Restoration | Is environmental harm corrected or contained? | Restoration plan, completion evidence, monitoring |
| Persistence | Does compliance continue after attention or project support falls? | Repeated inspections, longitudinal measurements |
| Transparency | Can outsiders verify the claim? | Public data, audit trails, raw records, independent review |

## The paper–reality gap

The gap should not be framed as a moral accusation that all official reporting is false. It is an empirical question about **measurement boundaries and incentives**. A report may legitimately show that a target was adopted, a facility was built, a collection percentage was reported, or a legal instrument exists. None of these alone proves that waste stopped entering a forest, lake, wetland, waterway, or informal disposal site.

The first diagnostic is therefore to compare four denominators:

1. **Reported denominator:** what the institution counts.
2. **Service denominator:** what enters the official collection or treatment system.
3. **Material denominator:** what is actually generated and moves through all formal and informal pathways.
4. **Receiving-burden denominator:** what accumulates in land, water, ecosystems, workers, and nearby communities.

A system may appear compliant under the reported denominator while remaining materially unsafe under the receiving-burden denominator.

## Meethotamulla as the anchor case

The official Supreme Court judgment is a direct example of the gap. It found unlawful dumping by Colombo Municipal Council and Kolonnawa Urban Council, failures by the Urban Development Authority and Western Province Waste Management Authority, and non-enforcement by the CEA. The Court issued restoration, cost-bearing, and future programme directions, but did not impose individual penal consequences in that fundamental-rights proceeding.[1]

This case should be coded across separate dimensions: legal violation, institutional responsibility, operational capacity, environmental harm, judicial remedy, absence of individual penal consequences, and required future compliance. It must not be reduced to “a fine was imposed” or “the law failed.”

## Nuwara Eliya as a controlled verification lead

The 2016 JICA survey documents Moonplains as a formally upgraded semi-engineered sanitary landfill approximately 1 km inside a forest reservation, receiving an average of 26 tonnes/day during an 11-day observation period. It also records no source segregation at that time, rainy-season access constraints, a perennial stream below the valley, operating records, staff, and machinery.[2]

This does not prove that the current council illegally dumps waste in a forest or lake. It does establish a strong verification design: compare the legal and administrative status of Moonplains with current physical conditions, receiving pathways, maintenance, collection practices, seasonal pressures, and downstream ecological risks.

## Anavaratha’s compliance test

For every future compliance claim, Anavaratha should ask:

> **Compliant with what rule, by which actor, over what area and period, measured through what evidence, against which denominator, with what consequence for non-compliance, and with what proof that the condition persisted?**
