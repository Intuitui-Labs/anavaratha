# Anavaratha Formal-Compliance vs Material-Reality Comparison v0.1

## Purpose

This comparison tests whether formal legal and administrative signals are sufficient to infer material waste-system performance. They are not. The rows below keep legal text, reported administration, physical operation, and verified outcomes separate.

| Formal signal | What it can legitimately show | Reality signal | What remains unknown |
|---|---|---|---|
| Open-burning regulation exists | Open burning is legally prohibited and a penalty path exists | Recent reporting describes continued dumping and public warning signage; open-burning prevalence is not quantified here | Detection, prosecutions, fines paid, and current prevalence |
| CEA has inspection, investigation, direction, and regulatory powers | A central institution has formal authority | Meethotamulla judgment found CEA failed to enforce against unlawful activity during 2009–2017 | Current inspection density, staffing, case throughput, and post-2026 performance |
| Local authorities have statutory waste obligations | Councils are legally responsible for relevant local waste functions | Supreme Court found CMC and KUC dumping unlawful and held them responsible for rights infringements | Whether current councils have adequate vehicles, staff, land, routes, and alternatives |
| Waste facility is upgraded or described as sanitary/semi-engineered | Capital intervention and a formal technical status exist | JICA described Moonplains as a semi-engineered model facility but also recorded mixed waste, no source segregation, rainy-season access difficulty, and forest-reservation location | Current condition, maintenance, leachate, stream protection, and present-day disposal practice |
| Collection coverage percentage is reported | A survey or administrative system recorded service access/use within a defined frame | Nuwara Eliya JICA survey reported high household collection access but lower daily frequency and mixed disposal at the site | Uncollected informal, seasonal, commercial, remote, and illegal pathways |
| National action plan includes targets and activities | The state has identified actions and intended outputs | Action register currently contains targets/plans, not measured implementation outcomes | Completion, financing, operational quality, persistence, and external verification |
| Court orders restoration and future disposal programmes | Judicial remedy and accountability have been established | Meethotamulla judgment notes past institutional failure and absence of individual penal consequences in that proceeding | Implementation of directions, current site condition, and recurrence elsewhere |
| Public warning board states a Rs. 5,000 fine | A local deterrence message is displayed | Daily Mirror report says CCTV still captured dumping near Athurugiriya | Whether warning was lawful/current, whether offenders were identified, and whether fines were imposed or collected |

## Core interpretation

The strongest available evidence does not justify a single statement such as “Sri Lanka has no enforcement” or “Sri Lanka is compliant.” It supports a more precise diagnosis: **formal authority and interventions coexist with incomplete detection, uneven operational capacity, distributed responsibility, incomplete public evidence, and continuing reports of unmanaged disposal**.

The gap is best represented as a chain:

> **Legal possibility → institutional capability → operational performance → material pathway → ecological and social outcome → persistence.**

A breakdown can occur between any two stages. For example, the law may prohibit open burning, but a remote site may lack detection. A council may have a collection percentage, but the statistic may exclude floating populations, commercial activity, uncollected waste, or waste transferred informally. A landfill may be formally upgraded, but rainy-season access, maintenance, leachate control, or source separation may remain weak.

## False-compliance indicators to test, not assume

Anavaratha should investigate the following patterns as hypotheses:

1. **Boundary compliance:** a facility meets its permit boundary while waste is displaced to roadsides, forests, waterways, or neighboring authorities.
2. **Target compliance:** an activity or target is reported as complete while the output is not independently measured.
3. **Coverage compliance:** collection coverage refers to households reached, while waste from floating populations, institutions, markets, and seasonal activity remains outside the denominator.
4. **Capital compliance:** a facility is built or upgraded while recurrent operations, maintenance, staffing, access, or markets are insufficient.
5. **Legal compliance:** fines or rules exist in law, but detection, attribution, prosecution, payment, or restoration is not observable.
6. **Project compliance:** a donor-supported intervention performs during the project period but weakens after external support ends.
7. **Spatial compliance:** a locality appears cleaner because burden has moved to a receiving site outside its administrative boundary.

These are not conclusions about any particular actor. They are testable mechanisms for the next evidence round.

## Data design consequence

Every future dataset should carry these fields where possible: legal provision; responsible actor; administrative boundary; material stream; period; denominator; observed/modelled/planned status; source; evidence strength; enforcement stage; physical destination; receiving burden; and persistence check.
