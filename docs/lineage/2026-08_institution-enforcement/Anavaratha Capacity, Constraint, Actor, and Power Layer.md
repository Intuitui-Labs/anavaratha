# Anavaratha Capacity, Constraint, Actor, and Power Layer

## 1. Why capacity must be expanded

Waste systems are often described as lacking capacity, but “capacity” can mean very different things. A local authority may have a vehicle but no fuel, a landfill but no usable remaining capacity, a recycling facility but no clean feedstock, a policy but no enforcement, or data but no institutional practice for using it. Anavaratha should therefore treat capacity as a multidimensional and relational concept.

## 2. Capacity dimensions

| Capacity | What it enables | What to examine |
|---|---|---|
| Physical | Storage, collection, transport, processing, treatment, and containment | Land, roads, vehicles, bins, transfer points, facilities, safety equipment |
| Operational | Repeated daily service | Routes, schedules, staffing, fuel, maintenance, downtime, supervision |
| Financial | Recurrent service and adaptation | Budget, cost recovery, fees, contracts, grants, maintenance, financial continuity |
| Technical | Appropriate design and safe operation | Skills, standards, repair, quality control, monitoring, environmental safeguards |
| Institutional | Coordination and accountability | Mandates, authority, reporting lines, procurement, enforcement, inter-agency links |
| Political | Continuity and legitimacy | Leadership, public commitment, conflict management, policy stability, land decisions |
| Social | Participation and behavior change | Trust, convenience, norms, information, voice, community organization |
| Market | Recovery and reuse | Buyers, prices, quality, aggregation, transport, demand, producer responsibility |
| Informational | Knowing and governing the system | Definitions, data continuity, coverage, metadata, verification, use in decisions |
| Ecological | Receiving and recovering without harmful overload | Drainage, soil, water, habitat, flood pathways, assimilation and regeneration limits |

## 3. Constraints are mechanisms

A constraint is not merely a missing input. It is the mechanism through which a system fails to meet a demand or requirement. For each constraint, Anavaratha should record the pressure it interacts with, the capacity it limits, the actor who can change it, the actor who bears its consequence, its time dependence, and the spatial location where the consequence appears.

| Constraint type | Example mechanism | Typical visible symptom |
|---|---|---|
| Scale mismatch | A standardized route or facility does not fit settlement density or authority size | Missed collection, overflow, illegal dumping |
| Land constraint | No socially and legally acceptable place for transfer or final disposal | Long transport, protest, uncontrolled disposal |
| Operating constraint | Vehicles, staff, fuel, or maintenance are insufficient for scheduled service | Irregular collection, roadside accumulation |
| Finance constraint | Capital investment is not matched by recurrent operating funds | Idle facility, broken equipment, project decay |
| Quality constraint | Mixed or contaminated material cannot meet processing or buyer standards | Rejection, low recovery, low price |
| Coordination constraint | Agencies have overlapping or disconnected mandates | Delayed action, duplicated work, unowned gaps |
| Political constraint | Decisions change with leadership, conflict, or land politics | Interrupted projects, uneven enforcement |
| Market constraint | Recovered material has no viable buyer or transport economics | Stockpiling, disposal of recoverables |
| Data constraint | Definitions or updates are inconsistent | Conflicting totals, weak planning, unaccountable targets |
| Ecological constraint | Drainage, flood, soil, water, or habitat cannot safely receive residuals | Leakage, flooding, contamination, marine transport |

## 4. Actor perspectives

A waste system is not governed by one actor. The same action may be a cost for one actor, an income for another, and a public benefit for a third. Every major claim should be read through at least five perspectives.

| Actor | Core question |
|---|---|
| Generator | What choices, costs, information, and conveniences shape discard? |
| Worker and informal actor | What hidden recovery, exposure, labor, and knowledge exist? |
| Operator or contractor | What operational conditions determine daily performance? |
| Authority and regulator | Who has mandate, finance, land, enforcement, and accountability? |
| Buyer or downstream processor | What quality, price, volume, and transport conditions determine recovery? |
| Receiving community | Who receives noise, traffic, odour, health risks, land-use limits, or stigma? |
| Ecological receptor | What happens to water, soil, air, habitat, and future recovery capacity? |

## 5. Power and burden distribution

Power should be treated as the ability to define categories, control decisions, allocate land and finance, set standards, withhold cooperation, capture recovered value, or transfer risk. A system may appear technically efficient while distributing risks unevenly to workers, low-income communities, peripheral locations, or downstream ecosystems.

Anavaratha should ask: Who decides what counts as waste? Who controls the route, facility, contract, market, or data? Who can refuse a facility? Who is visible in official records? Who captures value from recovery? Who bears exposure when the pathway fails? Which future population inherits the accumulated stock?

## 6. What this layer must deliver

Before selecting any local case, the project should have a general capacity ontology, a constraint-mechanism taxonomy, an actor map, a power and burden-distribution framework, and indicators for usable—not merely nominal—capacity. This layer will later explain why the same waste stream may produce different outcomes in different system types.
