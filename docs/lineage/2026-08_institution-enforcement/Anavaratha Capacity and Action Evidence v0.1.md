# Anavaratha Capacity and Action Evidence v0.1

## Status

This document combines real evidence from the World Bank What a Waste 3.0 Sri Lanka subset, Sri Lanka’s National Action Plan on Plastic Waste Management 2021–2030, the Western Province implementation study, and the 2024 Sri Lanka biodiversity/plastic issue brief. It does not claim to be a complete capacity inventory. It separates measured values, categorical statuses, reported conditions, policy targets, and recommendations.

## Capacity evidence from Sri Lankan studies and reports

| Capacity domain | Real evidence | Analytical interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Institutional staffing | The Western Province implementation study reports that normally five officers were responsible for SWM in each local government and ten in Colombo Municipal Council in its study design.[1] | A staffing count is an input proxy; it does not establish workload, competence, continuity, or service quality |
| Physical capacity | The same study identifies insufficient land, vehicles, instruments, and modern technology as implementation problems.[1] | Capacity gaps may occur at land, fleet, equipment, and technology levels |
| Operational capacity | The 2024 issue brief reports lack of maintenance and mismanagement at some waste sites, including poorly maintained leachate infrastructure.[2] | Nominal facility presence may overstate usable capacity |
| Technical and quality capacity | The issue brief reports poor sorting, mixed biodegradable/plastic/medical waste, low-quality compost, weak market trust, and low prices for mixed plastics.[2] | Quality and market capacity constrain recovery even when collection exists |
| Occupational capacity | The issue brief identifies insufficient OHS measures for workers.[2] | A system may move material while transferring burden to labor |
| Ecological capacity | The brief identifies waste sites near or within Key Biodiversity Areas and calls for infrastructure, monitoring, and protection of ecological receptors.[2] | Receiving ecology must be included in the capacity and burden ledger |
| Data capacity | Sri Lanka’s plastic action plan states that an inventory dataset was not available and identifies deficiencies in data and information.[3] | Information capacity is a core constraint, not a secondary administrative issue |

## Action architecture

The National Action Plan sets goals for inventory, single-use-plastic reduction, collection and safe treatment, packaging reduction, and marine-pollution reduction. It proposes steering committees, plastic-flow tracking, producer registration, facility audits, community collection, buyer–seller platforms, local-government by-laws, monitoring cells, and local-authority waste profiles.[3]

These records describe intended actions and implementation architecture. They do not prove that the actions were completed, effective, or persistent. The current status of each action should therefore be coded as planned, initiated, completed, verified, persistent, failed, or unknown.

## Action-side interpretation

The most important distinction for Anavaratha is between **action presence** and **action performance**. A policy, target, committee, facility, vehicle, training, or campaign is not an outcome. A defensible action evaluation needs a baseline, a defined intervention, a time period, a comparison or counterfactual where possible, a measurable outcome, and evidence of persistence or spillover.

The present evidence suggests that the action problem is not simply the absence of interventions. Sri Lanka has policies, plans, programs, facilities, private initiatives, community efforts, and institutional responsibilities. The deeper question is how these actions connect into sustained pathways, whether recurrent finance and maintenance exist, whether responsibilities are clear, whether material quality supports markets, and whether benefits and burdens are distributed equitably.

## Immediate data requirements

The next real-data search should prioritize: facility registries with coordinates and operating status; fleet and route records; staffing and worker arrangements; local-government budgets and recurrent costs; project start/end dates; equipment maintenance and downtime; collection and recovery records; contracts and buyers; facility inflow and residuals; by-law and enforcement records; independent evaluations; and post-project status.
