# Anavaratha Legal and Enforcement Map v0.1

## Purpose

This map organizes Sri Lanka’s waste-related legal framework as an enforcement chain rather than a list of laws. It distinguishes the legal duty, prohibited conduct, responsible institution, enforcement instrument, penalty or remedy, and evidence needed to determine whether compliance exists in practice.

## Penalty interpretation

A fine range in a statute is not an enforcement outcome. Anavaratha must record at least seven separate stages: suspected conduct, complaint or detection, inspection/evidence collection, notice or directive, charge/prosecution, conviction and sentence, and payment/remediation/verified compliance.

The 2017 open-burning Gazette is clear about the prohibition but does not state an independent fine amount; it directs that non-compliance is punishable under section 31 of the National Environmental Act. Since the 2026 amendment introduced section 23RR and altered multiple penalty clauses, the current penalty path for a specific open-burning case must be checked against the amended/consolidated law and the actual charge. Older public references to LKR 5,000 or LKR 10,000 should not be presented as the current general fine without case-specific legal verification.

## Enforcement institutions

The CEA is the central environmental regulator and has powers relating to standards, directions, inspections, investigations, complaints, licensing, and coordination. Local authorities are responsible for municipal waste services and are subject to environmental duties. Provincial waste-management authorities, urban-development bodies, divisional and district institutions, police, Magistrates’ Courts, project-approving agencies, and conservation or forest authorities may become relevant depending on the site and conduct.

The Meethotamulla judgment is especially important because the Court found not only the Colombo Municipal Council and Kolonnawa Urban Council responsible, but also the Urban Development Authority, Western Province Waste Management Authority, and CEA responsible for different failures. This demonstrates that “the authority” is not a single actor; responsibility is distributed across disposal decisions, land allocation, oversight, regulation, and local operations.

## Evidence needed to establish real compliance

| Question | Minimum evidence |
|---|---|
| Was the conduct detected? | Complaint, inspection record, CCTV, GPS, photograph, witness, or sampling record |
| Was the responsible party identified? | Waste traceability, vehicle/route records, packaging, admission, or chain of custody |
| Was a legal power used? | Notice, directive, licence action, seizure, charge sheet, or court filing |
| Was there a conviction? | Court record identifying offence, defendant, finding, and sentence |
| Was the fine paid? | Payment record, enforcement ledger, or court confirmation |
| Was harm corrected? | Restoration plan, completion record, environmental monitoring, and independent verification |
| Did compliance persist? | Repeated inspections or measurements over time |
| Did burden move elsewhere? | Destination records, spatial monitoring, complaints, and receiving-site evidence |
