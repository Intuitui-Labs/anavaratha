# Anavaratha Law–Capacity–Reality Evidence v0.1

## Executive finding

Sri Lanka has a substantial legal and institutional architecture for environmental protection and waste control. The harder question is whether that architecture can consistently detect, attribute, prosecute, remedy, and prevent waste disposal that occurs outside formal reporting systems.

The evidence supports a cautious but powerful conclusion:

> **The central problem is not simply absence of law. It is the gap between legal authority, operational capacity, evidentiary capacity, and material reality.**

This gap must be studied without assuming that every official report is false. A policy target, licence, facility, collection percentage, or compliance statement may be accurate within its own boundary while open dumping, burning, leakage, or accumulated burden continues outside that boundary.

## 1. Current law

The National Environmental Act No. 47 of 1980 establishes the Central Environmental Authority and gives it powers relating to standards, pollution control, coordination, inspection, investigation, complaints, information, and directions to local authorities. Sections 10(1)(i) and 12 are particularly important because they connect central environmental authority with local-authority environmental duties.[1]

The 2017 National Environmental (Prohibition of Open Burning of Refuse and Other Combustible Matters Inclusive of Plastics) Regulations No. 1 of 2017 prohibit a person from openly burning, or causing, allowing, or permitting the open burning of refuse and combustible matter including plastics. The Gazette states that non-compliance is an offence punishable under the National Environmental Act.[2]

The National Environmental (Amendment) Act No. 15 of 2026 was endorsed on 21 July 2026.[3] The amendment changes the soil-pollution penalty in section 23N to LKR 100,000–1,000,000, with LKR 5,000 per day for continuation after conviction, and allows corrective and restorative orders. It also inserts section 23RR, providing a general penalty of LKR 250,000–15,000,000, imprisonment up to two years, or both, for offences under the Act or regulations where no penalty is expressly provided. The exact current penalty applicable to a particular open-burning or dumping charge must be checked against the amended law, the regulation, and the charge used in the case.

## 2. What the law requires versus what enforcement requires

| Legal layer | Formal provision | Capacity required in practice |
|---|---|---|
| Prohibition | Open burning and harmful land pollution are prohibited | Detection in dispersed locations, especially at night or in remote areas |
| Local-government duty | Local authorities must perform statutory waste and environmental responsibilities | Vehicles, land, staff, finance, technical planning, operating alternatives |
| CEA authority | CEA may inspect, investigate, direct, coordinate, and regulate | Inspectors, laboratories, legal officers, transport, case management, follow-up |
| Penalty | Fines, imprisonment, continuing fines, and corrective measures may apply | Attribution, admissible evidence, timely prosecution, enforceable records |
| Restoration | Court or authority may require corrective action and restoration | Funding, technical design, responsible actor, monitoring, proof of completion |
| Persistence | Implied by continuing duties and follow-up | Repeated inspections and measurements after the case or project ends |

A fine in the statute is therefore only the outer legal possibility. Anavaratha must record whether there was detection, attribution, inspection, notice, charge, conviction, sentence, payment, restoration, and later verification.

## 3. Primary court evidence: Meethotamulla

The official Supreme Court judgment in SC/FR Applications 311/2015 and 318/2015 found Colombo Municipal Council primarily responsible for unlawful disposal, health hazards, pollution, and consequences including 40 deaths at Pothuvil Kumbura during 2009–2017. Kolonnawa Urban Council was found secondarily responsible. The Court held the dumping contrary to statutory obligations under the Municipal Councils Ordinance and Urban Councils Ordinance and contrary to prohibitions under sections 23B, 23H, 23K, and 23N of the National Environmental Act.[4]

The Court also found responsibility involving the Urban Development Authority, the Western Province Waste Management Authority, and the CEA for land-use, oversight, and enforcement failures. Relief included restoration and mitigation, costs to be jointly borne by CMC and KUC, and directions to establish and operationalize proper disposal programs within six years, including review of Aruwakkalu operations.[4]

Crucially, the Court expressly stated that the judgment did **not** impose individual penal consequences because of the nature of the jurisdiction, the evidence placed before the Court, and the absence of evidence of individual lapses. It also made no further compensation order because Rs. 908,314,020.70 had already been paid to affected persons, while ordering CMC and KUC to jointly pay litigation costs.[4]

This case is therefore not correctly summarized as “the council was fined.” It is evidence of institutional legal accountability, remedial orders, cost allocation, and the limits of a fundamental-rights proceeding as a criminal-penalty mechanism.

## 4. Other evidence of dumping and sensitive-site exposure

The Environmental Foundation reports that SCFR 243/2017 challenged alleged waste disposal at Muthurajawela Sanctuary and Kotikawatta Dumping Grounds. Its case summary states that proceedings ended in 2022 after the national policy had been tabled and circulated. The full judgment and any subsequent enforcement records still need to be retrieved.[5]

A 2026 Mongabay report, citing named officials and experts, reports continued reliance on open dumping, more than 260 open dumpsites including around 20 large sites, limited collection relative to generated waste, weak segregation, capacity constraints, and local-level enforcement problems. Because this is current journalism rather than a directly retrieved official dataset, Anavaratha should treat these figures as high-value leads requiring triangulation.[6]

A July 2026 Daily Mirror CCTV report near Athurugiriya shows continued dumping despite a warning board stating a Rs. 5,000 fine. It verifies the existence of a deterrence message and reported continued disposal, but not that a fine was imposed, collected, or prosecuted.[7]

## 5. Nuwara Eliya verification lead

The user’s recollection of a scenic lake and forest-linked disposal is not yet a verified current allegation. However, the JICA survey provides a strong factual lead. Its Nuwara Eliya Municipal Council section documents Lake Gregory as a tourist attraction and reports approximately 20,000 daily floating visitors, with much higher seasonal pressure. It records 97% household collection coverage in the 2015 survey, no source segregation at the disposal site, and an average of 26 tonnes/day received at Moonplains during an 11-day observation period.[8]

The same survey states that Moonplains was approximately 1 km inside a forest reservation, surrounded by plantation forest, with a perennial stream at the bottom of the valley used to discharge treated effluent from landfill and toilet-waste treatment facilities. It was upgraded as a semi-engineered sanitary landfill with JICA assistance in 2003. The survey also records rainy-season access problems, site records, staff, machinery, and legal-adherence fields.[8]

This does **not** prove that the current council is unlawfully dumping in a forest or lake. It demonstrates why formal status and physical reality must be checked together: a site can be formally upgraded, administratively recorded, and still require verification of current operation, maintenance, leachate, stream protection, seasonal overflow, source segregation, and surrounding ecological conditions.

## 6. The Anavaratha reality-gap test

For every future compliance claim, Anavaratha should ask:

> **Compliant with which rule, by which actor, over what boundary and period, measured through what evidence, against which denominator, with what consequence for non-compliance, and with what proof that the condition persisted?**

The minimum record chain is:

1. Legal rule and effective date.
2. Responsible actor and site boundary.
3. Material and conduct involved.
4. Detection or complaint.
5. Attribution and evidence chain.
6. Notice, directive, licence action, or charge.
7. Court or administrative outcome.
8. Fine, imprisonment, cost, corrective, or restoration order.
9. Payment and implementation.
10. Follow-up inspection or measurement.
11. Persistence and spillover to other sites or communities.

## 7. Present evidence gaps

The present review has found strong legal texts, a primary Supreme Court judgment, a primary JICA survey, policy documents, and recent reported leads. It has **not** yet found a complete public dataset of waste-related complaints, inspections, prosecutions, convictions, fines imposed, fines paid, restoration orders, or repeat violations by location and year.

That absence is itself an Anavaratha research finding, but it must not be turned into the claim that enforcement never occurs. The next evidence request should target CEA annual reports, police and Magistrate case records, local-authority complaint and fine registers, environmental-protection licence records, parliamentary questions, audit reports, court registries, and current site-monitoring data.
