# Anavaratha Capacity and Action-Side Data Ontology

## Purpose

The waste-pressure data already assembled describe population, generation, composition, collection, and disposal. They do not explain what a system can do or whether an intervention changes outcomes. This ontology adds the capacity and action side without collapsing assets, activities, targets, and outcomes into one score.

## Capacity layers

| Layer | Definition | Example variables | Evidence status to record |
|---|---|---|---|
| Asset capacity | What exists on paper or physically | Vehicles, bins, transfer stations, composters, sorting machines, landfills | Existence, specification, owner, location |
| Usable capacity | What can function under current conditions | Operating hours, downtime, fuel, maintenance, staff, access, contamination | Measured, reported, inferred |
| Throughput capacity | Quantity and quality that can be processed | Tonnes/day, route loads, facility inflow, recovery output, rejection rate | Unit, period, material boundary |
| Recurrent capacity | Ability to sustain the system | Budget, fees, salaries, fuel, repairs, contracts, replacement cycles | Annual/monthly and funding source |
| Institutional capacity | Ability to decide, coordinate, regulate, procure, enforce, and report | Mandate, staffing, committees, data system, accountability | Formal versus practiced |
| Market capacity | Ability to receive and pay for recovered material | Buyers, prices, quality thresholds, transport, demand | Current, seasonal, uncertain |
| Social capacity | Ability to participate and protect workers/communities | Convenience, trust, safety, voice, organization, inclusion | Survey, program record, qualitative |
| Ecological capacity | Ability of receiving environments to absorb or recover | Drainage, water, soil, habitat, flood, remediation limits | Ecological proxy, measured impact |

## Action layers

| Action layer | What it means | What must not be assumed |
|---|---|---|
| Policy target | Intended future condition | Target is not achievement |
| Enabling action | Law, standard, coordination mechanism, registration, financing | Enabling action is not implementation |
| Implementation activity | Training, route change, facility construction, source-separation campaign | Activity is not outcome |
| Output | Immediate deliverable | Output is not system performance |
| Outcome | Change in collection, recovery, leakage, cost, risk, or burden | Outcome requires baseline and comparison |
| Persistence | Whether change remains after support or leadership changes | Early success is not durable change |
| Spillover | Effects outside the intervention boundary | Improvement in one node may transfer burden elsewhere |

## Action evidence fields

Every action record should include the problem addressed, responsible actor, target population or material, geographic boundary, start and end date, funding and operating model, implementation status, output, intended outcome, observed outcome, evidence source, independent verification, persistence status, and unintended effects.

## Minimum capacity-action variables

The first real-data collection should seek: local-authority population and households; waste generated and collected; route and service coverage; fleet count and availability; staffing and worker arrangements; facility type, ownership, location, design and usable throughput; operating and maintenance records; budgets and contracts; recovery buyers and destinations; legal and institutional mandates; projects and their dates; targets and KPIs; independent evaluations; and evidence of persistence or failure.

## Non-negotiable distinction

A facility, vehicle, plan, budget, committee, or technology is an **input or enabling condition**. It becomes capacity only when it is usable in a functioning pathway. An intervention becomes successful only when a defined outcome changes relative to a baseline and the change persists without merely transferring burden.
