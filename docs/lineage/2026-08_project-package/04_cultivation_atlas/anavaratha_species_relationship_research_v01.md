# Anavaratha

## Species relationship research

**Candidate relationship: Yellow-eared Bulbul and fruiting plants in Horton Plains — Version 0.1**

## 1. Source

[Frugivory of Yellow-eared Bulbul (*Pycnonotus penicillatus*) and Seasonal Variation of Fruiting Phenology in Tropical Montane Cloud Forests of Horton Plains National Park, Sri Lanka](https://ijms.sljol.info/articles/10.4038/ijms.v6i1.90), P. H. S. P. Chandrasiri and W. A. D. Mahaulpatha, *International Journal of Multidisciplinary Studies* 6(1), 2019, pp. 34–47, DOI 10.4038/ijms.v6i1.90.

## 2. Directly documented relationship

The study investigated the endemic and threatened Yellow-eared Bulbul at Horton Plains National Park from September 2015 to November 2017. It used focal-animal sampling and faecal analysis to identify food items, identified feeding plants using field guides, tagged ten individuals per plant species for fruit-phenology observations, and estimated ripe-fruit cover.

The abstract reports that the bird consumed 16 feeding-plant species in 11 families, including six endemic, eight native, and one introduced species. *Rubus ellipticus* was the major fruit source. Faecal analysis identified seeds from nine plant species. This directly supports a documented frugivory relationship between *P. penicillatus* and multiple fruiting plants, while the seed evidence indicates that the bird ingested seeds. The abstract does not by itself establish successful post-defecation germination or the full effectiveness of the bird as a seed disperser for each plant, so the relationship should be labelled **frugivory with seed-ingestion evidence**, not automatically “confirmed effective seed dispersal.”

## 3. Seasonal evidence

The study reports that maximum ripe-fruit cover was recorded from *Solanum mauritianum* during the northeast monsoon, first inter-monsoon, and second inter-monsoon seasons. During the southwest monsoon, the highest ripe-fruit cover was recorded from *Berberis ceylanica*. The study also reports correlations between feeding attempts and ripe-fruit cover for *Symplocos bractealis*, *S. mauritianum*, and *Strobilanthes viscosa*, and between feeding attempts and the number of fruiting trees for *Elaeocarpus coreaceus*, *Passiflora tripartita*, *Eugenia mabaeoides*, and *S. viscosa*.

This gives Anavaratha a useful seasonal relationship record: bird feeding and fruit availability should be studied as a changing community pattern rather than as a fixed pair of species. The study period and Horton Plains geography must remain attached to the record.

## 5. Cultivation relevance

The value of this record is not that it identifies a plant to cultivate immediately. It demonstrates why a living-sector atlas should connect bird status, fruiting plants, habitat, seasonality, and evidence method. A cultivation or restoration decision in montane landscapes would need to consider the broader floral community and seasonal fruit availability, not only one preferred fruit source.

The study itself recommends monitoring seasonal fruiting phenology in and around Horton Plains for conservation purposes. Anavaratha should therefore treat seasonal records as a future evidence layer and should not substitute a static species list for ecological timing.

## 6. Remaining verification

The full article should be consulted to identify all 16 feeding plants, all nine seed-bearing species, the exact sampling design, faecal-analysis limitations, and whether any germination or post-dispersal evidence was measured. Current status, distribution, legal protection, and sensitive-location considerations for the bird and plant species should be checked against appropriate Sri Lankan and global sources before wider atlas use.

## 7. Full-study verification

The full article confirms the study design and refines the relationship. From December 2015 to November 2017, focal-animal sampling recorded feeding attempts, while feeding plants were identified to species level using field guides. Faecal samples were collected in the field, stored in 70% ethanol, and examined in the laboratory; observed seeds were separated and examined under a binocular microscope. For fruiting phenology, ten individuals of each identified plant species were geolocated, three branches per tagged tree were observed monthly through a spotting scope, and ripe-fruit percentage was estimated. Monthly observations were summarised into four seasons: first inter-monsoon (March–April), southwest monsoon (May–September), second inter-monsoon (October–November), and northeast monsoon (December–February).

The 16 feeding plants listed in the study are *Rubus ellipticus*, *Solanum mauritianum*, *Symplocos bractealis*, *Sarcococca brevifolia*, *Rhodomyrtus tomentosa*, *Rubus leucocarpus*, *Passiflora tripartita*, *Berberis ceylanica*, *Cestrum fasciculatum*, *Elaeocarpus coreaceus*, *Solanum lasiocarpum*, *Rubus indicus*, *Callophyllum walkeri*, *Syzygium rotundifolium*, *Eugenia mabaeoides*, and *Strobilanthes viscosa*. The study reports 108 faecal samples and identifies seeds of nine plant species. It reports that *Rubus ellipticus* was the major fruit source, and that seeds of *Rubus ellipticus* and *Passiflora tripartita* occurred in the greatest number of faecal samples, with *Solanum mauritianum*, *Symplocos bractealis*, *Berberis ceylanica*, *Rubus leucocarpus*, and *Cestrum fasciculatum* occurring to a lesser extent, and *Sarcococca brevifolia* and *Rubus indicus* in the least number of samples.

The full study reports significant positive associations between feeding attempts and ripe-fruit cover for *Symplocos bractealis* (Spearman rho 0.403, p=0.037), *Solanum mauritianum* (rho 0.551, p=0.003), and *Strobilanthes viscosa* (rho 0.452, p=0.018). It reports significant associations between feeding attempts and the number of trees in fruiting for *Elaeocarpus coreaceus* (rho 0.463, p=0.015), *Passiflora tripartita* (rho 0.487, p=0.010), *Eugenia mabaeoides* (rho 0.380, p=0.050), and *Strobilanthes viscosa* (rho 0.452, p=0.018). These are associations within the study design, not proof that fruit availability alone causes feeding or that every interaction produces effective dispersal.

The paper’s discussion also identifies an important ecological caution: *Passiflora tripartita* was introduced and described by the authors as invasive in the Horton Plains context, even though the bird fed on it. A plant being a food source for a threatened bird does not automatically make it an appropriate cultivation or restoration species. This is a useful example of why the atlas must retain relationships, origin, ecological risk, and management context together.
