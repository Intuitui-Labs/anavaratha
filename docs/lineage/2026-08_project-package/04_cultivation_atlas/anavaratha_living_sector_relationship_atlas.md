# Anavaratha

## Living-sector relationship atlas

**Sri Lanka regenerative-cultivation dimension — Atlas specification, Version 0.1**

## 1. Purpose

The Anavaratha living-sector atlas is a structured way to represent the relationships among landscapes, habitats, organisms, ecological functions, biocultural meanings, and stewardship. It is not intended to become a static encyclopaedia. It should remain revisable, source-linked, spatially aware, seasonal, and explicit about uncertainty.

The atlas should answer four connected questions:

> **Who lives here? What relationships connect them? What conditions sustain those relationships? What forms of care are proportionate?**

## 2. Atlas architecture

| Layer | Unit | Main output |
|---|---|---|
| **Place** | Ecological region, watershed, landscape, settlement, or site | Place profile |
| **Habitat** | Forest, wetland, farm, garden, coast, river, tank, grassland, or urban patch | Habitat profile |
| **Living being** | Tree, plant, animal, bird, insect, fungus, microorganism, or other organism | Organism record |
| **Relationship** | Pollination, dispersal, shade, soil, water, predation, medicine, food, culture, or care | Relationship record |
| **Condition** | Soil, water, season, shade, food, shelter, space, disturbance, access, or stewardship | Condition record |
| **Evidence** | Source, observation, dataset, testimony, image, map, or study | Evidence record |
| **Change** | Recovery, decline, succession, seasonal shift, intervention, or conflict | Change record |

## 3. Core record types

### 3.1 Place profile

A place profile should identify the ecological region, watershed, elevation, climate, soil, hydrology, land use, habitats, communities, institutions, history, and known biodiversity. It should record the boundaries used and explain where they may not correspond to ecological or social realities.

### 3.2 Habitat profile

A habitat profile should describe structure, dominant and associated beings, water and soil conditions, seasonality, disturbance, connectivity, human use, ecological function, existing regeneration, and stewardship. It should record whether the habitat is protected, managed, degraded, recovering, contested, or uncertain.

### 3.3 Organism record

Each organism record should include identity, names, taxonomic confidence, habitat, range, status, life requirements, ecological roles, associated beings, human relationships, threats or constraints, regenerative possibilities, evidence, and uncertainty.

### 3.4 Relationship record

A relationship record should identify the beings or systems involved, the relationship type, the place and season in which it occurs, the conditions required, the evidence supporting it, the consequences of change, and possible stewardship. Relationships should be represented in both directions where appropriate.

### 3.5 Condition record

A condition record should describe what allows a being or relationship to continue. It may concern shade, water, soil, flowers, fruit, nesting, breeding, movement, temperature, protection, access, knowledge, or maintenance. Conditions should be expressed as observable or investigable features rather than vague aspirations.

## 4. Relationship vocabulary

| Relationship type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Provides habitat** | One being or feature creates shelter, nesting, breeding, feeding, or refuge conditions |
| **Pollinates** | One organism contributes to the reproduction of another through pollination |
| **Disperses seeds** | One organism moves or enables the movement of seeds or propagules |
| **Provides food** | One being supplies food or nutritional support to another |
| **Provides shade** | Vegetation alters light, temperature, moisture, or exposure |
| **Supports water** | A being or habitat affects infiltration, retention, flow, filtration, or availability |
| **Builds soil** | Organisms contribute to structure, organic matter, decomposition, nutrients, or root relations |
| **Preys upon** | One organism feeds on another within a food web |
| **Competes with** | Organisms share limiting resources or conditions |
| **Hosts or shelters** | One organism provides a physical or biological home for another |
| **Signals condition** | Presence or change indicates an ecological condition or transition |
| **Used as food or medicine** | A human community relates to a being through nutrition or health practice |
| **Carries memory or meaning** | A being is associated with history, culture, ritual, language, or identity |
| **Stewarded by** | A person, community, or institution observes, cares for, propagates, protects, or transmits knowledge |
| **Conflicts with** | A relationship creates tension around access, livelihood, safety, conservation, or human–wildlife coexistence |

## 5. Seasonal dimension

The atlas should not represent life as static. Each relationship should record relevant seasonal patterns, including flowering, fruiting, seed release, breeding, migration, dormancy, rainfall, drought, flooding, harvest, and stewardship periods.

A seasonal calendar may reveal that a planting provides food for pollinators only during one period, that a species depends on water that is available only seasonally, or that a restoration intervention coincides with a vulnerable breeding or nesting period.

## 6. Evidence and uncertainty

Every record should include source, date, spatial coverage, method, confidence, and unresolved questions. Community testimony, scientific studies, government data, field observation, remote sensing, and historical material may all be valuable, but their evidentiary roles should be distinguished.

| Confidence | Use |
|---|---|
| **High** | Multiple credible sources or strong place-specific evidence converge |
| **Moderate** | Credible evidence exists but is limited by date, scale, method, or coverage |
| **Low** | A claim is plausible or locally reported but needs verification |
| **Unknown** | Evidence is insufficient to make a responsible claim |

Uncertainty is not a flaw in the atlas. It is information about where attention, research, or humility is needed.

## 7. Relationship-network views

The same underlying records should support several views:

| View | What it reveals |
|---|---|
| **Organism–habitat view** | Where organisms live and what conditions they require |
| **Pollination view** | Flowering plants, pollinators, seasonality, and continuity |
| **Seed-and-fruit view** | Fruiting plants, dispersers, movement, and recruitment |
| **Shade-and-water view** | Vegetation, microclimate, soil moisture, and hydrological relations |
| **Soil-life view** | Roots, fungi, microorganisms, decomposers, and soil conditions |
| **Medicinal-and-food view** | Plants, nutrition, health practices, knowledge, and propagation |
| **Endemic-and-threatened view** | Priority beings, habitats, corridors, and recovery conditions |
| **Biocultural view** | Names, stories, history, ritual, livelihoods, and stewardship |
| **Care-and-continuity view** | Who maintains which relationship, with what resources and authority |

## 8. From atlas to cultivation hypothesis

The atlas should not directly generate a planting list. It should generate a cultivation hypothesis with five parts:

1. **Living system:** the place, habitat, organisms, and relationships involved.
2. **Regenerative condition:** the condition that appears necessary for continuity.
3. **Constraint:** the ecological, social, institutional, temporal, or spatial factor limiting that condition.
4. **Proportionate care:** protection, observation, natural recovery, assisted regeneration, propagation, habitat creation, or another action.
5. **Learning design:** what will be observed, by whom, for how long, and how the hypothesis may be revised.

## 9. Ethical safeguards

The atlas should avoid exposing the exact locations of vulnerable species where disclosure could increase collection, disturbance, or exploitation. Medicinal knowledge should be recorded with consent and appropriate protection. Community knowledge should be attributed and reciprocated. Human–wildlife conflict should be represented without blaming either communities or animals. Restoration benefits and burdens should be visible together.

The atlas should also avoid turning living beings into commodities, carbon units, tourist attractions, or project symbols without acknowledging their wider ecological and cultural relationships.

## 10. Minimum viable atlas

The first version does not need to catalogue all Sri Lankan life. A minimum viable atlas could contain:

| Component | Initial target |
|---|---|
| **Place profiles** | A small set of contrasting ecological contexts |
| **Habitat profiles** | Forest, wetland/coast, agroforestry/homegarden, and urban/peri-urban examples |
| **Organism clusters** | Structural trees, pollinators, seed dispersers, soil life, medicinal/food plants, and threatened or endemic beings |
| **Relationship records** | At least one documented relationship for each selected organism cluster |
| **Seasonal records** | Flowering, fruiting, breeding, rainfall, and care periods where evidence exists |
| **Stewardship records** | Existing knowledge holders, caretakers, institutions, and responsibilities |
| **Uncertainty records** | Data gaps and claims requiring verification |

## 11. Working definition

> **The Anavaratha living-sector atlas is a revisable, evidence-linked map of the beings, relationships, conditions, and forms of care through which Sri Lankan landscapes remain alive and capable of renewal.**
