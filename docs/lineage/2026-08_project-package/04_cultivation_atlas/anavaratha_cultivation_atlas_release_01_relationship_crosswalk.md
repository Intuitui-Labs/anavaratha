## Cultivation Atlas Release 01

**Relationship and seasonality crosswalk — Version 0.1**

## 1. Purpose

The atlas becomes useful when a source-linked organism record can be connected to the conditions around it. This crosswalk therefore distinguishes **documented relationships**, **qualified interpretive links**, and **pending links**. A checklist appearance is never treated as evidence of habitat, cultivation suitability, medicinal efficacy, or ecological function.

## 3. Documented seasonal relationship

The most developed relationship in Release 01 is the Yellow-eared Bulbul–fruiting-plant community at Horton Plains National Park. The peer-reviewed study ran from September 2015 to November 2017 and combined focal-animal sampling, faecal analysis, field-guide identification, monthly observations of tagged plants, and ripe-fruit-cover estimation [2].

| Seasonal frame used in study | Months | Recorded relationship |
|---|---|---|
| **First inter-monsoon** | March–April | *Solanum mauritianum* had the highest reported ripe-fruit cover among the named seasonal maxima; feeding patterns varied across plants |
| **Southwest monsoon** | May–September | *Berberis ceylanica* had the highest reported ripe-fruit cover; *S. mauritianum* was a major feeding plant in the feeding-attempt summary |
| **Second inter-monsoon** | October–November | *Solanum mauritianum* had the highest reported ripe-fruit cover among the named seasonal maxima; *Rubus ellipticus* was highly consumed in the study summary |
| **Northeast monsoon** | December–February | *Solanum mauritianum* had the highest reported ripe-fruit cover; *Rubus ellipticus* was highly consumed in the study summary |

The study reports significant associations between feeding attempts and ripe-fruit cover for *Symplocos bractealis*, *Solanum mauritianum*, and *Strobilanthes viscosa*. It also reports significant associations between feeding attempts and the number of trees in fruiting for *Elaeocarpus coreaceus*, *Passiflora tripartita*, *Eugenia mabaeoides*, and *Strobilanthes viscosa*. These are study-specific associations, not universal ecological laws.

The study also records feeding on introduced *Passiflora tripartita*. This is a critical atlas lesson: a threatened bird’s use of a plant does not automatically make that plant suitable for restoration or cultivation. Origin, invasion risk, habitat effects, and management purpose must remain visible in the same record.

## 4. What the crosswalk permits

The crosswalk permits the following limited interpretations:

| Permitted | Not permitted |
|---|---|
| A named bird feeds on documented fruiting plants in a defined study area and period | The bird requires every listed plant everywhere in Sri Lanka |
| Seeds of nine plant species were identified in faecal samples | Every seed was effectively dispersed or recruited |
| Fruiting cover and feeding associations vary by plant and monsoon season | A static planting list can reproduce the observed ecological relationship |
| Some checklist rows have visually verified names, local names, and dated status fields | Checklist inclusion proves medicinal efficacy, safety, or cultivation suitability |
| Institutions provide distinct source, governance, and access pathways | Institutional pages can be merged into one unrestricted database |

## 5. Future relationship questions

The next ecological links should be selected only when evidence can support the full chain:

> **organism → relationship → condition → season → place → stewardship → measurable outcome**

For a plant–frugivore record, this means distinguishing fruit consumption from seed movement, seed movement from deposition, and deposition from recruitment. For a medicinal-garden record, it means distinguishing a plant list from a living cultivation system that includes soil, water, propagation, knowledge, labour, access, and care.
