# Anavaratha

## Species-level relationship atlas

**Sri Lanka regenerative-cultivation dimension — Preliminary synthesis, Version 0.1**

> **The atlas does not ask which beings are useful to Anavaratha. It asks which relationships must remain possible for living systems to continue.**

## 1. Scope

This first species-level atlas integrates four research clusters: structural trees and shade–habitat relations; pollinator and seed-dispersal relations; soil-life, medicinal, and food-plant relations; and endemic or endangered beings with habitat context.

It is not a complete species inventory and it is not a planting recommendation. Several records remain at the “documented” or “contextualised” stage. The purpose is to establish a disciplined pathway from evidence to relationship to future cultivation hypothesis.

## 2. Atlas status scale

| Status | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Lead** | A possible organism or relationship worth investigating |
| **Documented** | Identity or relationship is supported by a credible source |
| **Contextualised** | Habitat, function, season, human relationship, or conservation context is partly described |
| **Connected** | Associated beings, conditions, stewardship, and landscape relationships are mapped |
| **Actionable** | A proportionate care hypothesis can be stated responsibly |

The records below are mainly **documented** or **contextualised**. None should yet be treated as an actionable field recommendation.

## 3. Relationship records

### LSA-01 — Structural tree: Kumbuk (*Terminalia arjuna*)

| Field | Record |
|---|---|
| **Cluster** | Structural and shade-bearing trees |
| **Identity** | Kumbuk; *Terminalia arjuna*; Marutu in Tamil, as recorded by Dilmah Conservation [1] |
| **Working habitat** | Riparian, river-edge, tank-edge, or water-linked context; Sri Lankan habitat relationship requires further verification |
| **Potential functions** | Shade, canopy, soil and bank relation, water-edge habitat, food and shelter for associated beings |
| **Associated beings** | Riparian plants, insects, birds, fish, soil organisms, and communities; species-specific associations remain open |
| **Biocultural relation** | Present in a Sri Lankan flora and agricultural-heritage collection [1] |
| **Status** | Documented identity; contextual ecological functions remain to be verified |
| **Regenerative question** | Under what conditions does Kumbuk support water-edge habitat, shade, soil, and associated life? |
| **Care hypothesis** | First protect and study existing riparian relationships; consider propagation only after ecological fit and stewardship are established |

### LSA-03 — Flowering plant and pollinator network

| Field | Record |
|---|---|
| **Cluster** | Pollinators and flowering plants |
| **Identity** | Flowering plant–bee, butterfly, moth, fly, beetle, bird, or bat relationship; exact pairs to be selected |
| **Working habitat** | Homegardens, urban gardens, forests, farms, and other flowering habitats |
| **Potential functions** | Pollination, food, seasonal continuity, ecological indication, and plant reproduction |
| **Sri Lankan evidence** | A University of Jaffna homegarden study assessed plant–pollinator relations and reported high host-per-pollinator and pollinators-per-host values for bees and mango [2]. A separate dry-zone study examined flower visitors and visiting frequencies [3]. |
| **Conditions** | Flowering calendar, plant diversity, nesting, shelter, water, seasonality, and low harmful disturbance |
| **Status** | Documented relationship class; species-specific network requires further research |
| **Regenerative question** | Which flowering sequences and habitat conditions sustain pollinators and effective plant reproduction? |
| **Care hypothesis** | Cultivate seasonal habitat continuity, not simply attractive flowers or generalised “pollinator-friendly” planting |

### LSA-04 — Fruiting plant and seed-disperser network

| Field | Record |
|---|---|
| **Cluster** | Fruiting plants and seed dispersers |
| **Identity** | Fruiting plant–bird, bat, mammal, or other disperser relationship; exact pair to be selected |
| **Working habitat** | Rainforest, homegarden, agroforestry, wetland edge, and connected habitat |
| **Potential functions** | Food, movement, seed transfer, recruitment, and forest recovery |
| **Sri Lankan evidence** | Rainforest-restoration research in southwest Sri Lanka treats animal-mediated seed dispersal, advance regeneration, canopy, habitat diversity, and site-specific restoration pathways as connected processes [4]. |
| **Conditions** | Fruiting season, disperser movement, shelter, viable seed, suitable deposition, germination, and habitat continuity |
| **Status** | Documented relationship class; species-specific evidence remains open |
| **Regenerative question** | Does supporting a fruiting plant and its dispersers increase natural recruitment in the relevant landscape? |
| **Care hypothesis** | Protect fruiting and movement relationships before attempting to add species or manipulate dispersal |

### LSA-05 — Root–fungus and soil-life relationship

| Field | Record |
|---|---|
| **Cluster** | Soil organisms and roots |
| **Identity** | Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi–rice root relationship, with wider soil community to be studied |
| **Working habitat** | Rice rhizosphere and, by extension of research, homegardens, forest gardens, and restoration soils |
| **Potential functions** | Root association, plant development, soil biology, nutrient relations, and soil-system continuity |
| **Sri Lankan evidence** | A Sri Lankan study found that fungal colonisation depended on rice growth stage and interacted with fertilizer application and soil condition; colonisation increased over the sampling period [5]. |
| **Conditions** | Host stage, soil community, soil type, moisture, management, organic matter, and disturbance |
| **Status** | Documented relationship in a rice study; wider ecosystem implications require care |
| **Regenerative question** | How can cultivation support soil biological relationships rather than replace them with uniform inputs? |
| **Care hypothesis** | Observe and protect soil conditions first; test interventions only with appropriate ecological and agricultural evidence |

### LSA-06 — Medicinal-plant and knowledge relation

| Field | Record |
|---|---|
| **Cluster** | Medicinal and food plants |
| **Identity** | Sri Lankan medicinal-plant group; exact species set to be verified |
| **Working habitat** | Medicinal gardens, homegardens, forests, cultivated landscapes, and community spaces |
| **Potential functions** | Health relation, cultural memory, food, livelihood, seed continuity, biodiversity, and education |
| **Sri Lankan evidence** | An AGRIS/FAO record for a World Bank publication documents long-standing medicinal-plant use in Sri Lanka and notes medicinal, cultural, and religious relationships [6]. A Sri Lankan community-conservation source identifies village and school medicinal-plant demonstration plots described as Osu Uyana [7]. |
| **Conditions** | Correct identity, propagation, sustainable harvest, knowledge holder, consent, access, and conservation |
| **Status** | Documented biocultural category; Osu Uyana context and species list require verification |
| **Regenerative question** | How can medicinal-plant cultivation conserve biodiversity and knowledge without increasing extraction or making unsafe claims? |
| **Care hypothesis** | Begin with knowledge, conservation, propagation, and ethical protocols rather than commercial or therapeutic claims |

### LSA-07 — Food plant and homegarden network

| Field | Record |
|---|---|
| **Cluster** | Food plants and living nutrition |
| **Identity** | Food plants, fruit trees, vegetables, seeds, tubers, spices, and associated beings; exact set to be selected |
| **Working habitat** | Homegardens, agroforestry, farms, and peri-urban cultivation |
| **Potential functions** | Nutrition, household resilience, shade, biodiversity, pollination, seed, soil, livelihood, and culture |
| **Sri Lankan evidence** | The University of Jaffna study recorded diverse flora, fruit production, medicinal plants, shade, soil and water relations, and ecosystem-service dimensions in Jaffna homegardens [2]. World Agroforestry documents Kandyan homegardens as long-standing cultivated landscapes associated with perennial tree and fruit-tree diversity [8]. |
| **Conditions** | Soil, water, seasonality, seed continuity, labour, access, food preference, and ecological fit |
| **Status** | Contextualised systems record; species-specific relationship network remains open |
| **Regenerative question** | How can food cultivation increase ecological and nutritional diversity together? |
| **Care hypothesis** | Study and strengthen existing food and seed relationships before introducing new species or designs |

### LSA-08 — Red Fin Labeo (*Labeo lankae*) and freshwater-basin habitat

| Field | Record |
|---|---|
| **Cluster** | Endemic and endangered beings |
| **Identity** | Red Fin Labeo; *Labeo lankae* |
| **Working habitat** | Malwathu Oya River Basin in Sri Lanka’s North Central dry zone |
| **Status** | Endemic to Sri Lanka; the Sri Lanka Biodiversity Clearing-House/IUCN project page identifies it as Critically Endangered because of limited distribution [9] |
| **Potential functions** | Freshwater food-web, basin, movement, habitat, and conservation relationships |
| **Evidence gap** | Population, habitat, distribution, threats, and climate-change effects require detailed investigation [9] |
| **Regenerative question** | What water, habitat, food-web, movement, and institutional conditions allow the species and basin to persist? |
| **Care hypothesis** | Habitat survey, recovery planning, monitoring, community awareness, and basin responsibility; not direct propagation by default |

This record demonstrates that the living sector includes aquatic animals and basin relationships. The appropriate form of care may be habitat protection, monitoring, or governance rather than cultivation of the organism itself.

## 5. What the atlas currently says

The first species-level atlas supports five provisional conclusions.

First, trees should be studied as structural participants in living communities, not as isolated carbon or shade units. Second, attraction is not enough: pollination and seed dispersal require seasonal, habitat, and reproductive continuity. Third, soil life changes the meaning of cultivation by placing roots, fungi, microorganisms, and decomposition beneath visible outcomes. Fourth, medicinal and food plants connect biodiversity with health, nutrition, knowledge, culture, and household life. Fifth, endangered beings make habitat responsibility visible and show that conservation may require basin, landscape, institutional, and community work rather than direct multiplication of individuals.

## 6. Current research gaps

The atlas still needs verified species-level records for flowering plants and their Sri Lankan pollinators, fruiting plants and dispersers, structural-tree candidates across ecological regions, soil organisms beyond the rice rhizosphere, medicinal-plant species and Osu Uyana contexts, and additional endemic or endangered organisms from forest, wetland, coastal, agricultural, and urban systems.

It also needs seasonal calendars, local names in Sinhala and Tamil, georeferenced habitat information where ethically appropriate, community and knowledge-holder protocols, and stronger evidence on what kinds of cultivation actually improve continuity rather than only visible coverage.

## 7. Working principle

> **The next action is not to plant the most interesting species. It is to deepen the most revealing relationship.**
