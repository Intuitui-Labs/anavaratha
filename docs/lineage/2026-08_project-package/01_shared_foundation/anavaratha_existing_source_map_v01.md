# Anavaratha

## Existing-source map for regenerative cultivation in Sri Lanka

**Living-sector research — Working source map, Version 0.1**

> **Before creating a new catalogue, locate the knowledge, records, institutions, and communities that already hold the relevant information.**

## 1. Purpose

This source map identifies existing Sri Lankan and international resources that can support Anavaratha’s regenerative-cultivation research. It is designed to guide discovery, linking, verification, synthesis, and ethical reuse. It is not a complete directory, and the access and currency of each source should be checked again before use.

The present focus is the living sector: landscapes, habitats, trees, plants, animals, insects, soil life, wetlands, medicinal and food plants, conservation, and stewardship. Central Environmental Authority material is included where it provides access to wetlands, Red List, environmental information, or institutional context. Pollution-specific CEA material is marked as outside the present workstream.

## 3. What should be linked rather than recreated

| Anavaratha need | Existing source path | Proposed action |
|---|---|---|
| Plant identity and threatened flora | National Herbarium, National Red List, updated medicinal checklist | Link, cite, and request verification where necessary |
| Forest and watershed institutional geography | Forest Department divisions, regions, and technical units | Use as an institutional index and contact map |
| Protected areas and wildlife habitat | DWC protected-area network and permits | Link to official categories, boundaries, and research rules |
| Wetland records | CEA gateways and National Wetland Directory | Assess date, location precision, and update history before reuse |
| Endangered species ranges | IUCN Red List spatial data and Sri Lankan project records | Cross-check global layers with national and local evidence |
| Medicinal plants | 2025 updated checklist, National Herbarium, Ayurveda and practitioner sources | Use the checklist as a baseline and protect knowledge and sensitive locations |
| Homegarden and agroforestry relationships | University of Jaffna and World Agroforestry studies | Compare ecological zones and household systems rather than generalise one case |
| Soil and root relations | Sri Lankan rhizosphere and soil studies | Extract methods, conditions, growth stages, and limitations |
| Ecological zones and spatial context | FAO GAEZ, CBD, forest and watershed sources | Use three-zone layer only as orientation and add finer data |
| Restoration methods | UN Decade, IWMI, IUCN, peer-reviewed studies | Compare natural recovery, assisted regeneration, planting, and stewardship |

## 4. Authority and resolution notes

The strongest source is not always the most useful source. An official source may have high institutional authority but limited local detail. A peer-reviewed study may have strong methods but cover only one site. Community knowledge may have deep temporal and place-based resolution but require consent, attribution, and protection.

| Source type | Strength | Limitation to record |
|---|---|---|
| **Government institution** | Mandate, legal responsibility, official records, administrative coverage | Public page may not expose underlying data; updates can be irregular |
| **National catalogue** | Broad inventory or baseline | Taxonomy, location, and date may require updating |
| **Peer-reviewed study** | Explicit method and scholarly review | Often limited to one habitat, sample, or period |
| **University repository** | Local studies and hard-to-find evidence | Metadata and file access may be inconsistent |
| **International dataset** | Standardised spatial or status fields | May be coarse, incomplete, or not locally validated |
| **Community knowledge** | Long-term place-based observation and cultural meaning | Requires consent, attribution, reciprocity, and protection from extraction |
| **Project case study** | Practical lessons and implementation context | May present successes more clearly than failures or unintended effects |

## 5. Source-use workflow

Anavaratha should use the following sequence for every new research question:

1. Search the source map by domain, geography, scale, and living-sector relationship.
2. Open the original source rather than relying on a secondary summary.
3. Record title, holder, date, scope, method, access, licence, and limitations.
4. Compare at least one relevant source from another institution or method.
5. Extract only claims that the source actually supports.
6. Link the claim to the relevant atlas record and preserve uncertainty.
7. Contact the source holder or knowledge holder when access, interpretation, consent, or reuse is unclear.
8. Create a new record only when an identified gap or synthesis need justifies it.

## 6. High-value gaps

The source map already reveals likely gaps that may justify future synthesis rather than immediate primary data collection. These include cross-linking organism records to habitat and seasonal relationships; connecting medicinal-plant lists to conservation status and ethical knowledge protocols; comparing ecological zones and local studies; harmonising Sinhala, Tamil, English, and scientific names; tracking natural regeneration and recruitment; and making existing restoration lessons findable across institutions.

The most valuable new product may therefore be a **linked, source-aware relationship layer** rather than another independent catalogue.

## 7. Working principle

> **Anavaratha should become a careful connector of existing knowledge before it becomes a producer of new intervention data.**
