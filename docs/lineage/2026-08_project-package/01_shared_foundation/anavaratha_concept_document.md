# Anavaratha

## A Sri Lankan concept of ecological release, regeneration, and continuity

**Working concept document — Version 0.3**

> **Anavaratha creates the conditions for life to continue. It clears what suffocates living systems, restores what has been damaged, and cultivates the capacity of people and ecosystems to regenerate.**

## 1. The project in brief

Anavaratha is conceived as a Sri Lankan ecological initiative that joins two necessary movements. The first is the removal of conditions that restrict or damage life: unmanaged waste, pollution, contamination, ecological neglect, and the inefficient use of material resources. The second is the active cultivation of conditions in which life can flourish: healthy soil, clean water, trees, biodiversity, habitat, ecological knowledge, and community stewardship.

Sri Lanka is not merely a location in which a universal model will be applied. It is the living context through which the project must learn: an island of distinct ecological zones, watersheds, settlements, livelihoods, institutions, histories, and uneven environmental burdens. Anavaratha therefore needs both practical interventions and rigorous landscape sense-making.

The project does not treat waste management and ecological restoration as separate concerns. Waste management can clear the field, while ecological preservation and regenerative development can make that field capable of sustaining life. Yet neither side must always come first. A waste intervention may require extensive desk research before action, while cultivation may begin simply and locally as an early leverage point. Anavaratha exists in the relationship between these different entry points.

Its central proposition is simple:

> **Freedom from ecological harm should become freedom for ecological flourishing.**

## 2. The name and its working resonance

The exact Sanskrit form **Anavaratha** (अनवरथ) is attested in dictionary sources primarily as a proper name associated with a figure in the *Vishnu Purana*, not as an abstract term meaning ecological continuity [1] [2]. A closely related form, **Anavarata** (अनवरत), means incessant, continuous, uninterrupted, ceaseless, or eternal [3].

For the project, this related sense may provide a valuable poetic resonance: ecological work is not a single intervention or a finished object, but an ongoing practice of clearing, restoring, cultivating, and caring. The project should be transparent about this distinction. Anavaratha is not claiming that its name literally translates as “regeneration” or “freedom.” Rather, it gives the name a deliberate conceptual association with **continuity, persistence, and the unbroken passage of care**.

## 3. The two-sided ecological framework

Anavaratha has two sides, but they should not be understood as two isolated programmes or as a fixed sequence. They are complementary orientations and possible entry points within one ecological process. Waste analysis may begin with conceptual desk research, systems mapping, and rigorous sense-making. Cultivation may begin with a modest act—planting, soil care, seed work, or restoring a small site—while the broader landscape is still being studied.

| Side | Ecological concern | Movement | Intended result |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Clearing** | Waste, pollution, contamination, obstruction, and avoidable ecological harm | Removing what interferes with life | Space, safety, cleanliness, and renewed possibility |
| **Cultivating** | Trees, soil, water, biodiversity, habitat, knowledge, and stewardship | Building the conditions that allow life to flourish | Regeneration, resilience, participation, and continuity |

The first side is not merely negative in the sense of being passive or destructive. It is a form of ecological liberation through removal. Waste can occupy land, contaminate water, degrade soil, damage habitats, and limit the possibilities available to people and other living beings. To reduce and responsibly manage waste is therefore to remove barriers to life.

The second side is not merely decorative or symbolic. Planting trees matters only when it belongs to a larger living system of soil, water, species diversity, maintenance, and community care. Ecological development should therefore mean **regenerative development**: increasing the capacity of a place to support life over time rather than simply adding visible infrastructure or greenery.

The relationship between the two sides can be represented as a cycle:

> **Clear ↔ Restore ↔ Cultivate ↔ Enable ↔ Sustain**

The arrows are deliberately reversible. A project may enter through waste, planting, soil, water, education, or community organisation. What matters is that each entry point eventually connects to the others through evidence, feedback, and care. The process does not end with cleanliness or with a planting event; it moves toward the creation of ecological and social capacity.

## 4. Berlin as a bounded analogy

Isaiah Berlin’s distinction between negative and positive liberty provides a useful formal analogy for the project. In the philosophical distinction, negative liberty refers to the absence of obstacles, barriers, or interference, while positive liberty refers to the possibility of acting, directing one’s life, and realizing one’s purposes [4].

For Anavaratha, the analogy can be expressed as follows:

| Berlinian language | Anavaratha’s ecological translation |
|---|---|
| Freedom **from** interference | Freedom from waste, pollution, contamination, and ecological degradation |
| Freedom **to** act and realize purposes | Freedom for ecosystems and communities to grow, regenerate, participate, and care |

This is strictly an analogy of **shape and direction**, not an attempt to reproduce Berlin’s political theory. Berlin’s two forms of liberty have been interpreted as distinct and sometimes rival conceptions of a political ideal [4]. Anavaratha should not claim that its two sides are naturally harmonious or that ecological regeneration resolves that philosophical debate.

The project’s own synthesis is more specific: **the removal of ecological obstacles should open the possibility of regeneration, while regeneration gives purpose and duration to the work of removal**. The two sides form a continuous movement rather than a moral opposition between a negative and a positive half.

## 5. Inspiration constellation

Anavaratha draws from several kinds of inspiration, each of which contributes something different to the project’s identity.

### 5.1 _The Man Who Planted Trees_

The likely 1987 reference is Frédéric Back’s animated film **_The Man Who Planted Trees_**, based on Jean Giono’s story [5]. The film’s significance for Anavaratha lies in its emphasis on patient, persistent, largely unseen action. Its ecological imagination is built on the idea that repeated care, carried out over a long period, can transform an entire landscape.

This offers Anavaratha an ethic of **slow commitment**. The project should value continuity over spectacle, maintenance over announcement, and cumulative change over the expectation of immediate transformation.

### 5.2 Jadav Payeng and the Molai Forest

Jadav “Molai” Payeng, known as the “Forest Man of India,” offers a real-world parallel to the patient restoration ethic represented by *The Man Who Planted Trees*. A University College London case study describes how Payeng began planting in response to flood-related reptile deaths near the Brahmaputra and how the work developed into the Molai Kathoni forest on Majuli, Assam [11].

Payeng’s story and the 1987 film are **not the same story**. The film is a Canadian animated adaptation of Jean Giono’s literary story, while Payeng is a real Indian forest-restoration figure whose work unfolded over decades. Their relationship to Anavaratha is therefore one of parallel inspiration: both express persistence, care, and the possibility that repeated local action can transform a landscape.

Payeng’s example also introduces an essential caution. The UCL case study notes that the restored habitat brought ecological benefits but also created conflicts and economic burdens for nearby residents through animal movement and damage to local activities [11]. Anavaratha must therefore connect ecological restoration with social justice, local communication, land use, livelihoods, and community participation. A forest should not be judged only by its size or biodiversity; it must also be understood through the people and other species living around it.

### 5.3 Biomimicry

Biomimicry contributes a method rather than simply an aesthetic. The Biomimicry Institute describes the living world as a source of models from which humans can learn, including forms, processes, and ecosystems that have been tested through time [6].

For Anavaratha, this means asking ecological questions before imposing technical answers. How does a forest retain water? How does a healthy soil community build fertility? How does diversity create resilience? How do living systems use waste as a resource for another process? Biomimicry should guide the project toward cycles, interdependence, adaptation, feedback, and cooperation.

### 5.4 Vandana Shiva and Navdanya

Vandana Shiva’s work, especially through Navdanya, contributes an ethical and political dimension. Navdanya describes itself as an Earth-centric, women-centric, farmer-led movement concerned with biological and cultural diversity, seed sovereignty, living soil, agroecology, and Earth Democracy [7].

This influence helps Anavaratha avoid reducing ecology to landscaping or technical efficiency. It directs attention toward who controls seeds, land, knowledge, and food systems; toward the importance of local and traditional knowledge; and toward the relationship between ecological regeneration and community autonomy. It also reinforces the idea that biodiversity is not merely a collection of species but a living inheritance, a commons, and a condition of continuity.

### 5.5 Ecological restoration projects

Projects around the world provide practical precedents for the idea that damaged environments can be restored through organised, long-term work. Anavaratha should study such projects carefully, not to copy them mechanically but to understand their methods, institutional arrangements, ecological limits, and forms of local participation.

### 5.6 Ecosia and distributed ecological leverage

Ecosia contributes a different but complementary model. Its official Help Center explains that the search engine earns income from advertising and gives 100% of its profits from that revenue to climate action, with 80% used to finance planting and protecting trees in more than 30 countries [12].

The importance of Ecosia for Anavaratha is not that the project should reproduce a search-engine business model. Its contribution is the idea that an ordinary, repeated activity can be connected transparently to ecological action at a larger scale. This offers Anavaratha a design question: how can simple and accessible actions by many people create resources, knowledge, participation, or care for long-term ecological work in Sri Lanka?

Ecosia therefore complements the slower, place-based examples of the film and Payeng. Those examples emphasise sustained attention to a landscape; Ecosia emphasises distributed participation and ecological leverage. Anavaratha can hold both together, provided that any claim of impact is transparent and that local restoration is not reduced to an abstract number.

### 5.7 _Adaptation._ (2002)

Charlie Kaufman’s film **_Adaptation._ (2002)**, directed by Spike Jonze and beginning from Susan Orlean’s book _The Orchid Thief_, contributes a different kind of influence. It is not a scientific source about orchids or ecology. It is a methodological and artistic reference concerning the difficulty of representing living complexity through a constructed form.

For Anavaratha, the relevance of _Adaptation._ is the tension between the desire to classify a living subject and the fact that the subject always exceeds the classification. A medicinal plant, structural tree, pollinator, endangered fish, or soil organism should not be reduced to a single label such as useful, rare, native, medicinal, or ecosystem-friendly. Each must remain connected to habitat, season, other beings, people, knowledge, and uncertainty.

The film therefore introduces **representational humility**. Anavaratha’s atlas is not a final possession of knowledge or a total map of Sri Lanka. It is a provisional instrument that selects, connects, and makes visible. Because it shapes attention, it must also record its own assumptions, selection criteria, source authority, uncertainty, revisions, and omissions.

This gives the atlas an adaptive quality in four senses: biological, because living systems change; epistemic, because records improve or conflict; institutional, because responsibilities and permissions change; and formal, because the structure of the atlas must change when the relationships demand it. The observer and the project are not outside the living world they describe.

> **The map is not the living world, but the map changes the conditions under which the living world is seen.**

The influence of _Adaptation._ is therefore not a call to abandon structure. It is a call to make structure provisional, transparent, revisable, and accountable to what it cannot contain.

The project’s influence constellation can therefore be summarised as follows:

| Influence | Contribution to Anavaratha |
|---|---|
| *The Man Who Planted Trees* | An ethic of patience, persistence, and cumulative care |
| Jadav Payeng and the Molai Forest | A real-world example of long-term restoration, with a caution about local social impacts |
| Biomimicry | A method of learning from living systems |
| Vandana Shiva and Navdanya | An ethic of biodiversity, commons, seed freedom, and ecological sovereignty |
| Ecosia | A model of distributed everyday action linked to ecological finance |
| _Adaptation._ (2002) | Representational humility, adaptive categories, and awareness that living systems exceed their descriptions |
| Restoration precedents | Practical evidence, tested approaches, and lessons in implementation |

## 6. Core principles

### Principle 1: Clear before building

Anavaratha begins by identifying what is obstructing life. This may include unmanaged waste, toxic materials, degraded soil, polluted water, invasive practices, or systems that produce unnecessary waste. The project should not rush to add new interventions before understanding what must first be removed, reduced, or transformed.

### Principle 2: Restore conditions, not appearances

A green surface is not necessarily a healthy ecosystem. Tree planting, public space, and visible cleanliness should be evaluated in relation to soil, water, species diversity, maintenance, and long-term survival. Anavaratha should seek living function rather than symbolic greenness.

### Principle 3: Work with living systems

The project should learn from ecological processes instead of treating nature as passive material. Local climate, native species, seasonal rhythms, water movement, soil conditions, and existing forms of life should shape the intervention.

### Principle 4: Prefer diversity and locality

Resilient ecological systems tend to depend on relationships and diversity rather than uniformity. Anavaratha should favour locally appropriate species, multiple forms of knowledge, decentralised participation, and solutions that fit the place in which they operate.

### Principle 5: Convert capacity into continuity

The aim is not only to complete projects but to enable their continuation. Maintenance, training, seed saving, stewardship, monitoring, and community ownership should be treated as part of the ecological work itself.

### Principle 6: Measure living outcomes

Success should not be measured only through tonnes of waste collected or numbers of trees planted. It should also be understood through survival, soil improvement, water retention, biodiversity, shade, habitat, reduced waste generation, community participation, and the ability of the system to continue without permanent external control.

### Principle 7: Keep the map adaptive

Anavaratha should treat every catalogue, category, and relationship map as provisional. Records should carry source provenance, version dates, uncertainty, revision histories, and the reason for inclusion. A relationship should be marked as documented, probable, proposed, disputed, or unknown. This is not a weakness in the atlas; it is a way of preventing the representation from becoming more authoritative than the living evidence.

The project should also ask how its own choices shape attention. What becomes visible, valued, protected, cultivated, or ignored because Anavaratha has named and connected it? The atlas must remain a tool for relationship and learning, not a way to possess or close down the living world.

## 7. Two entry points and one shared intelligence

Anavaratha should recognise a difference in the character of its two entry points. The waste side often begins through conceptual desk research: tracing material flows, identifying institutional responsibilities, understanding collection and disposal systems, and making sense of pollution across scales. Its first task may be to understand before intervening.

The cultivation side may begin more simply and earlier. A small planting, seed exchange, soil intervention, compost system, or restoration experiment can create leverage, demonstrate possibility, and build relationships while the wider research continues. Simplicity here does not mean lack of rigour. It means choosing an action whose scale is proportionate to available capacity and whose effects can be observed, cared for, and learned from.

Both entry points must ultimately be connected through a shared landscape intelligence. The relevant questions include: What is happening? Where is it happening? At what spatial scale? Over what time horizon? Which populations and species are affected? Who benefits, who bears the burden, and who has the authority or capacity to act? What is proportionate to the place? What feedback will show whether the intervention is helping or creating a new problem?

A project may therefore move through the following connected stages, without assuming that every project begins at the first stage:

| Stage | Purpose | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Clear** | Reduce immediate ecological obstruction and harm | Waste audits, source segregation, reduction, repair, reuse, composting, safe recovery, pollution removal |
| **Restore** | Rebuild damaged physical conditions | Soil remediation, water recharge, erosion control, removal of harmful materials, revival of neglected spaces |
| **Cultivate** | Establish living systems | Native trees, mixed planting, seed work, habitat, pollinator support, biodiversity corridors |
| **Enable** | Build human and ecological capacity | Training, local stewardship, ecological education, community decision-making, livelihood connections |
| **Sustain** | Maintain continuity and learn over time | Monitoring, care schedules, adaptation, knowledge sharing, ecological indicators, intergenerational participation |

A community may enter through waste, water, soil, food, trees, education, or organisation depending on local needs. What must remain consistent is the underlying direction: **from reduced harm toward increased regenerative capacity, through a process that can begin simply but must learn rigorously**.

## 8. Sri Lankan landscape intelligence

Sri Lanka must be studied at more than one scale. A visible waste problem at a street, school, market, or neighbourhood level may be connected to municipal systems, river or drainage networks, coastal transport, national policy, consumption patterns, and global material flows. Similarly, a tree-planting project may affect a site immediately while its ecological value depends on watershed conditions, species relationships, land tenure, maintenance, climate, and long-term community use.

The project’s research should therefore build a layered understanding of place. It should examine ecological zones and watersheds; urban, rural, estate, coastal, dry-zone, wet-zone, and hill-country contexts where relevant; material and pollution pathways; human and non-human populations; institutional responsibilities; historical change; seasonal variation; and future pressures. It should distinguish measured evidence from local knowledge, assumptions, and hypotheses.

Proportionality should be a guiding principle. The scale of an intervention, the resources it consumes, the risks it creates, the benefits it promises, and the maintenance burden it imposes should be appropriate to the site and its communities. National data can reveal patterns, but it cannot replace local observation. A small intervention can be meaningful without pretending to solve a national problem, while a national strategy must not erase local differences.

Sri Lanka’s National Environmental Action Plan 2022–2030 places responsible consumption and production within national environmental planning and identifies waste minimisation and resource efficiency among its associated themes [8]. Recent work on Sri Lankan municipal solid waste also points to uneven collection coverage, the continuing significance of open dumping, and implementation challenges across national, provincial, and local-authority levels [9]. These conditions reinforce the need for Anavaratha to study not only what waste exists, but how responsibility, exposure, service, and capacity are distributed.

The World Bank’s account of Sri Lanka’s ecosystem conservation work further illustrates the value of connecting biodiversity protection, restoration, land-use planning, and community livelihoods [10]. For Anavaratha, this supports a model in which ecological cultivation is not separated from human capacity, livelihood, and stewardship.

## 9. The role of trees

Trees should be central to Anavaratha’s imagination, but they should not be treated as isolated objects or numerical achievements. A tree is part of a larger ecology involving soil organisms, fungi, insects, birds, water, shade, roots, people, and time.

The project should therefore ask not only how many trees are planted, but whether the planting creates a viable and cared-for system. Important considerations may include the suitability of species to the place, diversity of planting, water requirements, survival and growth, protection from damage, relationship to existing habitats, and the participation of local people in care.

The tree becomes a powerful symbol because it connects the two sides of Anavaratha. It is planted into ground that may first need to be cleared and repaired, but it also represents a future that cannot be rushed. It embodies the movement from **removing obstruction** to **creating shelter, habitat, food, shade, and continuity**.

## 10. Language and visual direction

The project’s language should avoid framing the two sides as “negative” and “positive” in a moral sense. More generative pairs include:

| Clearing side | Cultivating side |
|---|---|
| Release | Regeneration |
| Removal | Renewal |
| Clearing | Growing |
| Freedom from harm | Freedom for flourishing |
| Making space | Giving space life |
| Restoring possibility | Developing capacity |

Visually, Anavaratha could be expressed through a **fold, threshold, cycle, root system, spiral, or continuous turning**. The two sides need not appear as symmetrical halves. One may be open, sparse, or subtractive; the other may be dense, branching, and generative. Their connection should be visible, showing that clearing is not the end of the project but the opening through which regeneration begins.

## 11. Provisional mission statement

> **Anavaratha is an ecological initiative that transforms obstructed and degraded spaces into conditions for life. Through responsible waste practices, ecological restoration, biomimetic learning, biodiversity, trees, distributed participation, and community stewardship, it moves from freedom from environmental harm toward freedom for regeneration.**

A shorter public-facing version could be:

> **Clearing what harms life. Cultivating what allows it to flourish.**

A more poetic version could be:

> **One side clears the ground; the other makes the ground alive.**

## 12. The eagle-eye phase before field entry

Anavaratha should not rush from inspiration to intervention. Before selecting a site, partner, pilot, or planting programme, it needs a rigorous desk-based sense-making phase focused on Sri Lanka as a connected ecological and social landscape.

This phase should examine ecological zones, watersheds, forests, wetlands, coasts, agricultural and urban systems, waste and pollution flows, institutions, populations, livelihoods, existing initiatives, historical change, seasonal variation, and future pressures. It should work across scales and distinguish national patterns from district, local-authority, neighbourhood, and site-level conditions.

The two sides may enter this research differently. Waste management is likely to begin with conceptual desk research, systems analysis, material-flow mapping, governance, and the distribution of exposure and responsibility. Cultivation may appear simpler and may eventually offer an early leverage point, but it requires equal rigour: species, soil, water, land use, maintenance, biodiversity, local livelihoods, unintended effects, and long-term continuity must all be understood.

The purpose of the eagle-eye phase is not to produce a perfect or total account of Sri Lanka. It is to establish enough grounded understanding to identify relationships, uncertainties, affected populations, institutional capacities, and proportionate opportunities. Fieldwork should begin only when it can answer meaningful questions and test a carefully stated hypothesis rather than merely perform an attractive activity.

The eagle-eye phase should also include a reflexive question derived from _Adaptation._: how is Anavaratha’s own framing shaping what it can see? The project should periodically revisit its categories, ask what has been omitted, preserve meaningful disagreement, and allow new ecological or community knowledge to change the atlas rather than forcing new evidence into an old structure.

> **First see the whole landscape; then locate the leverage point—but keep the map open to revision.**

## 13. Questions for the next version

The concept is strong enough to document now, but several decisions will make the next version more specific. Anavaratha will need to define its initial geography and community, identify whether waste management is its primary programme or one part of a broader ecological practice, and decide whether its first work will focus on public spaces, degraded land, schools, farms, neighbourhoods, or another setting.

It will also need to establish what “development” means in practice, which ecological indicators matter, how local knowledge will guide decisions, how long-term maintenance will be funded and organised, how distributed participation can remain accountable to local places, and how the project will avoid turning regeneration into a short-term branding exercise. These questions do not weaken the concept. They are the conditions that can turn its philosophy into a durable practice.
