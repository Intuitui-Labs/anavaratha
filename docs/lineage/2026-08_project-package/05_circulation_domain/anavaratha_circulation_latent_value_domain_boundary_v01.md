# Anavaratha

## Provisional Circulation and Latent-Value Domain

**Concept and boundary note — Version 0.1**

## 1. The seed idea

A person may stop using an object without the object becoming useless. It may be needed by someone else, repairable, exchangeable, rentable, giftable, valuable in parts, useful for a different purpose, or worth preserving for future use.

The seed of this domain is therefore simple:

> **Before something becomes waste, it may still be waiting for another relationship.**

This is not only a marketplace idea. It is a question about how societies recognise, preserve, transfer, and reactivate value that is invisible from the perspective of the current owner.

## 2. Working domain definition

The domain is provisionally called the **Circulation and Latent-Value Domain**. It studies how things move from one user, context, or purpose to another while retaining or recovering usefulness.

The domain may include resale, gifting, sharing, lending, renting, swapping, repair, refurbishment, remanufacture, redistribution, community exchange, reuse of components, and creative repurposing. It may involve money, but it is not defined by buying and selling. Value may be economic, practical, social, cultural, educational, emotional, ecological, or emergency-related.

## 3. Why it is distinct from waste

Waste begins when a material or object is treated as discarded, unwanted, unusable, or without sufficient value in its present system. The circulation domain begins earlier, at the moment an object is **no longer needed by its current user but may still be useful elsewhere**.

| Question | Circulation and latent value | Waste and material responsibility |
|---|---|---|
| **Starting point** | “I no longer need this; who might?” | “This has been discarded or is creating burden; what now?” |
| **Primary concern** | Matching unused capacity with another need | Reducing harm, burden, exposure, and material leakage |
| **Main value** | Retaining or recovering usefulness | Preventing, managing, treating, or accounting for waste |
| **Typical pathways** | Reuse, exchange, lending, repair, resale, gifting, redistribution | Reduction, collection, treatment, recovery, safe disposal, accountability |
| **Success condition** | The object enters a meaningful new use without disproportionate burden | Harm and material burden are reduced or responsibly managed |
| **Failure condition** | Value is hidden, access is mismatched, or exchange creates new harm | Waste is displaced, externalised, accumulated, or poorly governed |

The two domains may eventually connect, but they should not be merged. A circulation record may later become a waste record if the object cannot find a safe or meaningful next use. That transition must be visible rather than assumed.

## 4. Why it is distinct from ecological cultivation

Regenerative cultivation concerns living systems, habitats, organisms, ecological conditions, and the care that allows life to continue. The circulation domain concerns mostly human-made or human-held things and the relationships through which their usefulness can continue.

They can meet around tools, seeds, containers, equipment, building materials, garden infrastructure, or knowledge resources, but the primary questions remain different.

## 5. The latent-value lifecycle

```text
Acquisition
    ↓
Use and care
    ↓
No longer needed by current user
    ↓
Recognised as potentially useful
    ↓
Matched with another need
    ↓
Transfer, sharing, repair, adaptation, or reconfiguration
    ↓
New use and new relationship
    ↓
Further circulation or responsible end-of-use decision
```

The key moment is not disposal. It is the interval between **personal disuse** and **material uselessness**. Anavaratha can study what makes that interval visible, trustworthy, accessible, and worth acting upon.

## 6. The domain’s central question

> **How can things that are no longer needed by one person find appropriate new relationships with people who need, value, repair, share, or responsibly transform them—before they become waste?**

This question contains several subquestions:

| Dimension | Question |
|---|---|
| **Visibility** | How does someone discover that an unused thing exists? |
| **Need** | How does another person express a genuine need? |
| **Fit** | How is condition, quality, compatibility, size, location, and usefulness understood? |
| **Trust** | How are people protected from fraud, unsafe goods, false descriptions, and coercive exchange? |
| **Access** | Who can participate, and who is excluded by money, transport, language, time, or digital access? |
| **Value** | Who decides whether an object is worthless, saleable, giftable, repairable, or socially important? |
| **Movement** | How much effort, packaging, travel, and coordination does circulation require? |
| **Care** | Who repairs, cleans, stores, tests, guarantees, or maintains the object? |
| **Power** | Who benefits from the exchange, and who bears the risk or labour? |
| **End point** | What happens when the next use ends? |

## 7. Working principles

The domain should be guided by the following provisional principles:

1. **Disuse is not uselessness.** An object’s current non-use does not determine its total value.
2. **Value is relational.** The same object may be unwanted in one context and essential in another.
3. **Circulation is not automatically good.** A transfer can create transport burdens, unsafe conditions, exploitation, clutter, or displacement of local repair.
4. **Repair before replacement where proportionate.** The domain should examine whether repair, adaptation, or component recovery is more appropriate than new acquisition.
5. **Need before accumulation.** The purpose is not to move more objects; it is to connect real needs with existing capacity.
6. **Trust is infrastructure.** Description, condition, identity, safety, accountability, and reputation determine whether circulation works.
7. **Access must be plural.** Digital marketplaces are only one possible form; community, institutional, neighbourhood, and informal pathways matter.
8. **Labour must be visible.** Sorting, cleaning, repairing, storing, transporting, listing, and mediating are real work.
9. **The last owner is not the only stakeholder.** Makers, repairers, recipients, communities, workers, and future waste handlers may all be affected.
10. **Keep the boundary visible.** A circulation pathway should show when an object has entered waste management or requires a different responsibility.

## 8. Possible future names

The working title is intentionally descriptive. Other names may emerge after research:

| Candidate | Emphasis |
|---|---|
| **Circulation** | Movement into new relationships |
| **Latent Value** | Value that exists but is not currently activated |
| **Second Relationship** | A thing’s next social and practical connection |
| **Renewed Use** | Continuation through reuse, repair, and adaptation |
| **Material Reciprocity** | Exchange, need, responsibility, and mutual benefit |
| **Commons of Use** | Shared access beyond individual ownership |

No final name should be chosen before the domain’s real scope, ethical tensions, and Sri Lankan forms are understood.

## 9. Immediate research posture

This domain should begin with the same discipline used for cultivation: desk research, sense-making, knowledge-building, relationship mapping, uncertainty review, and only then proportionate action. Its first task is not to build an app or marketplace. It is to understand existing circulation practices, informal exchange, repair cultures, resale systems, donation networks, institutional surplus, and the conditions under which a “next use” is genuinely beneficial.

## 10. Provisional relationship to Anavaratha

The Circulation and Latent-Value Domain could become a third direction within Anavaratha, or it could be a bridge between the waste direction and wider social-economic systems. That structural decision should remain open until the domain is researched more fully.

For now, its safest position is:

> **A distinct emerging domain under Anavaratha, with a primary focus on extending useful relationships and a clearly governed boundary with waste.**
