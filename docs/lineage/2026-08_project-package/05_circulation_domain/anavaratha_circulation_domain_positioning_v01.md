# Anavaratha

## Provisional Circulation and Latent-Value Domain

**Position within the larger project home — Version 0.1**

## 1. Why this domain matters

Anavaratha began to hold two major ecological directions: regenerative ecological cultivation and waste/material responsibility. This new idea reveals a third kind of question. Some things are no longer needed by their current users, but they are not yet waste. Their next possibility may be another person, household, institution, community, repairer, learner, or use.

The domain asks what happens in that interval of **latent usefulness**.

> **Waste asks what to do when a material has become a burden. Circulation asks whether a useful relationship has ended—or only changed.**

This is a **provisional extension**, not a final restructuring of every existing Anavaratha document. The cultivation branch remains the active research stream in the current project history. The circulation domain is now recognised as an emerging domain that requires its own desk-first research and boundary protocol.

## 3. The three primary questions

| Anavaratha direction | Primary question |
|---|---|
| **Regenerative ecological cultivation** | What conditions allow living systems and their communities of care to regenerate and continue? |
| **Waste and material responsibility** | What materials and harms move through systems, where do burdens accumulate, and how can harm be reduced and governed? |
| **Circulation and latent value** | How can things no longer needed by one user find appropriate new relationships before they become waste? |

The directions are related, but none should absorb the others. Cultivation studies living conditions. Waste studies material burdens and harms. Circulation studies the continuation of usefulness and the social, economic, technical, and ethical conditions required for that continuation.

## 4. Why circulation is not a subdivision of waste

Reuse and source reduction are often discussed within waste hierarchies and circular-economy frameworks. The EPA places source reduction and reuse above recycling, recovery, treatment, and disposal [1]. The Ellen MacArthur Foundation describes keeping products and materials in circulation through maintenance, reuse, refurbishment, remanufacture, recycling, and composting [2].

Those frameworks are important reference points. Anavaratha’s domain distinction is about **starting point and centre of attention**:

| Feature | Circulation domain | Waste direction |
|---|---|---|
| **Starting point** | An unused or surplus thing that may still serve a need | A discarded, harmful, accumulated, or burdensome material flow |
| **Centre of attention** | Next relationship and continued usefulness | Responsibility for burden, harm, exposure, leakage, and endpoint |
| **Typical actor** | Current user, next user, repairer, lender, donor, marketplace, community, institution | Producer, consumer, collector, worker, municipality, regulator, handler, affected community |
| **Primary failure** | A useful thing is abandoned, mismatched, inaccessible, or transferred harmfully | Harm is displaced, hidden, accumulated, or irresponsibly managed |
| **Boundary event** | No safe, meaningful, or proportionate next relationship remains | Material requires waste governance or responsible disposal/recovery |

The same object can appear in both domains at different times. The circulation record should preserve its own question and then record the transition if the object enters waste responsibility.

## 5. Relationship to regenerative ecological cultivation

Circulation and cultivation meet around tools, seed-storage equipment, nursery materials, irrigation components, shade structures, educational resources, and community infrastructure. They remain distinct because the objects and relationships are not the same as living organisms and ecological conditions.

A circulation record may support cultivation, but it should not be treated as ecological evidence. A used water tank may be useful for a garden; that does not prove that the garden’s hydrology is regenerative. A shared tool may enable care; that does not prove that the care is ecologically appropriate.

## 6. The common Anavaratha method

All domains can share a disciplined beginning:

> **Desk research → sense-making → knowledge-building → relationship mapping → uncertainty review → proportionate decision**

The evidence fields differ by domain, however.

| Shared field | Circulation-specific interpretation |
|---|---|
| **Relationship** | Current user, next user, repairer, lender, recipient, institution, or end-of-use handler |
| **Condition** | Function, safety, completeness, repairability, compatibility, and burden of movement |
| **Capacity** | Repair skill, storage, transport, trust, money, time, digital access, and institutional support |
| **Value** | Price, usefulness, social meaning, cultural value, learning, emergency use, or avoided replacement |
| **Uncertainty** | Unknown condition, unclear ownership, unsafe history, uncertain need, or disputed value |
| **Care** | Maintenance, repair, honest description, handover, safeguarding, and endpoint planning |

## 7. Possible role inside Anavaratha

The new domain may eventually function as a full third direction, a bridge between the waste direction and human economies, or a distributed layer appearing across several project areas. Those alternatives should remain open while research proceeds.

For now, the clearest position is:

> **Circulation and latent value is an emerging third domain under Anavaratha, adjacent to waste but not contained by it, and capable of supporting cultivation without becoming ecological cultivation itself.**

## 8. Immediate implication

The project should not jump directly to a marketplace or application. The next desk-first work should map existing Sri Lankan and comparative practices: second-hand exchange, repair, lending, donation, institutional surplus, community sharing, informal repair work, refurbishment, and material reuse. It should ask what already works, what fails, who carries the labour, who is excluded, and where the boundary between circulation and waste is actually experienced.

The purpose is not to create more movement. It is to understand how useful capacity can be recognised and connected with need in ways that are safe, fair, locally appropriate, and environmentally proportionate.
