# Anavaratha

## Circulation and Latent-Value Domain

**Existing models of circulation and exchange — Version 0.1**

## 1. Purpose

The new domain should learn from existing circulation models without copying them as ready-made solutions. The models below show different ways of keeping a thing useful: prevention, direct reuse, sharing, repair, resale, donation, refurbishment, redistribution, and component recovery.

## 2. Model comparison

| Model | Basic mechanism | Value retained | Coordination requirement | Main caution |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Direct reuse or donation** | A usable object moves to another person or organisation | Whole-object function | Visibility, matching, transport, trust, and honest condition description | Donation can become a way to move unwanted or unusable goods to others |
| **Second-hand sale or exchange** | An object is sold, swapped, or exchanged after first use | Whole-object function and possible financial value | Price, condition, identity, payment, safety, and dispute resolution | Money can exclude need and may encourage speculation or rapid turnover |
| **Sharing, lending, or renting** | Access is shared without every person owning the object | High utilisation from fewer objects | Scheduling, storage, maintenance, return, trust, and responsibility | Shared goods can fail without care, repair, and accountability |
| **Repair and refurbishment** | Existing function is restored or improved | Function, materials, repair skills, and local capacity | Diagnosis, parts, tools, skills, fair labour, and quality assurance | Repair may be unsafe, expensive, unavailable, or intentionally obstructed by design |
| **Remanufacture or adaptation** | A product or component is rebuilt for continuing or new use | Higher-value components and embodied work | Technical standards, design, parts, testing, and markets | The process can consume new materials or hide unsafe substitutions |
| **Institutional redistribution** | Surplus moves through charities, public bodies, schools, or community organisations | Social and practical value beyond a single owner | Intake, sorting, matching, storage, safeguarding, and accountability | Institutional control may be slow, paternalistic, or disconnected from actual need |
| **Reuse centres or material exchange** | A physical place receives, stores, displays, and transfers usable goods or building materials | Objects and components before disposal | Space, staff, transport, testing, pricing, and end-of-use rules | Storage and low-demand inventory can recreate a waste problem |
| **Product-as-service** | A user pays for access or function rather than owning the product | Product life, maintenance, and service relationship | Provider responsibility, maintenance, return, data, and contract design | Can become restrictive or increase dependence on a provider |

## 3. What authoritative frameworks contribute

The EPA hierarchy places source reduction and reuse above recycling, energy recovery, treatment, and disposal. It identifies reusing or donating items, reuse, and remanufacturing as forms of source reduction [1]. This supports Anavaratha’s decision to begin the domain before the waste threshold.

The Ellen MacArthur Foundation describes a circular economy in which products and materials remain in circulation through maintenance, reuse, refurbishment, remanufacture, recycling, and composting. Its model highlights circulating products and materials at their highest value, while also placing circulation alongside waste elimination and nature regeneration [2]. Anavaratha can use this as a conceptual map while keeping its own domain more specifically focused on **unused things finding new relationships**.

The Singapore National Environment Agency provides a practical public-facing example of a circulation gateway. Its “Donation, Resale and Repair Channels” page organises responses to items people no longer need into categories including items in good condition, items that can be repaired, and items spoilt or damaged beyond repair. It then directs users toward donation, resale, repair, and recycling pathways [3]. The value of this model is its decision orientation: the user is not asked to decide that everything is waste at the beginning.

## 4. Sri Lankan research leads

Sri Lanka should not be represented as having no circulation practices simply because a formal platform or dataset is difficult to locate. Existing reuse, repair, resale, donation, informal exchange, and surplus redistribution may be distributed across households, markets, repair workers, charities, institutions, construction activity, and digital platforms.

The current research queue should therefore map actual practices rather than search only for organisations using the word “circular.” Important leads include second-hand apparel markets, repair and reuse in electronics, construction-material reuse, informal repair work, household exchange, and institution-to-community redistribution [4] [5] [6]. These leads require source-by-source verification before being treated as established Sri Lankan models.

## 5. A useful distinction among models

The models fall into three broad families:

| Family | Main question | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Relationship transfer** | Who else can use this object now? | Donation, resale, gifting, swapping, lending |
| **Capacity renewal** | What work can restore or adapt its usefulness? | Repair, refurbishment, remanufacture, repurposing |
| **Access redesign** | Can people access function without individual ownership? | Sharing, rental, product-as-service, tool libraries |

A strong Anavaratha model may combine all three, but should not assume that every object needs a digital marketplace. Sometimes the most appropriate intervention is a repair person, a local noticeboard, a school store, a community tool library, a trusted second-hand market, or simply better information about an existing pathway.

## 6. What Anavaratha can learn

The most transferable insight is not a particular platform. It is the need for **classification before coordination**. An object must be understood as usable, repairable, adaptable, unsafe, incomplete, location-bound, culturally significant, or no longer worth moving before a next-use pathway is chosen.

The second insight is that circulation needs infrastructure beyond matching. Trust, repair, storage, transport, fair labour, safety, language, payment, and a responsible endpoint are all part of the system.

The third insight is that the highest-value pathway is not always the most environmentally or socially appropriate. A local repair may be better than a long-distance resale; a gift may be better than a sale; a shared tool may be better than many privately owned tools; and safe disposal may be better than transferring an unsafe object to someone with less power.

## 7. Repair Café as a community-capacity model

The Repair Café Foundation describes Repair Cafés as free meeting places where visitors repair household items together with expert volunteers using shared tools and materials. The model combines direct repair, practical learning, appreciation of repair skills, and local social connection. The organisation reports more than 2,500 Repair Cafés worldwide and describes the movement as supporting repair skills and more repairable products [7].

For Anavaratha, the important feature is not the branded format. It is the combination of **object repair, skill transfer, shared tools, volunteer or paid expertise, and community trust**. The model shows that circulation can be a social learning system rather than a transaction platform. It also warns that a free community repair event does not replace professional repair; it can instead help people recognise repair as an alternative and direct them to specialists when appropriate [7].

The model should be treated as comparative inspiration, not as proof that a Repair Café already exists in Sri Lanka or as a design to copy without checking local repair cultures, professional livelihoods, tools, languages, safety, and institutional context.
