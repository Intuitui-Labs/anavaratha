# Anavaratha

## Circulation and Latent-Value Domain

**Ethical and operational logic — Version 0.1**

## 1. The domain’s responsibility

The purpose of circulation is not to move the greatest possible number of objects. It is to create **appropriate new relationships** between unused capacity and real need, while preserving dignity, safety, fair labour, local repair, and a responsible endpoint.

A circulation system therefore has two responsibilities at once:

1. **Extend usefulness where this is genuinely beneficial.**
2. **Refuse or redirect circulation when transfer would create greater harm than retention, repair, recovery, or responsible disposal.**

The EPA hierarchy places source reduction and reuse above recycling, recovery, treatment, and disposal, but also recognises that no single management approach is suitable for every material or waste stream [1]. The circulation domain adopts this priority without turning it into an absolute rule.

## 2. Ethical principles

| Principle | Meaning for the domain |
|---|---|
| **Need before movement** | An item should move because a real or plausible need exists, not merely because the current user wants to clear space |
| **Dignity before disposal avoidance** | People receiving used goods are not endpoints for another person’s guilt, clutter, or inferior-quality surplus |
| **Honest condition** | Wear, defects, missing parts, safety concerns, age, provenance, and uncertainty must be visible |
| **Safety before circulation** | Unsafe, contaminated, recalled, illegal, or unsuitable goods must not be transferred simply to avoid a waste label |
| **Repairer respect** | Community repair should support rather than erase professional repair skills, livelihoods, and fair compensation |
| **Locality where proportionate** | Nearby circulation may reduce transport, packaging, and coordination burdens, but locality must not override real need or compatibility |
| **Plural value** | Economic price is one form of value among practical, social, cultural, educational, emotional, and emergency value |
| **Access without coercion** | Sale, gift, loan, barter, rental, and mutual aid should remain distinct, and participants should not be pushed into one mode |
| **Labour visibility** | Listing, sorting, cleaning, transporting, repairing, testing, storing, and mediating are work that should be acknowledged and fairly supported |
| **Reciprocity** | Communities and people whose knowledge, labour, space, or trust make circulation possible should receive appropriate benefit and credit |
| **Privacy and consent** | Personal data, household details, photographs, location information, and transaction histories should be collected minimally and used transparently |
| **No false circularity** | A transfer is not automatically sustainable; the system must examine burdens, failure, and the item’s next endpoint |
| **Exit responsibility** | Every circulation pathway should make the next end-of-use decision visible |

## 3. Operational cycle

```text
1. Declare the item or capacity
          ↓
2. Classify current status and condition
          ↓
3. Identify need, fit, and possible next users
          ↓
4. Compare pathways and burdens
          ↓
5. Verify safety, trust, permission, and value
          ↓
6. Transfer, share, repair, adapt, or retain
          ↓
7. Confirm outcome and record learning
          ↓
8. Prepare the next responsible endpoint
```

The cycle is designed to prevent a marketplace-first approach. Matching is only one step. A good system also needs condition assessment, repair pathways, safe handling, fair exchange, transport, storage, dispute resolution, and an end-of-use route.

## 4. Operational classification

| Classification | Minimum condition | Preferred pathway |
|---|---|---|
| **Ready for continued use** | Safe, functional, identifiable, and suitable for another context | Local sharing, lending, gifting, resale, or redistribution |
| **Underused but retained** | Useful to current user occasionally or as backup | Shared access, rental, lending, or cooperative ownership |
| **Repairable** | Function can be restored through proportionate skilled work | Professional or community repair with fair labour and parts transparency |
| **Adaptable** | Original use is limited but another use is plausible | Reconfiguration or repurposing with material and safety review |
| **Incomplete** | Missing parts or information but potentially recoverable | Parts search, repair assessment, or honest low-confidence transfer |
| **Culturally or personally significant** | Value depends on memory, identity, heritage, or relationship | Consent-based transfer, preservation, or non-commercial care |
| **Unsafe or restricted** | Risk cannot be responsibly controlled | Withhold from circulation and route to the responsible specialist or waste pathway |
| **No viable next relationship** | Need, fit, safety, or burden conditions fail | Responsible end-of-use management; do not force circulation |

## 5. The five-part suitability test

An object should become a circulation candidate only when five conditions are considered together:

| Test | Question |
|---|---|
| **Usefulness** | Is there a credible next use? |
| **Condition** | Is the object safe, functional, repairable, or honestly described? |
| **Fit** | Is it compatible with the next user’s context, skills, space, and resources? |
| **Burden** | Are movement, storage, repair, packaging, and coordination proportionate? |
| **Accountability** | Can the system identify who describes, transfers, repairs, supports, and receives it? |

Failure at one test should not be concealed by success at another. High usefulness does not cancel unsafe condition. Low price does not cancel high transport burden. A charitable intention does not cancel poor fit.

## 6. Exchange modes and fairness

The circulation domain should make the exchange mode explicit because each mode distributes power differently.

| Mode | Appropriate when | Fairness question |
|---|---|---|
| **Sale** | The recipient values the item and the price supports a viable exchange | Who can afford access, and who sets the price? |
| **Gift** | The object can meet a need without requiring payment | Is the item genuinely useful, or is disposal being disguised as generosity? |
| **Loan** | The need is temporary or the object is costly and shareable | Who maintains, returns, insures, and repairs it? |
| **Rental** | Recurring access is more useful than ownership | Who controls access and bears long-term maintenance responsibility? |
| **Swap** | Each party has something the other values | Are needs and values sufficiently balanced? |
| **Repair service** | The object’s value is recoverable through skilled work | Is the repairer fairly compensated and the result safe? |
| **Institutional redistribution** | A trusted organisation can match surplus with collective need | Are recipients involved in defining what is needed? |

## 7. Trust architecture

Trust should be designed as a set of visible practices rather than assumed from a platform or organisation.

| Trust element | Minimum practice |
|---|---|
| **Identity** | Describe what the object is, including model, size, age, origin where relevant, and missing information |
| **Condition** | Use consistent condition language and photographs or inspection where appropriate |
| **Safety** | Flag hazards, recalls, contamination, electrical or mechanical risks, and specialist-only items |
| **Expectation** | State whether the object is ready to use, needs repair, or is for parts |
| **Transfer** | Record handover, payment or gift terms, and responsibility after transfer |
| **Dispute** | Provide a fair way to correct inaccurate descriptions or unsafe exchanges |
| **Privacy** | Reveal only the location and personal data needed to coordinate the transfer |
| **Traceability** | Preserve a minimal history sufficient for repair, recall, accountability, or future end-of-use decisions |

## 8. Measuring success

A circulation system should not measure success only by listings, transactions, kilograms moved, or revenue. It should also ask whether real needs were met and whether burdens were fairly distributed.

| Outcome area | Possible question |
|---|---|
| **Usefulness** | Did the object enter a meaningful new use? |
| **Longevity** | Was its useful life extended, and by how much is known? |
| **Repair capacity** | Were repair skills, tools, parts, and local livelihoods strengthened? |
| **Access** | Did people with limited purchasing power gain safe access? |
| **Burden** | Were transport, storage, packaging, labour, and emissions proportionate? |
| **Quality** | Did the recipient receive what was represented? |
| **Equity** | Who benefited, who worked, and who bore risk? |
| **Waste avoidance** | Was disposal postponed without merely transferring the problem? |
| **Endpoint** | Is the next end-of-use pathway clearer than before? |
| **Learning** | Did the system improve its classifications and relationships? |

## 9. Ethical stopping rule

The domain should stop trying to circulate an object when the next use is unsafe, coercive, misleading, excessively burdensome, incompatible, or unsupported by real need. A responsible refusal is a success of the system, not a failure of circularity.

> **The goal is not to save every object. The goal is to avoid abandoning useful relationships too early, and to avoid creating harmful relationships in the name of reuse.**
