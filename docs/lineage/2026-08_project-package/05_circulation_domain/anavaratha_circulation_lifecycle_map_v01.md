# Anavaratha

## Circulation and Latent-Value Domain

**Lifecycle between use and waste — Version 0.1**

## 1. Purpose

The new domain occupies a specific interval in a thing’s life: the interval after its current user no longer needs it and before it has become materially or socially unusable. Its purpose is to understand how that thing may enter another useful relationship without creating disproportionate harm, cost, labour, risk, or exclusion.

The United States Environmental Protection Agency’s materials-management hierarchy places source reduction and reuse above recycling, energy recovery, treatment, and disposal, and identifies reusing or donating items, reuse, and remanufacturing as forms of source reduction [1]. The Ellen MacArthur Foundation describes circularity through maintenance, reuse, refurbishment, remanufacture, recycling, and composting, with products and materials kept in circulation at their highest value [2].

These sources help orient the domain, but Anavaratha’s question is narrower and more humanly situated: **what happens when an object is no longer needed by one person but may still be needed by someone else?**

## 2. Lifecycle map

```text
Need and acquisition
        ↓
Active use
        ↓
Maintenance and care
        ↓
Current user no longer needs the thing
        ↓
Assessment: useful, repairable, adaptable, shareable, or unsafe?
        ↓
────────────────────────────────────────────
│ New relationship pathways                  │
│ • keep and use differently                 │
│ • lend or share                            │
│ • gift or donate                           │
│ • sell or exchange                         │
│ • repair or refurbish                      │
│ • rent or provide as a service             │
│ • redistribute through an institution      │
│ • recover components or material           │
────────────────────────────────────────────
        ↓
Second or continuing use
        ↓
Further maintenance, repair, adaptation, or transfer
        ↓
Assessment again
        ↓
Material recovery, treatment, or responsible disposal
```

The map is not a promise that every object should circulate indefinitely. It is a decision structure that keeps several possibilities visible before disposal becomes the default.

## 3. The key boundary

The crucial distinction is between **personal disuse** and **material waste**.

| State | Meaning | Appropriate question |
|---|---|---|
| **In use** | The object is serving its present user or system | How can its useful life be maintained? |
| **Underused** | The object is usable but idle, occasional, or surplus | Could access be shared or redistributed? |
| **No longer needed by current user** | The object has lost relevance for its current owner but not necessarily for others | Who might need, value, repair, or adapt it? |
| **Circulation candidate** | Its condition, safety, identity, and logistics allow another relationship | What is the lowest-burden viable pathway? |
| **Repair or refurbishment candidate** | It is not ready for transfer but can return to use through proportionate work | Is repair worth the labour, cost, skill, and material input? |
| **Component or material candidate** | Whole-object use has ended but parts or materials retain value | Can components be safely recovered without disguising waste? |
| **Waste** | No safe, useful, affordable, or responsible next relationship is available | How should the remaining burden be governed? |

An object can move backwards or sideways in this map. A failed repair may return it to component recovery; a new need may reactivate an underused object; and a donated item may become waste if the recipient cannot use it.

## 4. Four value-preservation levels

The domain should distinguish levels because moving a whole object is often preferable to breaking it down into materials, but not always.

| Level | Pathway | Value preserved |
|---|---|---|
| **Level 1: Continue** | Maintain, share, lend, rent, or use differently | Whole-object function and relationship |
| **Level 2: Transfer** | Gift, donate, resell, exchange, or redistribute | Whole-object function in a new context |
| **Level 3: Renew** | Repair, refurbish, upgrade, adapt, or remanufacture | Whole-object function through renewed capacity |
| **Level 4: Recover** | Reuse components or recycle materials | Parts or material value after whole-object use ends |

This is a heuristic, not a universal ranking. A long-distance transfer can create more burden than local recycling; a cheap repair can be preferable to a complicated exchange; and an unsafe object should not circulate merely to avoid a waste label.

## 5. Decision gates

Every proposed circulation pathway should pass through five gates:

| Gate | Question | If the answer is weak |
|---|---|---|
| **Need** | Is there a real or plausible recipient need rather than a desire to move clutter? | Do not transfer automatically |
| **Condition** | Is the object functional, repairable, complete, safe, and honestly described? | Repair, disclose limits, or withhold |
| **Fit** | Is it compatible with the recipient’s context, skills, space, language, and resources? | Adapt, match differently, or do not circulate |
| **Burden** | Are transport, packaging, storage, labour, money, and time proportionate? | Choose a closer or simpler pathway |
| **Afterlife** | What happens when the next use ends? | Preserve a return, repair, recovery, or responsible end-of-use path |

## 6. Why circulation can fail

Circulation is not automatically beneficial. It can move unwanted goods into poorer communities, create hidden transport and storage burdens, conceal unsafe conditions, exploit repair or sorting labour, increase platform dependence, or generate a false sense that consumption has been made harmless. The domain must therefore study not just whether an item changes hands, but whether its next relationship is useful, safe, dignified, and proportionate.

The practical success condition is not “more objects circulated.” It is:

> **A real need is met through an existing object with less new production and without shifting disproportionate cost, risk, labour, or waste to someone else.**

## 7. The role of money

Money is one possible coordination mechanism, not the definition of value. A thing may circulate through sale, donation, barter, lending, rent, institutional redistribution, repair credit, or mutual aid. Different mechanisms distribute access and power differently.

| Pathway | Potential strength | Potential concern |
|---|---|---|
| **Sale** | Clear exchange and possible income recovery | Excludes people without money; can reward planned obsolescence or speculation |
| **Gift or donation** | Extends access beyond purchasing power | Can transfer unusable goods and hide the giver’s disposal problem |
| **Swap or barter** | Mutual exchange without standard price | Requires matching needs and may undervalue labour |
| **Lending or sharing** | High use from fewer objects | Requires trust, maintenance, scheduling, and return systems |
| **Repair or refurbishment** | Preserves function and skills | Requires time, parts, tools, training, and fair compensation |
| **Institutional redistribution** | Can match surplus with collective need | May become bureaucratic, slow, or paternalistic |

## 8. Connection to waste without becoming waste research

The circulation domain does not replace waste responsibility. It identifies and supports possible next-use relationships before the waste threshold, while recording when an object crosses into a waste pathway. The waste direction may later study the remaining burdens of objects that cannot circulate, but that is outside this domain’s immediate research scope.

The boundary can be stated simply:

> **Circulation asks how usefulness can continue. Waste asks how remaining burden and harm must be responsibly managed.**

## 9. Research fields for a future circulation record

Each record should preserve:

| Field | Question |
|---|---|
| **Object or capacity** | What is being offered, shared, repaired, or redistributed? |
| **Current status** | In use, underused, surplus, no longer needed, damaged, or unsafe? |
| **Potential next user** | Who might need or value it, and how is that need known? |
| **Condition** | What works, what does not, and what is uncertain? |
| **Repairability** | Can it be repaired, by whom, with which parts and skills? |
| **Compatibility** | Will it fit the next user’s context and resources? |
| **Exchange mode** | Sale, gift, loan, swap, rent, service, institutional transfer, or other? |
| **Burden** | What transport, storage, money, labour, packaging, and time are required? |
| **Trust** | How are identity, condition, safety, and accountability established? |
| **Afterlife** | What happens after the next use? |
| **Equity** | Who gains access, who performs the labour, and who bears risk? |
| **Evidence state** | What is documented, inferred, disputed, or unknown? |
