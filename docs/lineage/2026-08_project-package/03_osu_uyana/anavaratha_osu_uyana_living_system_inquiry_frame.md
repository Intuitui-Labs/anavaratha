# Anavaratha

## Osu Uyana Living-System Inquiry

**Cultivation-only research frame — Version 0.1**

## 1. Purpose

This inquiry asks what **Osu Uyana** can mean and become as a living cultivation system in Sri Lanka today. It begins with the user’s clarification that *Osu Uyana* means medicinal garden in Sinhala, while recognising that a translation does not automatically capture every local, institutional, historical, or ecological meaning.

The inquiry is not a plan to establish a garden. It is a desk-first attempt to understand the conditions, relationships, knowledge, and forms of stewardship through which a medicinal garden may support living continuity.

> **Osu Uyana is treated first as a question about relationships, not as a predetermined garden design.**

## 2. Working definition

For this inquiry, an Osu Uyana is provisionally understood as a **medicinal-plant cultivation and knowledge space whose ecological, cultural, institutional, and stewardship conditions must be investigated rather than assumed**.

This working definition remains open. An Osu Uyana may be an official production garden, a demonstration or education garden, a community garden, a school garden, a household medicinal garden, a research garden, a retained medicinal landscape, or a network connecting several such spaces. These forms should not be collapsed into one category before their sources and local uses are understood.

## 3. What the inquiry is trying to discover

| Inquiry dimension | Central question |
|---|---|
| **Meaning** | Who uses the term Osu Uyana, in which language, and for what kind of place or practice? |
| **Form** | Is the garden official, community-managed, household-based, school-based, productive, demonstrative, conserved, or mixed? |
| **Landscape** | In which Sri Lankan ecological regions, cultural landscapes, and settlement contexts does it occur? |
| **Conditions** | What soil, water, shade, moisture, elevation, climate, and disturbance conditions support it? |
| **Living community** | Which plants, trees, insects, birds, fungi, soil organisms, and people are involved? |
| **Knowledge** | Which medicinal, botanical, agricultural, cultural, and local-language knowledge systems meet there? |
| **Stewardship** | Who plants, tends, teaches, harvests, governs, protects, or decides what should not be collected? |
| **Time** | How do the garden and its relationships change across seasons, generations, cultivation cycles, and succession? |
| **Care** | What is the appropriate form of care: propagation, protection, observation, education, harvest restraint, habitat repair, or non-intervention? |
| **Continuity** | What conditions allow knowledge, seeds, soil life, people, and living beings to continue together? |

## 4. Historical caution

Current evidence supports the existence of contemporary Sri Lankan herbal-garden uses of the term Osu Uyana and broader histories of managed royal, monastic, and home gardens. It does not yet establish that an ancient formal institution was named Osu Uyana or that modern gardens are uninterrupted continuations of one ancient institution [1] [2].

The inquiry must therefore keep three layers separate:

| Layer | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **Contemporary usage** | Investigate directly through current Sinhala, institutional, community, and garden sources |
| **Historical managed gardens** | Study through chronicles, archaeology, historical landscapes, and scholarship |
| **Exact-name continuity** | Treat as unresolved until a source explicitly connects the name and institution across time |

## 5. Relationship to Anavaratha’s cultivation branch

The Osu Uyana inquiry follows Anavaratha’s living-sector descent:

> **Landscape → habitat → living community → organism → relationship → condition → proportionate care**

It therefore refuses to reduce a medicinal garden to a list of useful plants. A garden record should be capable of showing relationships among plants, soil, water, shade, pollinators, seed dispersers, fungi, people, knowledge, labour, seasonal timing, and institutions. A medicinal claim, ecological claim, and cultivation claim must each carry its own evidence.

## 6. Scope boundaries

This inquiry includes medicinal and herbal gardens, medicinal plants, soil and water conditions, associated biodiversity, ecological functions, local and institutional knowledge, stewardship, seasonality, conservation, seed and propagation questions, and the ethics of recording or sharing knowledge.

It excludes medical diagnosis, treatment advice, dosage, efficacy claims, uncontrolled plant lists, species prescriptions, sensitive collection locations, immediate site selection, field intervention, and the waste direction of Anavaratha.

## 7. Evidence posture

The inquiry uses existing sources first and treats Anavaratha as a careful connector rather than a replacement catalogue. Each claim should preserve source, language, date, geography, method, authority, access condition, confidence, and the distinction between direct evidence and Anavaratha interpretation.

The methodological influence of *Adaptation.* remains active here: the Osu Uyana map must be provisional, self-aware, and revisable. A category may help attention while also hiding local difference. A garden record may describe a place without containing the living system that sustains it.

> **The inquiry should map Osu Uyana carefully enough to remain aware of what the map cannot see.**

## 8. First research outputs

The inquiry should produce four linked outputs:

| Output | Function |
|---|---|
| **Documented-form register** | Records current Sri Lankan uses and types of Osu Uyana or medicinal gardens |
| **Living-condition matrix** | Connects garden form to soil, water, shade, climate, organisms, people, and time |
| **Knowledge-and-stewardship register** | Records authority, language, care, access, consent, attribution, and governance |
| **Osu Uyana atlas layer** | Adds carefully qualified garden and relationship records to the cultivation atlas |
