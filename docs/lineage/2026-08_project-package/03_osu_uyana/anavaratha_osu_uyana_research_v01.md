## Osu Uyana and medicinal-garden research

**Sri Lankan terminology and present institutional use — Version 0.1**

## 1. Current evidence from the Sri Lanka Ayurvedic Drug Corporation

**Source:** [Herbal Garden – Sri Lanka Ayurvedic Drugs Corporation](https://sladc.lk/en/herbal-garden/)

The SLDAC page uses “Osu Uyana” in Sinhala-language sections describing herbal-garden sites and presents these gardens as part of a state programme concerned with cultivating medicinal plants and herbs. The page describes herbal gardens across the country as holding diverse herbs and medicinal-plant species, with gardens that are state-owned or administered by the Sri Lanka Ayurvedic Drug Corporation. It also states that raw materials for Ayurvedic medicines are sourced primarily from in-house herbal gardens, with some obtained from other reliable sources.

The page contains detailed Sinhala-language garden records, including locations, administrative divisions, extent, ownership, agro-climatic zones, current and proposed crops, plant counts, cultivated acreage, ponds, and retained natural-forest areas. It lists, among others, Nikaweratiya, Medawachchiya Dangolla, and Medawachchiya garden sites. These records show that “Osu Uyana” is used in contemporary Sri Lankan institutional practice for medicinal or herbal gardens, including gardens with cultivation, demonstration, production, and some retained ecological features.

The page was blocked by a CAPTCHA during browser access, so the extracted text should be treated as a source lead requiring direct manual re-check before quoting detailed Sinhala entries or translating every field. The existence of the page and its broad institutional framing are useful, but individual garden figures, ownership statements, plant lists, and translations should not yet be copied into the atlas as final verified records.

**Current conclusion:** The exact contemporary phrase “Osu Uyana” is demonstrably used in Sri Lankan medicinal-garden contexts. This supports the user’s clarification that it means a medicinal garden in Sinhala. It does not, by itself, prove that the same phrase named an ancient formal institution.

## 2. Distinction from Ganewatta

**Official source:** [Ganewatta Medicinal Plant Garden – Department of National Botanic Gardens](https://botanicgardens.gov.lk/project/ganewatta-medicinal-plant-garden/)

Ganewatta is a verified official Sri Lankan medicinal-plant garden under the Department of National Botanic Gardens. It is relevant as a present institutional garden model, but its official page should not automatically be equated with the phrase “Osu Uyana.” The current evidence supports the broader relationship—Ganewatta is a medicinal plant garden and “Osu Uyana” is a contemporary Sinhala term used for medicinal/herbal gardens—but not an identity claim that every official medicinal garden is an Osu Uyana or that Ganewatta historically continues an ancient institution under that exact name.

## 3. Historical question

The historical question remains open and should be separated into three claims:

| Claim | Current status |
|---|---|
| “Osu Uyana” means medicinal or herbal garden in contemporary Sinhala use | Supported by current Sri Lankan usage and the user’s linguistic clarification; institutional source verification is continuing |
| Sri Lanka has long histories of managed medicinal, royal, monastic, or home gardens | Plausible and supported by broader historical or ethnobotanical literature, but each claim requires its own primary or scholarly source |
| An ancient formal institution was named “Osu Uyana” and directly continues into modern gardens | Not yet verified; do not assert |

## 4. Research implication for Anavaratha

For Anavaratha, Osu Uyana should presently be treated as a **living contemporary category and research doorway**, not as a completed historical origin story. Its possible meanings include a medicinal-plant garden, a cultivation and propagation site, a knowledge-demonstration space, a production garden, a community or school garden, or a wider relationship among plants, healing knowledge, caretakers, and landscape.

The next step is to compare current uses across official, community, Sinhala-language, and scholarly sources while recording who uses the term, what kinds of gardens it names, which plants and practices are included, and whether the term carries local institutional or cultural meanings that cannot be reduced to an English translation.

## 5. Ganewatta official-page check

**Official page:** [Ganewatta Medicinal Plant Garden – Department of National Botanic Gardens](https://botanicgardens.gov.lk/project/ganewatta-medicinal-plant-garden/)

The current official Department of National Botanic Gardens page confirms the project title “Ganewatta Medicinal Plant Garden.” The accessible page provides the project identity but little descriptive content in the extracted text, and it does not itself define or use the phrase “Osu Uyana.” This reinforces the cautious distinction: Ganewatta is an official medicinal-plant-garden example, while “Osu Uyana” is a contemporary Sinhala term used in wider herbal-garden contexts. Their relationship should not be asserted as equivalence without a source that explicitly makes that connection.

**Access status:** Official project identity verified; detailed purpose, history, plant holdings, management model, and relationship to the term Osu Uyana require additional sources or direct institutional clarification.

## 6. Scholarly historical-garden evidence

**Scholarly source:** [The National Botanic Gardens of Sri Lanka: Origin and evolution](https://cjs.sljol.info/articles/10.4038/cjs.v55i2.9697), Ceylon Journal of Science, 2026, DOI 10.4038/cjs.v55i2.9697.

The article’s abstract states that early forms of managed plant landscapes in Sri Lanka—including royal, monastic, and home gardens—are recorded in ancient chronicles and archaeological evidence from the Anuradhapura and Polonnaruwa periods. It describes these gardens as combining aesthetic, cultural, and utilitarian functions and reflecting knowledge of plant cultivation and landscape design. It further describes the later development of Peradeniya as a formal botanical institution concerned with plant introduction, economic botany, research, conservation, education, and tourism.

This source supports a careful historical proposition: Sri Lanka has documented histories of managed plant landscapes with cultural and utilitarian dimensions. It does not, on the abstract alone, establish that an ancient garden was formally called “Osu Uyana,” nor that a current garden is an uninterrupted institutional continuation of one ancient site. The exact-name continuity claim remains open.

**Historical conclusion:** The broad historical background is now better supported, but the modern Sinhala term and the ancient institutional history must remain analytically separate until a source directly connects them.
