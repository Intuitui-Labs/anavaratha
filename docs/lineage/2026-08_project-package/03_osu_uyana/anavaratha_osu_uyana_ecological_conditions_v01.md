# Anavaratha

## Osu Uyana Living-System Inquiry

**Ecological and living-system conditions — Version 0.1**

## 1. Purpose

An Osu Uyana should not be understood as a container for medicinal plants alone. It is a living system whose viability depends on relationships among landscape, climate, soil, water, shade, plants, animals, insects, fungi, people, knowledge, labour, and time.

This matrix converts the documented garden forms into questions about conditions. It does not provide a universal design or recommend species for planting.

## 2. Condition matrix

| Condition layer | What the evidence suggests | Questions for Osu Uyana research |
|---|---|---|
| **Ecological region** | Ganewatta is located in Sri Lanka’s Intermediate Zone; the 2026 ecogeographical-mapping study frames medicinal-plant conservation and cultivation through ecological distribution and environmental requirements [1] [2] | Which garden forms belong to Dry, Intermediate, Wet, montane, coastal, or urban contexts? |
| **Climate** | Ganewatta’s official page describes a tropical climate with a distinct dry season and an average temperature of 25–30°C [1] | How do dry seasons, rainfall timing, heat, humidity, and extreme events affect survival, phenology, and care? |
| **Soil** | Jaffna homegarden research reports medium effectiveness of soil-conservation practices, while the medicinal-plant mapping literature identifies environmental requirements as a basis for cultivation [2] [3] | What soil depth, texture, drainage, organic matter, salinity, pH, and biological activity are present? |
| **Water** | Jaffna homegarden research reports low-medium effectiveness of water-conservation practices [3] | What water sources, seasonal constraints, drainage patterns, ponds, recharge conditions, and competing uses shape the garden? |
| **Shade and structure** | In the Jaffna study, tree canopy played a key role in regulating the homegarden environment; temperature was described as medium-cool and shade as medium-high [3] | Which layers provide shade, shelter, humidity, vertical structure, and microclimates without suppressing other organisms? |
| **Plant diversity** | The 125 Jaffna homegardens contained 135 plant species from 58 families, including more than 30 medicinal plants in trees, shrubs, and vines [3] | Is diversity arranged by household use, habitat, canopy layer, season, cultural practice, or chance? |
| **Animal and insect relations** | The Jaffna study recorded domestic fauna and reports a strong host–pollinator relationship involving bees and mango [3]; Ganewatta’s official page describes bee-keeping for pollinator conservation [1] | Which pollinators, herbivores, seed dispersers, predators, and domestic animals are present, and what conditions support them? |
| **Soil organisms** | Existing Anavaratha evidence includes Sri Lankan research on arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi in crop rhizospheres [4] | Which fungi and microorganisms are associated with medicinal plants, and how do management practices affect them? |
| **Propagation and seed continuity** | Ganewatta reports three nurseries, annual propagation, a seed room, and mother-plant collections [1] | How are seed identity, provenance, storage, viability, exchange, and local authority governed? |
| **Water and aquatic elements** | SLDAC garden records reportedly include ponds and water-plant sections; the details require direct re-check [5] | Are ponds functional habitat, irrigation infrastructure, cultural features, demonstration elements, or all of these? |
| **Seasonality** | The Yellow-eared Bulbul study at Horton Plains shows that fruit availability and feeding relationships vary across monsoon and inter-monsoon seasons [6] | What medicinal-plant growth, flowering, fruiting, harvest, seed, pollinator, and labour rhythms change through the year? |
| **Disturbance and succession** | Garden form may combine cultivated plots, retained natural vegetation, nurseries, paths, and educational spaces; Ganewatta reports retained collections and multiple functions [1] | Which areas are intensively managed, lightly managed, recovering, protected, or intentionally left alone? |
| **Human capacity** | Ganewatta is described as supporting education and research for students, doctors, botanists, schoolchildren, scientists, and the public [1] | What knowledge, labour, tools, funding, time, institutional continuity, and training sustain each garden? |
| **Landscape connection** | Ecogeographical mapping is presented as a basis for conservation, cultivation, and sustainable use rather than as a substitute for local evidence [2] | How does a garden connect to surrounding farms, forests, wetlands, homegardens, corridors, markets, and communities? |

## 3. The garden as a layered system

The conditions can be represented as a set of interacting layers rather than a checklist:

```text
Landscape and ecological region
        ↓
Climate, soil, water, shade, and disturbance
        ↓
Plant collection, seed continuity, and propagation
        ↓
Pollinators, soil organisms, birds, animals, and other living relations
        ↓
Knowledge, labour, institutions, households, and community stewardship
        ↓
Seasonal care, harvest restraint, learning, conservation, and renewal
```

The arrows do not imply a one-way hierarchy. A change in stewardship can alter soil and water; a change in rainfall can alter plants and labour; a change in plant diversity can alter pollinators; and a change in knowledge governance can alter what is cultivated, exchanged, or protected.

## 4. Ganewatta condition profile

Ganewatta is currently the most fully documented official garden form in this inquiry. The Department of National Botanic Gardens describes it as a 21.04-hectare medicinal-plant garden in the Intermediate Zone, with more than 300 medicinal-plant species, ex situ conservation, commercial cultivation, education, research, systematic cultivation, propagation nurseries, seed preservation, a mini herbarium, and bee-keeping [1].

The value of the Ganewatta record is not that it supplies a model to copy. Its value is that one official description makes several system layers visible at once:

| Visible capacity | Ecological question still required |
|---|---|
| Large medicinal-plant collection | How are habitat fit, provenance, and genetic diversity managed? |
| Ex situ conservation and gene pool | Which species or populations are prioritised, and what happens to them over time? |
| Three propagation nurseries | What are the substrate, water, labour, disease, and genetic controls? |
| Seed room and mother plants | How is seed identity retained, and who governs exchange? |
| Bee-keeping | Which pollinator species are present, and is the activity conservation, education, production, or all three? |
| Water plants and other sections | How do aquatic and terrestrial areas connect hydrologically and ecologically? |
| Education and research | How is knowledge shared, attributed, protected, and updated? |

## 5. Jaffna homegarden condition profile

The University of Jaffna study examined 125 homegardens in the Jaffna Peninsula and documented 135 plant species from 58 families, more than 30 medicinal plants, domestic fauna, carbon, fruit, nuts, fodder, pollinator relations, shade, and conservation practices [3]. It reports that tree canopy helped regulate the homegarden environment and records medium or low-medium effectiveness across several conservation dimensions.

This is not evidence that every Osu Uyana should resemble a homegarden. It demonstrates that medicinal cultivation can be embedded within multifunctional household systems where medicine, food, shade, biodiversity, income, labour, and environmental regulation meet. For Anavaratha, this makes the household garden an important comparative form rather than a secondary or informal version of an institutional garden.

## 6. Ecogeographical condition principle

The 2026 ecogeographical-mapping study frames medicinal-plant conservation, cultivation, and sustainable use through ecological distribution and environmental requirements [2]. Its accessible article page is a preview and states that no datasets were generated or analysed in the study, so it should be used here as a methodological and conceptual source rather than as a downloadable garden-level dataset.

The principle for Anavaratha is therefore:

> **Ecological mapping can orient a garden inquiry, but local soil, water, shade, living relationships, stewardship, and seasonal evidence must determine what the map cannot decide.**

## 7. What cannot yet be concluded

The current evidence does not establish a universal Osu Uyana design, a national medicinal-garden species mix, a list of plants suitable for every ecological zone, or a direct link between Ganewatta and the Sinhala term Osu Uyana. It also does not establish medicinal efficacy, safe use, or propagation requirements for individual plants.

The next condition-level research should therefore compare one official garden, one SLDAC Osu Uyana site, and one community, school, or household form using the same fields: soil, water, shade, living community, seasonal rhythm, propagation, knowledge, labour, authority, and care.
