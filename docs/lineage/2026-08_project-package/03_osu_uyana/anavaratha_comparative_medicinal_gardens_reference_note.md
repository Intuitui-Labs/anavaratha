# Anavaratha

## Medicinal gardens across cultures

**Osu Uyana and the global ecology of healing gardens — Comparative reference note, Version 0.1**

## 1. The short answer

Yes. Many countries and cultures have created places where medicinal plants are grown, studied, interpreted, conserved, exchanged, or used. But they do not all represent the same institution or historical tradition.

Some were household or village gardens. Some were royal or monastic landscapes. Some were physic gardens attached to medical teaching. Some became botanical research institutions. Others are modern public gardens, conservation collections, ethnobotanical gardens, school gardens, or community learning spaces.

The important comparison is therefore not “Which country invented the medicinal garden?” It is:

> **How has each society connected living plants with healing, knowledge, place, care, access, and continuity?**

## 2. Sri Lanka as the starting point

A 2026 peer-reviewed history of Sri Lanka’s National Botanic Gardens states that early managed plant landscapes—including royal, monastic, and home gardens—are recorded in ancient chronicles and archaeological evidence from the Anuradhapura and Polonnaruwa periods. It describes these landscapes as combining aesthetic, cultural, and utilitarian functions, and traces the later development of formal botanical gardens and modern scientific institutions [1].

Sri Lanka also has contemporary medicinal-garden infrastructure. The official Department of National Botanic Gardens describes the Ganewatta Medicinal Plant Garden as a 21.04-hectare garden in the Intermediate Zone with more than 300 medicinal plant species, including native and endemic plants. It carries out ex situ conservation, maintains nurseries and seed preservation, demonstrates systematic cultivation, and provides educational and research opportunities [2].

This supports a careful conclusion: **Sri Lanka has a long history of managed plant landscapes and a modern medicinal-garden tradition.** It does not yet prove that the exact contemporary expression “Osu Uyana” was the formal name of an ancient institution. That linguistic and historical question should remain open to further verification.

## 4. Major models

### 4.1 The living pharmacy

The earliest and most intuitive model is the garden as a source of medicinal plants for direct use. This can occur at household, village, monastery, hospital, or practitioner level. Its strength is proximity: the plant is known in relation to body, season, place, and practice. Its risk is that knowledge may be lost, commercialised, or used unsafely when the social context is removed.

### 4.2 The physic garden

European physic gardens, including Padua, Oxford, and Chelsea, developed around the study and cultivation of medicinal plants. Padua was established in 1545 to help university students study medicinal plants alive, while Oxford was founded as a physic garden in 1621 [5] [6]. Chelsea was founded in 1673 by the Worshipful Society of Apothecaries [7].

These gardens show a transition from medicinal cultivation toward taxonomy, medical education, pharmacy, scientific research, seed exchange, public interpretation, and biodiversity conservation. They also show that classification systems change. A garden can preserve continuity while its intellectual framework evolves.

### 4.3 The medical-classification garden

The UC Botanical Garden at Berkeley’s Chinese Medicinal Herb Garden organises living plants by traditional Chinese medicine function groups and labels them with scientific name, Chinese medicinal name, transliteration, plant part, symptoms treated, and functional category [4]. This does not make the garden a comprehensive representation of Chinese medicine. It makes it an interpretive translation of a medical knowledge system into living space.

This model is particularly relevant to Anavaratha because it suggests that the garden’s design can express relationships and categories rather than simply display species. However, medicinal claims must be bounded, and visitors should not be encouraged to harvest or self-medicate.

### 4.4 The conservation and cultivation garden

Ganewatta in Sri Lanka and the Indian National Medicinal Plants Board represent a model in which medicinal plants are connected to conservation, cultivation, seed, health, livelihoods, and national coordination [2] [3]. This model is closer to Anavaratha’s regenerative direction because it treats plants as living resources requiring continuity, not merely as ingredients.

Yet the conservation garden must still ask whether ex situ cultivation supports or distracts from in situ habitat protection. A plant collection is not the same as a functioning ecosystem.

### 4.5 The cultural-interpretation garden

SANBI’s Free State Medicinal Garden uses a Basotho hut and a traditional Afrikaner home to culturally contextualise South African traditional medicine. It displays dried plants and selects plants partly on the basis of common use or rarity [9]. The garden also clearly states that its labels do not guarantee the safety or effectiveness of the treatments described.

This offers two important lessons: cultural setting is part of plant meaning, and public interpretation must communicate uncertainty and risk. The garden should not turn living cultural knowledge into decorative scenery.

### 4.6 The ethnobotanical and community-learning garden

The University of Kansas Native Medicinal Plant Garden combines native plants, historical medicinal use, food, field research, interpretive signs, pollinators, student beekeeping, and a community garden [10]. It demonstrates that a medicinal garden can be an ecological learning network in which human health is one relationship among many.

For Anavaratha, this is a powerful model because it connects medicinal plants to native biodiversity, insects, food, research, and participation without reducing the garden to a pharmacy.

## 5. What is common across the examples

| Shared pattern | Meaning for Anavaratha |
|---|---|
| **Living plants are more than specimens** | They carry use, story, habitat, season, and responsibility |
| **Classification shapes experience** | Beds, labels, paths, and groupings teach a worldview |
| **Gardens are knowledge infrastructures** | They preserve names, uses, cultivation, and relationships |
| **Conservation and use are entangled** | A medicinal plant may be culturally important and threatened at the same time |
| **Seed and propagation matter** | Continuity depends on living material and practical care |
| **Public interpretation needs limits** | Cultural use is not automatically proof of safety or efficacy |
| **Governance determines meaning** | Household, community, state, university, and commercial models distribute authority differently |
| **The wider landscape remains essential** | A garden should not substitute for forest, wetland, soil, watershed, or community context |

## 6. What differs across cultures

The examples differ in who controls the garden, who may enter, who may harvest, who may teach, how knowledge is classified, whether plants are wild or cultivated, whether the garden is in situ or ex situ, and whether the primary purpose is healing, research, conservation, education, livelihood, ritual, or public memory.

These differences matter for Anavaratha. It should not copy the geometry of a European physic garden, the functional taxonomy of a Chinese interpretation garden, the national coordination model of India, or the cultural architecture of a South African garden without asking whether the model fits Sri Lankan ecology, history, language, institutions, and knowledge rights.

## 7. What Anavaratha can learn

The comparative study suggests that Anavaratha’s medicinal-garden idea should be understood as a **living knowledge ecology** with seven connected layers:

| Layer | Anavaratha interpretation |
|---|---|
| **Living collection** | Medicinal and food plants, structural trees, soil life, pollinators, and associated beings |
| **Healing relation** | Documented Ayurvedic, indigenous, traditional, or research relation, carefully bounded |
| **Ecological relation** | Shade, soil, water, habitat, pollination, dispersal, season, and regeneration |
| **Knowledge relation** | Sinhala, Tamil, and scientific names; practitioners; stories; methods; transmission |
| **Conservation relation** | Native, endemic, threatened, seed, propagation, in situ, and ex situ dimensions |
| **Public relation** | Learning, access, interpretation, safety, participation, and responsibility |
| **Institutional relation** | Herbarium, botanical garden, university, government, community, and knowledge authority |

## 8. Proposed definition for Anavaratha

> **Anavaratha’s Osu Uyana is not simply a garden of medicinal plants. It is a Sri Lankan living knowledge ecology in which plants, people, habitats, healing traditions, biodiversity, cultivation, and continuity are made visible and cared for together.**

This definition keeps the garden connected to the wider regenerative-cultivation framework. It also avoids claiming that all medicinal knowledge is public, that all plants should be harvested, or that a garden can replace the ecosystems from which knowledge and life emerge.

## 9. Questions for further research

The next comparative and Sri Lankan research should ask which historical Sri Lankan gardens were medicinal in function; how the term **Osu Uyana** is used across Sinhala, Tamil, Ayurvedic, indigenous, institutional, and community contexts; how Ganewatta and other medicinal gardens govern collections and seed; which plants are cultivated versus wild-collected; how knowledge is transmitted; and how medicinal gardens connect to forests, homegardens, wetlands, farms, and watersheds.

It should also ask a more fundamental question:

> **Is the future Osu Uyana a bounded garden, a network of gardens, or a way of relating living beings across Sri Lanka?**
