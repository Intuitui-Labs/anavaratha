# Anavaratha

## Osu Uyana Living-System Inquiry

**Stewardship, knowledge, and ethics — Version 0.1**

## 1. Purpose

A medicinal garden is sustained not only by soil and water but by people, institutions, memory, labour, rules, and decisions about what may be shared or harvested. This document treats stewardship as part of the ecological system rather than as an administrative layer added afterwards.

## 2. Stewardship is distributed

The documented garden forms suggest that responsibility may be distributed across several kinds of actors. Ganewatta is officially operated under the Department of National Botanic Gardens and combines conservation, cultivation, education, research, propagation, seed preservation, and public access [1]. SLDAC material describes a network of herbal gardens associated with medicinal production and cultivation [2]. Community and school garden leads suggest smaller spaces in which local participation, education, and maintenance may be central [3].

| Stewardship actor | Possible responsibility | Question to verify |
|---|---|---|
| **Department of National Botanic Gardens** | Garden management, collections, ex situ conservation, research, nurseries, education | Which decisions are centralised, and which are made at garden level? |
| **Sri Lanka Ayurvedic Drugs Corporation** | Herbal-garden cultivation, medicinal raw materials, production, and garden administration | How are plant identity, harvest, seed, and supply-chain decisions governed? |
| **Ayurveda and traditional practitioners** | Medicinal knowledge, plant selection, preparation traditions, and teaching | What knowledge is public, restricted, lineage-based, or unsafe to publish? |
| **Botanists and herbarium staff** | Taxonomic authentication, vouchers, nomenclature, and documentation | How are local names and practitioner identifications reconciled? |
| **Schools and community groups** | Demonstration, care, education, local observation, and continuity | Who owns the garden, who maintains it, and what happens when funding or leadership changes? |
| **Households** | Daily cultivation, food and medicinal use, seed saving, care, and informal teaching | How are labour, land, water, and knowledge distributed within households? |
| **DWC, Forest Department, and CEA** | Protected-area, forest, wetland, habitat, permit, and environmental governance | Which agency has authority over a particular place, species, or activity? |
| **Researchers and public visitors** | Documentation, learning, and interpretation | What permissions, attribution, reciprocity, and data restrictions apply? |

## 3. Knowledge is plural

The World Bank’s 2004 Sri Lankan Indigenous Knowledge brief describes Ayurveda and traditional healthcare as long-used systems and notes that medicinal plants include endemic species becoming increasingly rare [4]. This is useful historical context, but it is an older brief and should not be used as a current species total or as proof that all traditional knowledge is uniform.

The 2025 checklist itself reports a survey involving government Ayurveda and traditional practitioners, herbal collectors, and Ayurveda officers, showing that the source base includes both compiled botanical information and practitioner-oriented knowledge [5]. The article’s existence does not mean that every piece of knowledge gathered through such work is freely reusable by a future atlas.

For Anavaratha, knowledge should be recorded in layers:

| Knowledge layer | Appropriate treatment |
|---|---|
| **Public botanical information** | Cite, link, and preserve taxonomic source and date |
| **Institutional garden information** | Attribute institution, record access status, and do not imply unrestricted reuse |
| **Local-language naming** | Preserve Sinhala wording and transliteration; do not erase local distinctions through translation |
| **Practitioner knowledge** | Record authority, context, consent, and any restriction before publication |
| **Household or community practice** | Attribute the community and describe reciprocity, not just extract a plant-use fact |
| **Sacred, secret, or commercially sensitive knowledge** | Withhold or generalise unless the knowledge holders authorise a defined use |
| **Anavaratha interpretation** | Label as interpretation rather than source fact |

## 4. Ethical decision gates

### 4.1 Before recording

The researcher should ask who produced the knowledge, who has authority to speak for it, whether the source is public or restricted, whether translation changes meaning, and whether recording could increase extraction, commercial pressure, or unwanted attention.

### 4.2 Before linking

A relationship should not be created merely because two records appear in the same garden. The atlas should distinguish plant presence from medicinal use, medicinal use from therapeutic efficacy, and cultivation from sustainable harvest. It should also distinguish a garden manager’s institutional description from a community’s own account of the garden.

### 4.3 Before sharing

The atlas should check whether names, coordinates, photographs, plant lists, seed information, or practitioner statements could expose sensitive populations, enable unauthorised collection, or remove knowledge from its original context. Generalisation, restricted access, delayed publication, or non-public recording may be more ethical than full disclosure.

### 4.4 Before action

Any future field visit, specimen collection, protected-area research, or monitoring must pass the relevant institutional permission process. The DWC’s research-permission pathway is therefore a governance condition, not an administrative afterthought [6]. A medicinal-garden action may also require garden-manager approval, community consent, practitioner involvement, and a clear plan for attribution and benefit.

## 5. Stewardship capacities

The Osu Uyana inquiry should assess stewardship as a set of capacities rather than simply asking who owns the land.

| Capacity | What it includes |
|---|---|
| **Identity capacity** | Correct naming, vouchers, local names, and recognition of uncertainty |
| **Ecological capacity** | Understanding soil, water, shade, pests, pollinators, disease, and seasonal change |
| **Propagation capacity** | Seed provenance, nursery care, mother plants, genetic diversity, and distribution |
| **Knowledge capacity** | Teaching, language, memory, practitioner authority, and responsible documentation |
| **Care capacity** | Labour, tools, water, time, maintenance, monitoring, and succession of caretakers |
| **Governance capacity** | Rules, accountability, permissions, conflict resolution, and institutional continuity |
| **Reciprocity capacity** | Benefits, credit, access, training, compensation, and community control over use |
| **Adaptive capacity** | Revising practices when evidence, climate, seasons, or relationships change |

## 6. The garden as a commons of responsibility—not automatically of unrestricted access

An Osu Uyana may support shared learning and public benefit, but “shared” must not be interpreted as “open for anyone to collect, copy, commercialise, or publish.” A living garden can be public in one sense and protected in another. Its seeds, local names, medicinal knowledge, rare species, labour, and institutional records may each have different access conditions.

> **A garden can invite learning without surrendering the authority of those who sustain it.**

## 7. Ethical record fields

Every Osu Uyana record should contain:

| Field | Required question |
|---|---|
| **Knowledge holder** | Who produced, maintains, or authorises this knowledge? |
| **Language** | What language carries the original meaning? |
| **Consent** | Was recording or reuse authorised, and for what purpose? |
| **Attribution** | How should people, communities, institutions, and sources be credited? |
| **Access** | Is the information public, restricted, sensitive, or withheld? |
| **Reciprocity** | What does the source community or institution receive in return? |
| **Sensitivity** | Could publication cause ecological, cultural, commercial, or personal harm? |
| **Authority** | Which institution or community governs the relevant decision? |
| **Uncertainty** | What is unknown, disputed, translated, inferred, or time-limited? |
| **Revision** | Who can correct, update, or withdraw the record? |

## 8. Working stewardship principle

The Osu Uyana inquiry should not ask only, “What plants are in the garden?” It should also ask:

> **Who keeps the garden alive, who keeps the knowledge alive, who bears the labour, who holds authority, who benefits, and what must remain protected?**

These questions belong inside the ecological record because without them a medicinal garden can be biologically maintained while its knowledge, care relationships, and justice conditions are weakened.
