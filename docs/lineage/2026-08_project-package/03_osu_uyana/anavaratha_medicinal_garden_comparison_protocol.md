# Anavaratha

## Comparative medicinal-garden research protocol

**Osu Uyana and related traditions — Working protocol, Version 0.1**

## 1. Purpose

This protocol guides a comparative study of medicinal gardens in Sri Lanka and other countries. The purpose is not to rank traditions or search for one universal model. It is to understand how different societies have organised plants, healing, ecological care, education, knowledge, and access.

## 2. What counts as a medicinal garden

A medicinal garden is a deliberately maintained place where plants associated with healing, health, bodily care, food, ritual, research, education, or therapeutic practice are cultivated, conserved, interpreted, exchanged, or used. The term may describe very different forms, including a household garden, a temple or monastery garden, a royal garden, a hospital garden, a university collection, a botanical garden, a community garden, or a conservation nursery.

A garden should not be classified solely by the presence of medicinal plants. Its purpose, governance, knowledge system, access, ecological practice, and relationship to living communities must also be recorded.

## 3. Comparison fields

| Field | Questions |
|---|---|
| **Name and language** | What is the local term? What does it literally and culturally mean? |
| **Origin** | When and why did the garden or tradition emerge? |
| **Place** | What ecological region, settlement, institution, or landscape contains it? |
| **Purpose** | Healing, cultivation, conservation, education, research, ritual, livelihood, or multiple purposes? |
| **Plant knowledge** | Who identifies, grows, uses, teaches, or protects the plants? |
| **Governance** | Household, community, temple, monastery, royal, state, university, hospital, NGO, or mixed? |
| **Access** | Who can enter, learn, harvest, receive care, or make decisions? |
| **Ecology** | Does it conserve species, seeds, soil, water, habitat, pollinators, or genetic diversity? |
| **Continuity** | How are plants, skills, names, recipes, stories, and responsibilities transmitted? |
| **Ethics** | How are knowledge rights, consent, benefit-sharing, sacredness, and sensitive information handled? |
| **Evidence** | What primary records, archaeological sources, institutional pages, studies, or living practitioners support the account? |
| **Current form** | Does the tradition continue, survive as a heritage reference, or exist only as a modern reconstruction? |

## 4. Historical evidence levels

| Level | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Primary historical evidence** | Garden plan, manuscript, inscription, herbarium, archaeological evidence, institutional record, or contemporary account |
| **Scholarly reconstruction** | Peer-reviewed historical or ethnobotanical interpretation based on sources |
| **Living continuity** | Current practice documented with appropriate community or practitioner engagement |
| **Institutional continuity** | Current garden or programme maintained by a recognised institution |
| **Modern analogy** | A contemporary garden inspired by an older tradition but not demonstrably continuous with it |
| **Unverified association** | A term, story, or claim requiring further confirmation |

## 5. Comparison discipline

Historical gardens should not be romanticised as automatically sustainable or egalitarian. Modern gardens should not be treated as ancient traditions merely because they use old plant names. A medicinal garden may conserve plants while excluding the communities whose knowledge created its meaning. A community garden may hold deep knowledge without having formal institutional recognition.

The comparison should preserve differences in medical theory, religion, ecology, social structure, colonial history, property, access, and knowledge ownership.

## 6. Relevance to Anavaratha

The comparison should ask what each tradition teaches Anavaratha about:

| Question | Relevance |
|---|---|
| How is a plant collection made meaningful? | Connects species to story, use, place, and responsibility |
| How are living collections maintained? | Connects cultivation to seed, soil, water, labour, and continuity |
| Who has authority over knowledge? | Prevents extraction and unsupported repackaging |
| How does a garden relate to its wider landscape? | Prevents the garden becoming an isolated display |
| How are health and ecology connected? | Links medicinal plants to habitat and human well-being |
| How is access organised? | Clarifies whether the garden is private, educational, communal, or public |
| How does the garden change over time? | Makes succession, adaptation, loss, and renewal visible |

> **The comparison is valuable when it reveals relationships and responsibilities, not when it produces a list of famous gardens.**
