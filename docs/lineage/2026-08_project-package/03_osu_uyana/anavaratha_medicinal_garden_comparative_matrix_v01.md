# Anavaratha

## Comparative medicinal-garden matrix

**Osu Uyana and related traditions — Working comparison, Version 0.1**

## 1. Scope

This matrix compares documented examples and traditions as different models rather than as interchangeable versions of one universal medicinal garden. It includes Sri Lankan managed plant landscapes and contemporary medicinal gardens; Indian national coordination; Chinese medicinal interpretation; European physic gardens; Islamic botanical and healing scholarship; a South African medicinal garden; and a Native medicinal-plant research garden in the United States.

## 2. Comparative matrix

| Model | Primary purpose | How plants are organised | Governance | Ecological dimension | Main lesson for Anavaratha |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Sri Lankan royal, monastic, and home gardens** | Cultural, aesthetic, utilitarian, and managed-landscape functions | Place, use, cultivation, and cultural setting | Historical royal, monastic, household, and local forms | Managed plant landscapes connected to place and everyday life | A medicinal garden may be part of a wider living landscape rather than a specialised institution |
| **Ganewatta medicinal garden** | Medicinal collection, ex situ conservation, cultivation, education, research, and indigenous-medicine context | Medicinal species and systematic cultivation | Department of National Botanic Gardens | Native/endemic collection, nursery propagation, seed preservation, herbarium, and ex situ conservation | A garden can join living collection, conservation, propagation, research, and public learning |
| **India — National Medicinal Plants Board** | National coordination of medicinal-plant conservation, cultivation, trade, export, health, and livelihoods | Policy and programme categories | Government board within Ministry of AYUSH | Conservation and cultivation linked to livelihoods and health security | The garden can be understood within a larger policy and livelihood system |
| **China — UC Berkeley interpretive garden** | Living interpretation and public education in traditional Chinese medicine | Traditional functional categories of medicine; labelled beds | University botanical garden | Living collection and public learning; not a comprehensive conservation garden | A medicinal system can shape the spatial and interpretive design of a garden |
| **Padua botanical garden** | Medical education, plant observation, botany, research, and biodiversity conservation | Historic *Hortus Simplicium* plus modern scientific collections | University and heritage institution | Living collection moved from medicinal teaching toward research and conservation | A medicinal garden can evolve into a scientific and conservation institution |

| **Native medicinal-plant research garden, University of Kansas** | Native-plant education, ethnobotany, field research, food, medicine, pollinators, and community gardening | Demonstration beds, research rows, interpretive signs | University, beekeeping club, students, staff, faculty, community gardeners | Native plants, bees, prairie context, research, and food cultivation | A medicinal garden can be a multispecies ecological learning system, not only a remedy collection |

## 3. Functional comparison

| Function | Sri Lankan relevance | International parallels | Anavaratha question |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Healing** | Ayurveda, indigenous medicine, medicinal plants, practitioner knowledge | Chinese medicine, European physic gardens, Indian AYUSH | How can use be documented without claiming efficacy or appropriating knowledge? |
| **Conservation** | Native/endemic plants, threatened species, ex situ collections, seed preservation | Ganewatta, Padua, SANBI, university gardens | Which plants need in situ protection, ex situ cultivation, or both? |
| **Education** | Schools, community gardens, botanical institutions, medicinal-garden interpretation | Berkeley, Oxford, SANBI, Kansas | What should a visitor learn about plant, habitat, relationship, risk, and care? |
| **Research** | Herbarium, universities, Ayurveda, botanical and ecological studies | Padua, Oxford, Chelsea, Kansas | How can garden records connect to rigorous ecological research? |
| **Food and livelihood** | Homegardens, medicinal plants, rural and household use | India, Kansas, Sri Lankan homegardens | How are health, nutrition, livelihoods, seed, and ecological care related? |
| **Seed and continuity** | Seed preservation, propagation, garden transmission | Chelsea seed exchange, Ganewatta nursery, homegardens | How does the garden maintain living continuity across generations? |
| **Place and habitat** | Dry, Intermediate, Wet zones; forest, wetland, homegarden, and local landscapes | Islamic ecological classification, native-plant gardens, Padua collections | How does a garden remain connected to local ecological fit rather than becoming a generic display? |

## 4. Historical continuity versus modern analogy

The evidence supports a careful distinction. Sri Lanka has scholarly evidence for managed royal, monastic, and home gardens in ancient periods, and it has modern institutional medicinal gardens such as Ganewatta. The available evidence does not yet establish that the exact contemporary expression **Osu Uyana** was used as a named institution in those ancient periods.

Similarly, modern gardens in other countries may be inspired by older medical traditions without being direct continuations of an ancient garden. Padua and Chelsea provide unusually well-documented institutional continuities, while the Islamic comparison is stronger as a botanical and healing knowledge tradition than as evidence for one standard “Islamic medicinal garden.”

## 5. Design principles for Anavaratha

The comparison suggests that Anavaratha should consider a medicinal garden as a **living knowledge ecology** with several connected layers:

| Layer | Content |
|---|---|
| **Living collection** | Plants, trees, fungi, pollinators, soil life, and associated beings |
| **Medical relation** | Documented traditional, Ayurvedic, indigenous, or modern research use, with claims bounded |
| **Ecological relation** | Soil, water, shade, habitat, pollination, dispersal, season, and regeneration |
| **Knowledge relation** | Names, languages, practitioners, stories, methods, permissions, and transmission |
| **Conservation relation** | Native/endemic status, threat, propagation, seed, in situ and ex situ care |
| **Public relation** | Access, education, interpretation, safety, participation, and responsibility |
| **Institutional relation** | Herbarium, botanical garden, university, government, community, and knowledge authority |

## 6. What Anavaratha should avoid

Anavaratha should avoid presenting one universal medicinal-plant system, claiming that ancient and modern gardens are the same, equating medicinal use with clinical proof, publishing sensitive knowledge or locations without permission, treating ex situ collections as substitutes for habitat protection, and selecting plants only because they are attractive, rare, famous, or commercially valuable.

## 7. Comparative conclusion

Yes, many countries have created medicinal-plant gardens, but they have done so through different institutional and cultural forms. Some began as living pharmacies or teaching grounds. Some became university laboratories, conservation collections, seed-exchange networks, cultural interpretation spaces, or community learning gardens. Others exist within wider household, monastic, agricultural, or knowledge landscapes.

The strongest common thread is not a particular geometry or plant list. It is the attempt to make relationships among **plants, health, place, knowledge, care, and continuity** visible and transmissible.

> **Anavaratha can learn from these examples without copying any one of them: preserve the living collection, reveal its relationships, respect its knowledge holders, and connect the garden to the wider Sri Lankan landscape.**
