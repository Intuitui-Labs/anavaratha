# Anavaratha

## Osu Uyana Living-System Model

**Synthesis for the regenerative ecological-cultivation branch — Version 0.1**

## 1. The central shift

The inquiry began with a simple translation: *Osu Uyana* means medicinal garden in Sinhala. It now leads to a more substantial proposition. An Osu Uyana should not be understood merely as a place where medicinal plants are collected or planted. It is a **living cultivation and knowledge system** whose continuity depends on ecological conditions, relationships among organisms, people, institutions, and time.

This does not establish one fixed meaning for every use of the phrase. Contemporary Sri Lankan sources point to multiple forms: official medicinal-plant collections, production and nursery systems, educational and research gardens, community or school plots, household cultivation, and historical managed landscapes [1] [2] [3].

> **An Osu Uyana is not defined only by what is planted. It is defined by the relationships and responsibilities that allow medicinal life and knowledge to continue.**

## 2. The seven-layer model

| Layer | Core concern | Evidence to connect |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Landscape** | Ecological region, cultural landscape, settlement, forest, wetland, coast, farm, or homegarden | Agro-ecological mapping, local geography, land-use history, institutional and community sources |
| **2. Habitat conditions** | Soil, water, shade, climate, drainage, moisture, disturbance, elevation, and microclimate | Soil and water records, garden observations, ecological studies, seasonal data |
| **3. Living community** | Medicinal plants, trees, pollinators, birds, seed dispersers, fungi, soil organisms, domestic animals, and people | Species records, relationship studies, biodiversity observations, local knowledge |
| **4. Medicinal knowledge** | Sinhala names, practitioner knowledge, household practice, Ayurveda, indigenous medicine, teaching, and memory | Language-specific sources, practitioner and community authority, consent, attribution |
| **5. Continuity systems** | Seeds, mother plants, nurseries, propagation, vouchers, herbarium records, exchange, and renewal | Garden records, seed and nursery documentation, National Herbarium and institutional sources |
| **6. Stewardship and governance** | Ownership, labour, institutional mandate, community authority, access, benefit, protection, and permits | Institutional documents, community accounts, governance records, DWC and garden permissions |
| **7. Time and adaptation** | Seasons, flowering, fruiting, harvest, succession, generational change, climate variability, and revision | Repeated observations, phenology, histories, version records, and adaptive monitoring |

The layers are interdependent. Soil and water shape plant survival; plant diversity shapes pollinator and bird relations; knowledge shapes what is valued and cultivated; stewardship shapes whether seeds and care continue; and seasonal change can alter both ecological and human labour.

## 3. Four forms of Osu Uyana

The evidence does not support a single universal Osu Uyana. It supports a family of related forms that should be compared without being collapsed.

| Form | Primary purpose | Characteristic capacity | Main risk if simplified |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Conservation garden** | Preserve medicinal diversity, rare taxa, seed, and living collections | Ex situ conservation, mother plants, seed room, vouchers, monitoring | A collection becomes disconnected from habitat and local authority |
| **Production garden** | Supply medicinal raw materials or plants | Cultivation, nursery, harvest, distribution, quality control | Commercial output overrides ecological limits or knowledge rights |
| **Learning garden** | Teach plant identity, medicine, ecology, and care | Demonstration, school or public education, interpretation | Knowledge is simplified or detached from its source communities |
| **Community or household garden** | Integrate medicine with food, shade, livelihood, culture, and daily care | Local adaptation, household labour, seed exchange, practical knowledge | Informal labour and knowledge are extracted without reciprocity |

A single garden may hold more than one form. Ganewatta, for example, is officially described through conservation, commercial cultivation, propagation, seed preservation, education, research, systematic cultivation, and bee-keeping [1]. The correct question is therefore not “Which type is it?” but “Which functions are present, who governs them, and how do they interact?”

## 4. The ecological logic of an Osu Uyana

The model can be expressed as a chain of conditions:

```text
Ecological region
      ↓
Soil, water, shade, climate, and disturbance
      ↓
Plant and seed continuity
      ↓
Pollinators, birds, insects, fungi, and soil life
      ↓
Medicinal knowledge, labour, and stewardship
      ↓
Seasonal care, conservation, learning, and responsible use
      ↺
Revision through observation and changing conditions
```

This is not a linear production pipeline. It is a living feedback system. A garden’s management can alter soil organisms and pollinators; a change in water availability can alter plant survival and labour; a change in knowledge governance can alter what is harvested or protected; and a seasonal relationship such as fruiting and frugivory can reveal conditions that a static plant list cannot show.

## 5. The human–more-than-human relationship

An Osu Uyana should represent humans as participants and stewards, not as the only users of the garden. The Yellow-eared Bulbul study at Horton Plains demonstrates the relevance of this wider view: an endemic and threatened bird was documented feeding on multiple fruiting plants, with seeds of nine plant species identified in faecal samples and relationships varying across monsoon and inter-monsoon seasons [4].

This does not mean every medicinal garden should reproduce Horton Plains. It means a garden inquiry should ask which other living beings are attracted, sheltered, fed, pollinated, or affected by cultivation, and whether a proposed care practice strengthens or weakens those relationships.

## 6. The knowledge–ecology boundary

The medicinal value of a plant and its ecological role are related but not identical claims. A checklist can document identity, local name, synonymy, and status without documenting habitat, propagation, therapeutic safety, or ecological function. A garden can preserve a plant ex situ without preserving all of the relationships that sustain it in situ.

| Claim | Must not be inferred from |
|---|---|
| Plant identity | A common name alone |
| Medicinal use | Checklist inclusion alone |
| Therapeutic efficacy or safety | Traditional use mention alone |
| Habitat suitability | Presence in another garden |
| Ecological function | Planting or collection alone |
| Sustainable harvest | Commercial cultivation alone |
| Knowledge permission | Public availability of a source |

## 7. The Anavaratha model of proportionate care

Care should emerge from the diagnosed condition rather than from a fixed preference for planting. The model proposes the following sequence:

| Condition | Possible proportionate care |
|---|---|
| Identity uncertain | Taxonomic verification, voucher, local-name clarification, or withholding |
| Population threatened | Protection, habitat care, seed conservation, or restricted access |
| Habitat damaged | Natural regeneration, soil or water repair, or reduced disturbance |
| Knowledge at risk | Consent-based teaching, attribution, language preservation, or restricted recording |
| Seed continuity weak | Provenance-controlled propagation or seed stewardship |
| Pollinator or bird relation weak | Habitat structure and seasonal food investigation |
| Garden labour unstable | Governance, training, succession, and reciprocal support |
| Evidence insufficient | Observation, source recovery, or deliberate non-intervention |

This is not an action plan. It is a reasoning structure for preventing disproportionate action.

## 8. Working definition for Release 01

> **For Anavaratha, an Osu Uyana is a locally situated medicinal cultivation and knowledge system in which plants, soil, water, other living beings, people, institutions, and seasonal practices are held in accountable relationship. Its form is plural, its boundaries are ecological and social, and its care must remain proportionate to evidence, authority, capacity, and risk.**

This definition is intentionally provisional. It should change when better Sinhala-language, practitioner, community, garden, ecological, or historical evidence becomes available.

## 9. What the model enables next

The model enables a comparative study of three or more actual Sri Lankan garden forms using one shared schema. The comparison should include Ganewatta, an SLDAC garden that uses Osu Uyana terminology, and a village, school, household, or community form. It should then connect each form to its soil, water, shade, living community, seasonality, knowledge authority, labour, governance, and care practices.

The model does not yet justify selecting a site or recommending species. Its immediate purpose is to make the right questions visible.
