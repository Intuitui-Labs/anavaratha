# Domain 01 — Sri Lanka’s ecological regions and biogeographic foundations

## 1. Definition and scope

This domain establishes the ecological base for all later Anavaratha research. It studies Sri Lanka as a geographically compact but highly varied island whose climatic, topographical, geological, soil, and hydrological differences produce distinct living systems. It includes climatic and agro-ecological zones, elevation, forests, wetlands, rivers, irrigation landscapes, coasts, marine environments, agricultural systems, and the distribution of species and ecological services.

The purpose is not to divide Sri Lanka into rigid categories. It is to understand how ecological differences overlap and how cultivation practices must be adapted to place.

## 2. What the evidence shows

The Convention on Biological Diversity country profile describes Sri Lanka’s biological diversity as strongly shaped by ecological, climatic, soil, and topographical variability. It identifies a wide range of forest types, including rainforests, mountain cloud forests, dry-zone monsoon forests, and arid thorn scrub forests, alongside agricultural, aquatic, marine, and coastal ecosystems [1]. It also identifies diverse wetlands, 103 major rivers, associated marshes, approximately 12,000 irrigation tanks, and significant agro-biodiversity shaped by long-term hydraulic systems and farmer selection [1].

The island’s ecological character is therefore not captured by a single national average. Rainfall, elevation, soils, water availability, seasonality, land use, and proximity to the sea create different conditions for regeneration. A cultivation practice that is ecologically appropriate in a wet-zone forest context may not be appropriate in a dry-zone landscape, an urban wetland, a highland catchment, or a coastal lagoon.

The same profile records high levels of endemism and historical decline in biodiversity and natural forest cover, although the figures cited are drawn from earlier national reporting and must be treated as dated evidence rather than current estimates [1]. This distinction is important for Anavaratha’s research discipline: baseline facts need a date, geographic scope, definition, and source context.

## 3. Living-system relationships

The island’s ecological regions should be studied as connected systems rather than isolated compartments. Elevation and rainfall influence vegetation; vegetation influences soil and water; watersheds connect uplands to lowlands, wetlands, and coasts; agricultural systems depend on seasonal and stored water; and settlements alter ecological relationships through land use and infrastructure.

Sri Lanka’s ecological map should therefore be read through flows and dependencies. A forest is also a water and soil system. An irrigation tank is also habitat, agricultural infrastructure, and part of a cultural landscape. A wetland is also flood regulation, biodiversity habitat, and a space of human use. A coast is connected to rivers, sediment, fisheries, mangroves, seagrass, and communities.

## 4. Sri Lankan research priorities

The next stage of research should assemble and compare maps and evidence on climatic zones, agro-ecological regions, elevation, geology and soils, watersheds, rivers and tanks, forest types, protected areas, wetlands, mangroves, agricultural landscapes, settlements, and land-use change. These layers should not be treated merely as visual overlays. Each should be accompanied by information on seasonality, ecological function, community dependence, governance, and uncertainty.

A priority is to identify where the boundaries of one system do not match the boundaries of another. Administrative boundaries may not follow watersheds. Forest boundaries may not follow wildlife movement. Coastal ecosystems may depend on upstream conditions. Community livelihood areas may overlap with conservation or restoration priorities. These mismatches are likely to be important future questions for Anavaratha.

## 5. Regenerative conditions

From this domain, the following conditions appear foundational for regenerative cultivation:

| Condition | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Place-specific ecological knowledge** | Cultivation must match climate, soil, water, elevation, species, and seasonality |
| **Watershed awareness** | Upland, lowland, wetland, agricultural, and coastal processes are connected |
| **Ecological diversity** | Different regions and habitats require different forms of care and restoration |
| **Historical awareness** | Present conditions are shaped by earlier land use, water systems, cultivation, and conservation decisions |
| **Scale awareness** | Site-level actions may produce landscape-level effects, and national patterns may hide local differences |
| **Data humility** | Older, incomplete, or differently defined data must not be presented as precise current truth |

## 6. Implications for Anavaratha

Anavaratha should not begin with one universal planting method or one national restoration aesthetic. It should develop a place-sensitive cultivation intelligence capable of asking what kind of living system is present, what relationships sustain it, what has changed, what is already regenerating, and what kind of care is proportionate.

The first domain establishes the project’s foundational position:

> **Sri Lanka is not one cultivation landscape. It is a set of connected and differentiated living landscapes.**

Any later field hypothesis should identify its ecological region, watershed or hydrological context, elevation and soil conditions, relevant species and habitats, communities and livelihoods, and the scale at which its effects may travel.

## 7. Open questions

The major open questions are how Sri Lanka’s ecological and agro-ecological classifications should be reconciled for Anavaratha’s purposes; which spatial datasets are current and accessible; how climate change is altering ecological boundaries and seasonal patterns; where natural regeneration is already occurring; how local and traditional classifications differ from official maps; and how ecological regions intersect with social and institutional boundaries.
