# Anavaratha

## Sri Lanka Eagle-Eye Research Framework

**Desk-based sense-making phase — Working version 0.1**

## 1. Purpose

Before Anavaratha chooses a field site, programme, partner, or intervention, it should first build a grounded understanding of Sri Lanka as a connected ecological and social system. The purpose of this phase is not to produce a single definitive map or to delay action indefinitely. It is to prevent premature action based on isolated images, fashionable solutions, incomplete data, or assumptions imported from elsewhere.

The central question is:

> **What conditions shape ecological harm and ecological possibility in Sri Lanka, and where might a proportionate form of regeneration eventually create meaningful leverage?**

This is an eagle-eye view, but not a distant or abstract one. It must retain enough resolution to show differences between places, populations, institutions, ecosystems, and time periods.

## 2. Research posture

The research should be rigorous without pretending to be neutral or complete. It should combine official data, academic research, civil-society knowledge, local and traditional knowledge, maps, histories, media, project documentation, and lived accounts. Each source should be identified by origin, date, scale, method, uncertainty, and possible bias.

Anavaratha should distinguish among three levels of statement:

| Level | Meaning | Example form |
|---|---|---|
| **Evidence** | A documented observation, measurement, policy, study, or testimony | “This source reports…” |
| **Interpretation** | A reasoned connection drawn across evidence | “Taken together, these sources suggest…” |
| **Hypothesis** | A proposition that requires further verification | “This may indicate…” |

The project should not convert national averages into local truths, treat tree numbers as ecological success, or assume that a visible problem has a single cause. The objective is to understand relationships, distributions, feedback, and consequences.

## 3. The seven research lenses

### Lens 1: Ecological systems

Map Sri Lanka’s major ecological zones, watersheds, rivers, wetlands, forests, mangroves, coastal systems, agricultural landscapes, urban ecosystems, and areas of ecological significance. Examine the relationships among soil, water, vegetation, wildlife, climate, land use, and human settlement.

### Lens 2: Waste and pollution flows

Trace material flows from production and consumption through collection, sorting, reuse, recycling, treatment, disposal, leakage, burning, dumping, waterways, and the sea. Distinguish solid, liquid, gaseous, chemical, biological, plastic, and noise or other forms of pollution where relevant. Identify where the flow is visible, where it becomes hidden, and where responsibility changes hands.

### Lens 3: Populations, exposure, and distribution

Identify who produces, handles, lives near, depends on, benefits from, or bears the risks of different environmental systems. Include residents, waste workers, farmers, fisher communities, estate communities, urban populations, children, women, older people, people with disabilities, animals, and other affected life. Study not only averages but unequal exposure, access, voice, and capacity.

### Lens 4: Institutions and governance

Map ministries, provincial bodies, local authorities, agencies, schools, universities, community groups, religious institutions, businesses, informal workers, civil-society organisations, and customary or local governance systems. Clarify mandates, overlaps, gaps, incentives, enforcement, funding, knowledge, and accountability.

### Lens 5: Space and scale

Examine the problem at nested scales: household, plot, street, institution, neighbourhood, town, local-authority area, watershed, district, province, island, region, and global material system. A site should never be analysed as though its boundaries are the same as the boundaries of its effects.

### Lens 6: Time and change

Study historical conditions, current pressures, seasonal variation, disaster events, economic cycles, policy shifts, migration, demographic change, climate trends, growth, abandonment, and intergenerational effects. Distinguish between immediate outputs, medium-term changes, and outcomes that may require decades.

### Lens 7: Existing intelligence and precedents

Document what Sri Lankan communities, researchers, public institutions, practitioners, and organisations are already doing. Identify existing restoration, waste, agroecology, biodiversity, circular-economy, seed, water, and education initiatives. The aim is to learn, connect, and avoid duplicating or displacing valuable work.

## 4. Core analytical matrix

Each major issue, system, or opportunity should be recorded through a common matrix.

| Question | What to examine |
|---|---|
| **What is happening?** | The observed environmental or social condition |
| **Where?** | The relevant site, zone, watershed, jurisdiction, or flow |
| **At what scale?** | Household, local, municipal, watershed, regional, national, or global |
| **When?** | Historical, seasonal, current, projected, or intergenerational time frame |
| **To whom?** | Populations, workers, communities, species, and institutions affected |
| **Who has capacity?** | Existing actors, knowledge, resources, authority, and relationships |
| **Who carries the burden?** | Exposure, labour, risk, cost, displacement, or maintenance burden |
| **What drives it?** | Material, economic, political, cultural, ecological, and behavioural causes |
| **What sustains it?** | Feedback loops, incentives, habits, infrastructure, narratives, or institutional gaps |
| **What could change it?** | Leverage points, constraints, dependencies, and possible unintended effects |
| **What is unknown?** | Missing data, contested claims, unmeasured populations, or unresolved uncertainty |

## 5. Proportionality framework

Proportionality should guide both research and future action. A proposed response should be assessed against the scale and character of the problem rather than chosen because it is visually attractive or easy to announce.

| Dimension of proportionality | Guiding question |
|---|---|
| **Ecological** | Is the intervention appropriate to the ecosystem and its carrying conditions? |
| **Spatial** | Does its geographic scale match the causes and effects being addressed? |
| **Temporal** | Is the time horizon long enough for the intended outcome? |
| **Social** | Are affected populations represented and protected from new burdens? |
| **Institutional** | Does the responsible actor have the mandate and capacity to maintain it? |
| **Material** | Does the solution reduce or reproduce resource and waste pressures? |
| **Financial** | Are the costs, benefits, and maintenance obligations transparent? |
| **Epistemic** | Is the confidence of the claim proportionate to the quality of the evidence? |
| **Political** | Does the intervention shift power, responsibility, or access in ways that require explicit attention? |

## 6. How the two sides enter the research

The waste side often enters through systems analysis. It asks where materials originate, how they move, where they accumulate, who handles them, why existing systems fail, and how burdens are distributed. It may require extensive desk research before any practical action can be responsibly proposed.

The cultivation side can enter through ecological possibility. It asks where regeneration is already occurring, what conditions allow it, which living systems can provide leverage, and how simple acts such as soil care, seed work, planting, water retention, or habitat protection might contribute. It must not be treated as exempt from rigour. A cultivation proposal requires equal attention to species, place, water, soil, land use, social impact, maintenance, and time.

These are not sequential departments. They are two ways of seeing the same landscape. Waste reveals obstruction and unequal burden. Cultivation reveals capacity and possibility. The research task is to understand where they intersect.

## 7. Evidence and source discipline

Every important claim should be entered into a source register containing the title, organisation or author, date, geographic scope, population scope, method, key finding, limitations, and relevance to Anavaratha. Sources should be grouped into policy, official statistics, academic research, maps and spatial data, civil-society reports, local knowledge, and project case studies.

When sources disagree, the disagreement should be documented rather than silently averaged away. Differences may result from different definitions, years, geographies, methods, or interests. Uncertainty is not a weakness of the research; hidden uncertainty is.

## 8. Outputs before field entry

The desk phase should produce a small set of working outputs rather than an unnecessarily encyclopaedic report:

| Output | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Sri Lanka ecological systems map** | Shows major ecosystems, watersheds, zones, pressures, and connections |
| **Pollution and material-flow map** | Shows where waste and pollutants originate, move, accumulate, and escape |
| **Population and burden map** | Shows affected groups, exposure, access, labour, benefits, and vulnerabilities |
| **Institutional map** | Shows responsibilities, capacities, overlaps, gaps, and existing initiatives |
| **Time-and-change timeline** | Shows historical shifts, seasonal variation, policy changes, and long-term pressures |
| **Initiative and knowledge inventory** | Shows existing work that Anavaratha can learn from or support |
| **Opportunity and uncertainty matrix** | Identifies possible leverage points while showing what remains unknown |
| **Research synthesis** | Explains relationships, contradictions, patterns, and hypotheses for later investigation |

## 9. Gate before field action

Anavaratha should not choose a pilot simply because a site is accessible or because a planting activity is easy to organise. Before entering the field, the desk phase should be able to answer, with appropriately stated confidence:

1. What larger system does the possible site belong to?
2. Which ecological and social conditions are relevant beyond the site boundary?
3. Who is affected, who has knowledge, who has authority, and who may carry new burdens?
4. What is already being done, and what would Anavaratha add rather than duplicate?
5. Why is the proposed entry point proportionate to the place and the evidence?
6. What would success, failure, unintended harm, and learning look like over time?
7. What information is still missing, and can field research be designed to resolve it?

Fieldwork should begin only when it is clear that it will answer meaningful questions, build relationships responsibly, and test a grounded hypothesis. The objective is not to postpone action forever. It is to ensure that action emerges from understanding.

## 10. Working principle

> **First see the whole landscape; then locate the leverage point.**

For Anavaratha, the eagle-eye view is not separate from cultivation. It is what allows cultivation to become proportionate, place-sensitive, socially accountable, and ecologically meaningful.

## 11. Provisional Sri Lanka evidence baseline

The first desk pass already indicates that Anavaratha should connect several existing knowledge systems rather than begin from a blank slate. Sri Lanka’s National Environmental Action Plan 2022–2030 provides a national policy frame for responsible consumption and production, including waste minimisation and resource efficiency [1]. A recent municipal solid-waste report indicates uneven service coverage, the continuing relevance of open dumping, and implementation challenges across national, provincial, and local-authority levels [2].

Biodiversity planning work has used spatial and socio-economic data to connect conservation priorities with development planning, ecosystem services, and national and sub-national implementation [3]. Sri Lanka’s Central Environmental Authority maintains pollution-regulatory and monitoring functions, including air-quality, emissions, noise, vibration, compliance, and incident-based monitoring [4]. Ecosystem-conservation work has also connected protected-area management, restoration, land-use planning, biodiversity, and community livelihoods [5].

These sources are not yet a complete account of Sri Lanka. They are starting points that show why the research must connect material flows, ecological systems, institutions, population distribution, spatial planning, and time. They also show the importance of documenting what is monitored, what is governed, what is unevenly served, and what remains uncertain.

### Current research gaps

The next desk pass should seek comparable and disaggregated information on solid-waste generation and collection by local-authority type; disposal, leakage, burning, and informal recovery; plastics and marine or river pathways; hazardous and electronic waste; air, water, soil, and noise pollution; ecological zones and watershed relationships; forest, wetland, mangrove, agricultural, and urban systems; population exposure and environmental labour; local institutional capacity; and the survival and maintenance of restoration projects.

It should also identify methodological gaps. Available sources may use different definitions of waste, pollution, forest, restoration, exposure, or success. National-scale figures may not be comparable with district, municipal, or site-level observations. These differences must be recorded as part of the sense-making process rather than hidden inside a single index.

## 12. Sense-making model: from landscape to leverage

The eagle-eye view should move through four analytical passes. The first pass describes the landscape: ecosystems, settlements, infrastructure, material flows, institutions, and populations. The second pass identifies pressures: pollution, extraction, degradation, waste, climate stress, exclusion, weak services, and other constraints on life. The third pass examines relationships: upstream and downstream effects, feedback loops, unequal burdens, institutional gaps, ecological dependencies, and interactions between human and non-human populations. The fourth pass considers leverage: where a proportionate intervention could alter a relationship, increase capacity, or enable existing positive processes.

| Pass | Main question | Output |
|---|---|---|
| **Describe** | What exists and how is it arranged? | Baseline landscape picture |
| **Diagnose** | What is causing harm or limiting capacity? | Pressure and burden map |
| **Relate** | How do systems, populations, institutions, and timescales interact? | Systems and feedback model |
| **Locate leverage** | Where could a proportionate action create meaningful change? | Prioritised hypotheses, not premature projects |

A leverage point should be judged by more than apparent simplicity. Anavaratha should ask whether it can reduce harm, create ecological capacity, build social capacity, connect scales, be maintained, reveal new knowledge, and avoid transferring burdens elsewhere. A small action is valuable when it is strategically connected to a wider system, not merely because it is small.

## 13. Research maturity levels

The desk phase can classify its understanding of each issue or opportunity according to research maturity.

| Level | Condition | Appropriate language |
|---|---|---|
| **Mapped** | The issue, place, actor, or flow has been identified | “We know this exists and where it appears.” |
| **Characterised** | Its scale, timing, affected populations, and institutional setting are reasonably described | “We have a working picture of how it behaves.” |
| **Connected** | Causes, feedbacks, dependencies, and unintended effects are understood across scales | “We can explain important relationships.” |
| **Testable** | A grounded leverage hypothesis and learning design can be stated | “We can responsibly design field research.” |

Field entry should normally require the relevant question to reach the **testable** level, while acknowledging that not every aspect of the national landscape will be equally understood. This prevents both premature intervention and the impossible demand for total knowledge.

## 14. The desk phase as a living cycle

The research should not be treated as a report that is written once and then closed. It should operate as a cycle of collecting, comparing, mapping, questioning, revising, and identifying uncertainty. New evidence may change the perceived importance of an issue, reveal that a local problem is connected to a different scale, or show that an apparently beneficial intervention could create a new burden.

> **See broadly. Trace relationships. State uncertainty. Locate leverage. Enter carefully. Learn continuously.**
