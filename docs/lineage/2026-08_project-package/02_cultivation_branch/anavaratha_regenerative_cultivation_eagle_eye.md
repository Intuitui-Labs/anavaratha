# Anavaratha

## Regenerative cultivation in Sri Lanka

**Eagle-eye desk research framework — Cultivation dimension only, Version 0.2**

> **Anavaratha studies and cultivates the conditions through which living systems can flourish, regenerate, and continue.**

## 1. Scope

This document focuses exclusively on Anavaratha’s **regenerative cultivation dimension** in Sri Lanka. Its subject is the positive creation of ecological capacity through forests, trees, soil, water, biodiversity, habitats, seed and food systems, ecological knowledge, community stewardship, and long-term care.

The project is not beginning with a field site or a predetermined intervention. It begins with an eagle-eye view of Sri Lanka’s living landscape: its ecological regions, watersheds, forests, wetlands, mangroves, coasts, agricultural systems, urban ecosystems, communities, livelihoods, institutions, knowledge traditions, histories, and future pressures.

The guiding question is:

> **What conditions allow living systems and the communities that care for them to regenerate in Sri Lanka, and where might careful cultivation create durable ecological capacity?**

## 2. What regenerative cultivation means

Regenerative cultivation is broader than planting trees. It means supporting the conditions through which ecological systems can become healthier, more diverse, more resilient, and more capable of continuing over time.

It may include forest restoration, natural regeneration, native and locally appropriate planting, agroforestry, soil renewal, water retention, watershed care, wetland and mangrove restoration, habitat creation, seed conservation, biodiversity corridors, ecological education, and community stewardship. The appropriate form will depend on the place.

The important distinction is between **adding greenery** and **cultivating living capacity**. A successful project is not defined only by the number of trees planted or the area covered. It is defined by whether the relationships among soil, water, plants, animals, people, and institutions become more capable of sustaining life.

## 3. The positive direction

As a strictly bounded analogy, Anavaratha’s cultivation dimension can be associated with the positive direction of freedom: not freedom as the removal of a barrier, but freedom as the development of the capacity to act, grow, participate, and realise possibilities.

In ecological terms, this means asking what allows a forest to establish, what allows soil to remain fertile, what allows water to circulate, what allows biodiversity to persist, and what allows communities to care for living systems over generations. The project is not attempting to reproduce Berlin’s political philosophy. It is using the idea of **capacity and flourishing** as a conceptual orientation for regenerative work.

> **The aim is not simply to protect life from harm, but to enable life to become more capable of renewal.**

## 4. The eagle-eye view

Before choosing a field intervention, Anavaratha should understand Sri Lanka through several connected layers. The eagle-eye view is not a distant generalisation. It must move between the island-wide pattern and the specific conditions of a place.

| Layer | Questions |
|---|---|
| **Ecological** | What ecosystems, species, soils, waters, habitats, and ecological processes are present? |
| **Spatial** | How are ecological regions, watersheds, corridors, settlements, farms, cities, coasts, and protected areas connected? |
| **Temporal** | How have these systems changed historically, seasonally, and across generations? |
| **Social** | Which communities, livelihoods, households, workers, and species depend on or care for these systems? |
| **Institutional** | Which public bodies, community groups, farmers, researchers, organisations, and knowledge holders shape the landscape? |
| **Cultural** | What local, Indigenous, agricultural, religious, and practical knowledge already exists? |
| **Material and biological** | How do water, nutrients, seeds, species, shade, soil organisms, and energy move through the system? |
| **Future-facing** | What pressures, opportunities, uncertainties, and thresholds may shape the next decades? |

The objective is to understand relationships rather than collect disconnected facts. A forest should be studied in relation to water, soil, species movement, land use, communities, and time. A community garden should be studied in relation to food, knowledge, care, access, seasonal cycles, and institutional support. A mangrove should be studied in relation to tides, sediment, fisheries, coastal protection, livelihoods, and natural regeneration.

## 5. Research lenses

### 5.1 Forests and trees

Study Sri Lanka’s forest types, native and endemic species, forest edges, fragmented habitats, reforestation histories, natural regeneration, canopy structure, understorey, wildlife movement, and relationships between forests and communities. The question is not merely where trees are absent, but what ecological conditions allow forest systems to return and persist.

### 5.2 Soil and water

Examine soil health, erosion, moisture, fertility, groundwater, streams, rivers, wetlands, water retention, seasonal flows, and watershed relationships. Cultivation should begin from an understanding of the physical conditions that make life possible. A planting programme that ignores water, soil, and seasonality risks creating an appearance of regeneration without regenerative function.

### 5.3 Biodiversity and habitat

Map species diversity, endemism, habitat connectivity, pollinators, birds, mammals, amphibians, aquatic life, seed diversity, and relationships among species. Biodiversity should be understood not only as a conservation category but as a source of resilience, ecological function, cultural value, food, medicine, and continuity.

### 5.4 Coastal, wetland, and mangrove systems

Study the distinctive conditions of mangroves, lagoons, estuaries, seagrass-linked systems, coastal forests, and wetlands. Sri Lanka’s mangrove-regeneration experience demonstrates the importance of moving from mass planting toward creating the conditions for natural regeneration, with science, local communities, and ecological balance at the centre [1].

### 5.5 Agricultural and agroecological systems

Examine farms, home gardens, agroforestry, seed systems, food cultures, traditional cultivation, farmer knowledge, land access, and relationships between agricultural productivity and ecological renewal. The project should ask how cultivation can support both biodiversity and human nourishment without reducing living systems to production inputs.

### 5.6 Urban and peri-urban ecosystems

Study urban trees, home gardens, school grounds, public landscapes, streams, wetlands, heat, shade, birds, pollinators, and community spaces. Urban cultivation can be a meaningful entry point, but it should be connected to larger ecological functions and local stewardship rather than treated as isolated beautification.

### 5.7 Communities and stewardship

Understand who cares for land and water, who holds ecological knowledge, who depends on particular species or landscapes, and how responsibilities are shared. Restoration must be socially situated. Jadav Payeng’s forest-restoration story is inspiring because it demonstrates long-term personal commitment, but it also shows that ecological gains can create new burdens for nearby communities if animal movement, livelihoods, and communication are not considered [2].

## 6. Sri Lankan evidence base

Sri Lanka already has important examples of landscape-scale ecological thinking. A Convention on Biological Diversity project integrated spatial and socio-economic data to identify biodiversity priorities in relation to national and sub-national development planning, including the distribution of biodiversity, irreplaceable sites, and ecosystem services [3]. This supports an evidence model in which ecological value is studied together with social and spatial relationships.

The World Bank’s account of Sri Lanka’s ecosystem-conservation work connects biodiversity protection with land-use planning, forest management, restoration, community engagement, and local livelihood opportunities [4]. This is relevant to Anavaratha because regenerative cultivation cannot be separated from the people and institutions that maintain it.

The UN Decade on Ecosystem Restoration identifies Sri Lanka’s mangrove regeneration initiative as an example of long-term restoration that shifted from planting alone toward nurturing the conditions for natural recovery [1]. This is close to Anavaratha’s central intuition: the task is to create the conditions in which life can continue with increasing strength.

These sources are starting points, not a complete national diagnosis. The desk phase should also identify differences between ecological zones, seasonal conditions, land-use histories, community experiences, and institutional capacities.

## 7. Inspiration lineage

### _The Man Who Planted Trees_

Frédéric Back’s 1987 animated film, based on Jean Giono’s story, contributes an ethic of patience, repetition, and cumulative care [5]. Its importance is not simply the act of planting. It is the belief that quiet work sustained over time can transform an entire landscape.

### Jadav Payeng

Jadav Payeng’s real-life forest-restoration work in Assam offers a parallel example of persistence and long-duration attention. Unlike the film, it is not a fictional story, and the two should not be conflated. Payeng’s example also provides a necessary lesson in social complexity: restoration must account for people, animals, livelihoods, and institutions around the restored landscape [2].

### Biomimicry

The Biomimicry Institute describes the living world as a source of models, forms, processes, and ecosystems from which humans can learn [6]. For Anavaratha, biomimicry is a method of asking how living systems retain water, build fertility, cooperate, adapt, diversify, recover, and create conditions for other life.

### Vandana Shiva and Navdanya

Navdanya contributes an ecological and ethical emphasis on biological and cultural diversity, living soil, living food, agroecology, seed sovereignty, and Earth-centred relationships [7]. This helps Anavaratha understand regeneration as a question of knowledge, commons, autonomy, and care—not only land cover.

### Ecosia

Ecosia contributes the idea of distributed ecological leverage. Its model connects ordinary repeated activity with funding for climate action and tree planting [8]. For Anavaratha, the useful question is how simple actions, repeated by many people, can build ecological knowledge, participation, resources, or long-term stewardship in Sri Lanka.

### _Adaptation._ (2002)

Charlie Kaufman’s film **_Adaptation._ (2002)**, directed by Spike Jonze and beginning from Susan Orlean’s _The Orchid Thief_, contributes a methodological caution rather than ecological evidence [9]. It concerns the difficulty of representing a complex living subject through a constructed form.

For Anavaratha, this means that the eagle-eye framework and living-sector atlas must remain provisional. A tree, medicinal plant, pollinator, endangered animal, or soil organism cannot be fully represented by one label such as native, useful, rare, medicinal, or ecosystem-friendly. Each record must remain connected to habitat, season, other beings, people, knowledge, and uncertainty.

The project should therefore record not only what it knows, but also why a being was selected, which sources support the record, what has been omitted, where classifications conflict, and how the record may change. The atlas is not a final possession of the living world. It is an adaptive instrument that changes as new ecological evidence, local knowledge, seasons, relationships, and stewardship conditions become visible.

> **The map is not the living world, but the map changes the conditions under which the living world is seen.**

This gives the cultivation research a reflexive dimension: Anavaratha must examine how its own categories influence attention, value, protection, cultivation, and care. The project should not abandon structure; it should make structure transparent, revisable, and accountable to what it cannot contain.

## 8. Conditions before intervention

The desk phase should not attempt to decide immediately which place to plant or restore. It should first establish a working understanding of the conditions that make cultivation appropriate. In the spirit of _Adaptation._, this understanding should remain open to revision rather than becoming a premature claim of completeness.

| Condition | What must be understood |
|---|---|
| **Ecological fit** | Which species, habitats, soils, waters, and ecological processes belong to the place? |
| **Regenerative potential** | Is natural regeneration already occurring, and what is preventing or supporting it? |
| **Community relationship** | Who uses, lives with, depends on, or cares for the landscape? |
| **Stewardship capacity** | Who can maintain, observe, adapt, and protect the work over time? |
| **Temporal fit** | What seasonal and intergenerational horizon is required? |
| **Spatial fit** | Does the intervention belong to a site, corridor, watershed, farm system, or wider landscape? |
| **Unintended effects** | Could the intervention alter access, species movement, water, livelihoods, or local relationships? |
| **Learning value** | What would the intervention teach that the desk phase cannot establish alone? |

A small action may eventually be appropriate, but only when it is connected to a real ecological hypothesis and a responsible learning system. Simplicity is valuable when it creates leverage without pretending to solve more than it can.

## 9. Research maturity and field-entry threshold

The desk phase can classify its understanding of a possible cultivation opportunity in four levels.

| Level | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Mapped** | The ecosystem, place, actors, and broad conditions have been identified. |
| **Characterised** | Species, soil, water, communities, institutions, seasonality, and pressures are reasonably described. |
| **Connected** | Relationships, dependencies, feedbacks, benefits, burdens, and unintended effects are understood across scales. |
| **Testable** | A proportionate cultivation hypothesis and learning design can be stated responsibly. |

Field entry should begin only when the relevant opportunity has reached the **testable** level. This does not require total knowledge of Sri Lanka. It requires enough knowledge to avoid symbolic action, respect local conditions, identify who should be involved, and know what the intervention is meant to learn or enable.

## 10. Working principle

> **First understand the living landscape; then cultivate the conditions for regeneration.**

Anavaratha’s cultivation dimension is therefore not a tree-planting campaign in search of a landscape. It is a patient process of learning how landscapes live, how communities care, and where a proportionate act of cultivation might help life become more continuous, diverse, and self-renewing.

## 11. Next descent: the living sector

After understanding Sri Lanka’s landscapes, habitats, and regenerative conditions, Anavaratha descends into the living sector: trees, plants, animals, birds, insects, fungi, soil organisms, and the relationships among them. This is not a flat inventory of species. It is a relational inquiry into who lives where, what each being requires, what it enables, what depends on it, what cultural or historical meanings it carries, and what conditions allow its relationships to continue.

The living-sector descent includes structural trees and plants; pollinators and seed dispersers; soil life; endemic, endangered, and historically significant beings; medicinal and food plants; shade- and water-related organisms; attractor species; and the people and institutions that steward them. The analytical unit is the being-in-relationship-with-habitat, season, other beings, and community.

> **Anavaratha cultivates the conditions in which living beings can continue to meet one another.**

This descent produces a relationship atlas rather than a planting list. It links organism identity, habitat, status, ecological function, biocultural meaning, seasonal pattern, stewardship, evidence, and uncertainty. “Osu Uyana” is included as a reference for deeper investigation into medicinal-plant gardens and Sri Lankan knowledge systems, with its exact meanings and institutional contexts to be verified through appropriate sources and knowledge holders.

The next research movement is therefore: **landscape → habitat → living community → organism → relationship → condition → proportionate care**.
