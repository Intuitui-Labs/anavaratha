# Anavaratha

## Threatened-species, habitat, and protected-area crosswalk

**Sri Lanka regenerative-cultivation dimension — Working crosswalk, Version 0.1**

## 1. Purpose

This crosswalk connects threatened or endemic living beings with the habitats, corridors, protected areas, basins, institutions, and evidence systems that sustain them. It uses the Red Fin Labeo as an anchor case and establishes a reusable pattern for future species.

The core principle is:

> **A threatened organism should be crosswalked to the conditions of its survival before any intervention is proposed for the organism itself.**

## 4. Reusable threatened-species crosswalk

| Source field | Crosswalk destination | Interpretation rule |
|---|---|---|
| Scientific name | Taxonomic identity | Preserve source name and taxonomic version |
| Common/local names | Biocultural identity | Record language and source; do not silently standardise |
| Red List category | Conservation status | Record assessment date and authority |
| Distribution | Spatial range | Distinguish confirmed, historical, inferred, and incomplete range |
| Habitat | Habitat record | Do not infer habitat from range alone |
| HydroBASIN | Freshwater spatial layer | Use basin as a management context, not a substitute for local habitat |
| Protected-area overlap | Governance and habitat | Verify boundaries and current management status |
| Corridor relation | Connectivity | Require evidence of movement or a clearly stated hypothesis |
| Threats | Constraint record | Preserve source scope and separate direct from inferred causes |
| Recovery action | Care hypothesis | Treat project intention separately from demonstrated outcome |
| Community work | Stewardship record | Record participation, authority, benefits, and burden |
| Sensitive location | Access control | Generalise or withhold where disclosure could cause harm |

## 5. Protected-area crosswalk

The DWC describes protected areas as refuges for species, protectors of ecosystem services and cultural or historical areas, and reference sites for monitoring external change [3]. It also describes jungle corridors as a response to habitat fragmentation and a means of supporting animal movement between protected areas [3]. These claims can support the conceptual structure of the atlas, but the atlas must not treat a protected-area designation as proof of ecological integrity or a corridor designation as proof of successful movement.

| Protected-area element | Possible atlas relation | Required verification |
|---|---|---|
| National reserve or sanctuary | Habitat refuge and legal status | Current boundary, management plan, species use |
| Marine national park | Marine/coastal habitat | Current habitat, species, use, and permit context |
| Jungle corridor | Movement and connectivity | Species movement, barriers, land tenure, community context |
| Reference area | Baseline and monitoring | Comparable external sites and time series |
| Research permission | Ethical field access | Current permit rules and responsible authority |

## 6. Habitat-first decision logic

| Situation | Responsible interpretation |
|---|---|
| Species is threatened but habitat is unknown | Investigate habitat before intervention |
| Species range is historical only | Mark as historical and do not claim current presence |
| Species is present but recruitment is unknown | Study breeding, seed, juvenile, or regeneration conditions |
| Species occurs outside protected areas | Map community, private-land, corridor, and institutional stewardship |
| Recovery plan exists | Link the plan, assess implementation, and do not duplicate it |
| Species location is sensitive | Use controlled access and aggregated public information |
| Human–wildlife tension exists | Record benefits, burdens, safety, livelihoods, and ecological needs together |

## 7. Next extraction priorities

For Red Fin Labeo, the next step is to retrieve the species-recovery plan, current distribution evidence, basin and habitat information, water and seasonal data, and community or institutional roles. For additional species, the atlas should choose cases from forest, wetland, coastal, agricultural, and urban systems so that it does not equate conservation with one habitat type.

> **The status of a species tells us that attention is needed; the habitat relationship tells us what attention may responsibly mean.**
