# Species cluster 04 — Endemic and endangered beings with habitat context

**Anavaratha living-sector atlas — Sri Lanka, working research note, Version 0.1**

## 1. Research question

How should Anavaratha study endemic and endangered beings as part of living habitats and relationships, rather than as isolated symbols of loss?

The first anchor case is the Red Fin Labeo (*Labeo lankae*), an endemic freshwater fish connected to the Malwathu Oya River Basin. This case is useful because it makes the habitat relationship explicit: the organism cannot be understood without the basin, water, movement, food web, local communities, institutions, and recovery plan around it.

## 2. Anchor case — Red Fin Labeo (*Labeo lankae*)

The Sri Lanka Biodiversity Clearing-House, describing an IUCN project, identifies Red Fin Labeo as endemic to Sri Lanka and classified as Critically Endangered because of its limited distribution [1]. The project page states that earlier studies recorded a wider dry-zone distribution, while more recent evidence restricted its known presence to the mid and lower Malwathu Oya Basin in the North Central dry zone [1]. It also identifies information gaps concerning population, habitat, distribution, threats, and climate-change impacts.

| Field | Preliminary entry |
|---|---|
| **Organism** | Red Fin Labeo, *Labeo lankae* |
| **Status** | Endemic to Sri Lanka; listed on the project page as Critically Endangered |
| **Habitat context** | Freshwater system in the Malwathu Oya River Basin; earlier dry-zone distribution reported |
| **Relationships to study** | River flow, water conditions, food web, breeding, movement, basin, community, and institutions |
| **Evidence gap** | Population, habitat, distribution, threats, and climate-change effects require detailed investigation |
| **Conservation response** | Distribution survey, species-recovery plan, habitat enrichment, and school/community awareness [1] |
| **Regenerative question** | What conditions allow the fish and its freshwater habitat to persist and recover? |

This is not a direct cultivation target. It is a living-sector anchor that teaches Anavaratha to understand habitat responsibility at basin scale.

## 3. Why habitat must come first

An endangered organism may require protection of a river, forest, wetland, nesting site, corridor, food web, or seasonal habitat rather than direct propagation. If habitat conditions are not restored, moving or breeding individuals may provide only temporary relief. Conversely, a habitat intervention may support multiple species even when the original target is one endangered being.

The atlas should therefore trace the organism outward:

> **Organism → habitat → ecological relationships → landscape or basin → human use and governance → recovery conditions**

This prevents “species rescue” from becoming disconnected from the living system that made the species possible.

## 4. Priority dimensions

| Dimension | Question for the Red Fin Labeo and future cases |
|---|---|
| **Distribution** | Where is the organism confirmed, historically reported, or uncertain? |
| **Habitat** | What water, soil, vegetation, flow, shelter, food, and breeding conditions are required? |
| **Connectivity** | Can individuals move among habitat patches, tributaries, pools, tanks, or seasonal areas? |
| **Food web** | What organisms support or depend on the species? |
| **Seasonality** | How do rainfall, flow, breeding, temperature, and food availability change through the year? |
| **Threats** | Which ecological, spatial, climatic, institutional, or human-use conditions reduce continuity? |
| **Recovery** | Which conditions or relationships must return before the population can recover? |
| **Community** | Who lives with, uses, observes, or can help protect the habitat? |
| **Governance** | Which authorities and institutions have responsibility or influence? |
| **Uncertainty** | Which claims require survey, monitoring, or specialist verification? |

## 5. Other priority groups

The initial atlas should later include representatives from other Sri Lankan living systems, such as endemic forest trees, amphibians, reptiles, birds, mammals, freshwater organisms, insects, and medicinal plants. Selection should be based on ecological and biocultural relationships, not only on public familiarity or visual appeal.

A species may be prioritised because it is endemic, endangered, habitat-specific, a connector, an indicator, culturally significant, or a carrier of a relationship that supports many other beings. The atlas should also include ordinary or common organisms so that conservation attention does not become detached from everyday ecological function.

## 6. Social responsibility

The IUCN project identifies awareness work at school and community levels and partnership with relevant authorities as part of the Red Fin Labeo conservation approach [1]. This demonstrates that species recovery includes learning, institutions, and local relationships as well as biological monitoring.

Endangered-species information can be sensitive. Exact locations may need to be withheld. Local knowledge should not be published without consent. Communities should not be blamed for species decline when broader infrastructure, policy, market, or land-use systems are involved. At the same time, local observations and stewardship should not be treated as secondary to outside expertise.

## 7. Regenerative conditions

| Condition | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Habitat continuity** | Maintains the places required for feeding, breeding, shelter, and movement |
| **Hydrological fit** | Supports water-dependent species through appropriate flow, depth, temperature, and seasonality |
| **Food-web integrity** | Maintains prey, plants, microorganisms, and associated organisms |
| **Connectivity** | Allows movement and reduces isolation |
| **Low harmful disturbance** | Protects sensitive life stages and habitats |
| **Community participation** | Makes observation, awareness, and care locally meaningful |
| **Institutional responsibility** | Clarifies who surveys, protects, monitors, and responds |
| **Long-term monitoring** | Reveals whether recovery is real, seasonal, or temporary |

## 8. Implications for Anavaratha

The Red Fin Labeo case makes the living-sector direction precise. An endangered being is not only an object of rescue. It is a signal that a habitat, relationship, or governance system may require attention.

The project should ask what kind of cultivation is appropriate. It may be protection, habitat restoration, water or corridor care, natural regeneration, species monitoring, local education, knowledge preservation, or institutional coordination. Direct propagation or translocation should never be assumed to be the correct response.

> **For endangered beings, cultivate the conditions of survival before attempting to multiply the survivors.**

## 9. Open questions

The next research stage should obtain species-level literature for Red Fin Labeo; map its confirmed and historical range; understand water, food, breeding, movement, and basin relationships; review the species-recovery plan; and identify the role of local communities and authorities.

It should then select a small number of additional endemic or endangered cases from different habitats and compare them using the same atlas fields. The aim is to understand how conservation responsibility changes across freshwater, forest, wetland, coastal, agricultural, and urban systems.
