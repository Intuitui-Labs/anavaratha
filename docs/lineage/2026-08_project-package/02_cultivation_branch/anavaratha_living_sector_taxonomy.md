# Anavaratha

## Living-sector descent and relationship taxonomy

**Sri Lanka regenerative-cultivation dimension — Working framework, Version 0.1**

> **The living sector is the part of the landscape understood through the beings that inhabit it, the roles they perform, the relationships they form, and the conditions that allow those relationships to continue.**

## 1. Why descend from landscape to living beings?

The eagle-eye view identifies Sri Lanka’s ecological regions, watersheds, forests, wetlands, coasts, farms, urban spaces, communities, and institutions. The next analytical movement descends into the living sector: trees, plants, animals, birds, insects, fungi, soil organisms, and other beings that make those systems alive.

This descent is not a move from the important to the less important. It is a change in resolution. The landscape cannot be understood without its living beings, and the living beings cannot be understood without the landscape that sustains them.

Anavaratha should therefore avoid building a simple species catalogue. The purpose is to build a **relationship atlas** that asks where an organism lives, what it needs, what it provides, what it depends on, what depends on it, how people relate to it, and what forms of cultivation may support its continuity.

## 2. The nested descent

| Level | Object of attention | Core questions |
|---|---|---|
| **Landscape** | Ecological region, watershed, coast, settlement, or agricultural mosaic | What living systems exist here and how are they connected? |
| **Habitat** | Forest, wetland, mangrove, farm, garden, stream, tank, grassland, or urban patch | What conditions and communities define this habitat? |
| **Community** | Assemblage of plants, animals, insects, fungi, and microorganisms | Which beings interact, compete, depend on, or enable one another? |
| **Organism** | Tree, plant, animal, bird, insect, fungus, or soil organism | What does this being require, do, and make possible? |
| **Relationship** | Pollination, seed dispersal, shade, predation, decomposition, nesting, medicinal use, cultural relation, or stewardship | What connection carries life, knowledge, value, or continuity? |
| **Condition** | Soil, water, shade, temperature, season, shelter, food, space, disturbance, and care | What allows the organism and relationship to continue? |

## 3. Living-sector categories

The categories below are analytical lenses, not rigid biological divisions. One being may belong to several categories at once.

| Category | What it includes | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Trees and structural plants** | Canopy trees, understory plants, shrubs, climbers, grasses, aquatic plants, and ground cover | Create structure, shade, habitat, food, roots, litter, moisture conditions, and future regeneration |
| **Animals and birds** | Mammals, reptiles, amphibians, birds, fish, and other visible fauna | Indicate habitat quality and perform roles in movement, predation, grazing, pollination, seed dispersal, and food webs |
| **Insects and pollinators** | Bees, butterflies, moths, beetles, flies, ants, dragonflies, and other invertebrates | Support pollination, decomposition, predation, nutrient cycling, and ecological signals |
| **Soil organisms and fungi** | Fungi, bacteria, earthworms, termites, mites, nematodes, and other soil life | Build soil structure, decompose organic matter, cycle nutrients, and connect roots to wider systems |
| **Endemic and endangered beings** | Species or populations with restricted ranges, high conservation concern, or irreplaceable local value | Require careful habitat protection, recovery planning, and attention to ecological and genetic continuity |
| **Historical and culturally related beings** | Species connected with stories, rituals, medicine, food, agriculture, place, memory, or identity | Reveal biocultural relationships and values not captured by ecological metrics alone |
| **Medicinal and food plants** | Plants associated with food, medicine, nutrition, household use, or local health traditions | Connect biodiversity with wellbeing, knowledge, seed continuity, and livelihood |
| **Functional or service-bearing beings** | Species that provide shade, water retention, soil protection, habitat, cooling, wind protection, nectar, fruit, or other functions | Make ecological relationships visible without reducing beings to services alone |
| **Attractor and connector beings** | Plants that attract pollinators, fruit-eaters, seed dispersers, birds, insects, or other organisms; organisms that connect habitats | Help cultivate relationships and ecological networks |

## 4. Functional roles without reduction

The project may use functional descriptions such as “shade tree,” “water-preserving plant,” “pollinator-attracting species,” “seed-disperser,” or “soil-builder.” These descriptions are useful for understanding relationships, but they must not replace the organism’s full ecological identity.

A tree that provides shade may also host insects, support fungi, feed animals, protect soil, hold water, carry cultural meaning, and reproduce through dispersed seed. A medicinal plant may also support pollinators and soil organisms. An insect often described as a pest may be food for birds, a decomposer, or part of a wider ecological balance.

The project should therefore record both **functional role** and **intrinsic or relational significance**.

## 5. Priority relationship types

| Relationship | Example research question |
|---|---|
| **Pollination** | Which plants depend on which insects, birds, bats, or other pollinators? |
| **Seed dispersal** | Which animals move seeds, and what habitats allow the relationship to continue? |
| **Shade and microclimate** | How do trees and layered vegetation alter temperature, moisture, and shelter? |
| **Water retention and movement** | Which plants and habitats help slow, hold, infiltrate, or circulate water? |
| **Soil formation and renewal** | Which roots, fungi, microbes, detritivores, and ground organisms build living soil? |
| **Food-web relation** | Who eats whom, and what changes when one population declines or increases? |
| **Habitat creation** | Which beings create nesting, refuge, breeding, or feeding spaces for others? |
| **Medicinal and food relation** | Which plants support human health, nutrition, and local knowledge? |
| **Cultural and historical relation** | Which beings carry memory, ritual, story, place, or identity? |
| **Stewardship relation** | Who protects, cultivates, observes, uses, propagates, or transmits knowledge about the being? |

## 6. The organism relationship record

Each priority organism or group should eventually have a relationship record containing:

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| **Identity** | Common name, Sinhala and Tamil names where appropriate, scientific name, and taxonomic confidence |
| **Habitat** | Ecological region, habitat type, elevation, soil, water, and spatial range |
| **Status** | Endemic, native, introduced, threatened, endangered, common, recovering, or uncertain; always with source and date |
| **Life requirements** | Food, water, soil, shade, temperature, nesting, breeding, movement, and seasonal conditions |
| **Ecological roles** | Pollination, dispersal, shade, water, soil, habitat, predation, decomposition, or other relationships |
| **Associated beings** | Plants, animals, insects, fungi, microorganisms, and communities connected to it |
| **Human relationships** | Food, medicine, livelihood, ritual, history, education, stewardship, or conflict |
| **Threats and pressures** | Habitat loss, fragmentation, climate, invasive species, disturbance, extraction, disease, or knowledge loss |
| **Regenerative opportunity** | Protection, natural recovery, propagation, habitat creation, seed work, monitoring, or other appropriate care |
| **Evidence and uncertainty** | Sources, observation date, geographic coverage, confidence, and unresolved questions |

## 7. The descending research movement

The living-sector research should move through five questions:

1. **Who is alive here?** Identify organisms and communities in a place.
2. **What relationships are present?** Map dependence, exchange, attraction, movement, and function.
3. **What is vulnerable or irreplaceable?** Identify endemic, endangered, rare, historical, medicinal, food-related, or keystone relationships.
4. **What conditions support continuity?** Study soil, water, shade, habitat, food, seasonality, space, protection, and stewardship.
5. **What can be cultivated responsibly?** Choose protection, propagation, assisted regeneration, habitat creation, seed work, or another proportionate form of care.

## 8. Working principle

> **Do not ask only what a species can do for the project. Ask what relationships the project must support for the species and its community of life to continue.**

This is the ethical centre of the living-sector descent. Functional categories help identify leverage, but they should not turn living beings into instruments. Regenerative cultivation should increase the possibility of reciprocal flourishing among organisms, habitats, and communities.

## 9. Relationship to biocultural knowledge

The living sector includes scientific classifications and local ways of knowing. Sinhala and Tamil names, medicinal traditions, agricultural knowledge, stories, rituals, food practices, and historical associations may reveal relationships that do not appear in a conventional biodiversity database.

These forms of knowledge should be recorded with attribution, context, consent, and reciprocity. Anavaratha should not extract local knowledge merely to populate an atlas. The atlas should help return visibility, recognition, learning, and value to the people and living beings connected to that knowledge.

## 10. Initial priority clusters for Sri Lanka

The first deep explorations may be organised around clusters rather than isolated species:

| Cluster | Possible focus |
|---|---|
| **Forest structure** | Native canopy, understory, fruiting trees, climbers, epiphytes, fungi, and regenerating seedlings |
| **Pollinator and flower relations** | Flowering plants, bees, butterflies, moths, flies, beetles, and seasonal continuity |
| **Seed and fruit networks** | Fruiting plants, birds, bats, mammals, seed dispersal, and regeneration |
| **Water-linked life** | Riparian plants, wetland vegetation, fish, amphibians, aquatic insects, and water-dependent fauna |
| **Soil-life networks** | Roots, fungi, earthworms, termites, decomposers, microbes, and soil-building plants |
| **Medicinal and food biodiversity** | Osu Uyana-related medicinal traditions, edible plants, homegardens, seed systems, and nutrition |
| **Endemic and threatened relations** | Priority species, habitat requirements, corridors, recovery, and conservation responsibility |
| **Shade and climate relations** | Trees and layered vegetation that create cooler, moister, safer microhabitats |
| **Cultural and historical beings** | Species connected with place, memory, ritual, agriculture, medicine, and identity |

“Osu Uyana” should be treated as a term or reference requiring contextual verification in the Sri Lankan knowledge landscape, not assumed to describe one uniform category. The research should distinguish formal medicinal-garden institutions, traditional medicinal knowledge, household plants, and broader cultural associations.

## 11. What this makes Anavaratha

This descent changes Anavaratha from a general restoration framework into a **living relationship practice**. The project can move from landscape to habitat, habitat to community, community to organism, organism to relationship, and relationship to condition.

Its concern is not only whether a place has more trees or more animals. It is whether the conditions for mutual dependence, reproduction, movement, nourishment, shade, water, memory, medicine, and care are becoming stronger.

> **Anavaratha cultivates the conditions in which living beings can continue to meet one another.**
