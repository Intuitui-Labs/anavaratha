# Domain 04 — Biodiversity, habitat, and ecological connectivity

## 1. Definition and scope

This domain examines biodiversity as a network of living relationships rather than a list of species. It includes genetic diversity, species diversity, habitat quality, ecological functions, habitat fragmentation, corridors, seasonal movement, seed dispersal, pollination, ecosystem services, and the human communities connected to these systems.

The central question is:

> **How can regenerative cultivation strengthen the diversity, continuity, and ecological relationships through which Sri Lankan living systems persist?**

## 2. What the evidence shows

Sri Lanka’s biodiversity is shaped by ecological, climatic, soil, and topographical variation, and includes forest, agricultural, aquatic, marine, wetland, and coastal ecosystems [1]. The Convention on Biological Diversity identifies high species richness and endemism alongside significant biodiversity decline and historical reduction of natural forest cover [1]. These conditions make habitat quality and ecological connection central to cultivation.

A study of Muthurajawela Marsh and Negombo Lagoon shows how a wetland can provide several interconnected ecosystem services, including flood attenuation, water purification, carbon sequestration, and fish breeding and nursery functions [2]. The study uses land-use change, Earth-observation data, and spatial modelling to examine past changes and future scenarios. Its approach demonstrates that biodiversity and habitat should be studied through both ecological function and spatial change.

The Convention on Biological Diversity also documents a Sri Lankan pilot that integrated spatial and socio-economic data to identify biodiversity priorities in relation to development planning, including the distribution of biodiversity, irreplaceable sites, and ecosystem-service sources [3]. The Western Ghats and Sri Lanka biodiversity-hotspot profile provides an additional conservation context for considering habitat, species, and spatial priorities [4].

## 3. From species lists to living networks

Species inventories are important, but they do not by themselves show whether a living system is functioning. Anavaratha should ask how species interact, where they move, what they require seasonally, how they reproduce, and what happens when habitat is fragmented.

Connectivity may involve forest corridors, streamside vegetation, wetland chains, coastal habitats, home gardens, agricultural mosaics, or less visible networks of seed dispersal, pollination, groundwater, and seasonal movement. A corridor should therefore be understood functionally rather than only geometrically. A planted strip that does not support movement, food, shelter, or reproduction may not provide meaningful ecological connection.

Biodiversity also exists at multiple levels. Genetic variation supports adaptation; species diversity supports ecological roles; habitat diversity supports different life histories; and landscape diversity allows ecological processes to continue across space and time. Cultivation should seek to strengthen all relevant levels rather than reduce biodiversity to the presence of a few attractive species.

## 4. Habitat quality and fragmentation

Habitat quality includes food, shelter, nesting or breeding conditions, water, soil, microclimate, protection, and the absence or management of harmful disturbance. Fragmentation can separate populations, interrupt movement, reduce gene flow, increase edge effects, and weaken ecological processes.

However, landscapes are not simply divided into “natural” and “non-natural” areas. Farms, home gardens, tanks, plantations, villages, urban wetlands, sacred landscapes, and managed forests may all contribute differently to ecological connectivity. Anavaratha should study how living organisms use these mosaics and which management practices support or interrupt their relationships.

## 5. Ecosystem services and intrinsic value

Ecosystem services can help explain how biodiversity supports people through water regulation, food, fisheries, pollination, soil formation, climate moderation, medicine, protection, and cultural meaning. They are useful for making ecological relationships visible in planning and decision-making.

At the same time, biodiversity should not be valued only when it provides a measurable service to humans. Anavaratha should hold together instrumental, intrinsic, cultural, and intergenerational values. A species or habitat may matter because it performs an ecological function, because a community has a relationship with it, because it has its own living significance, or because future generations should inherit the possibility of encountering it.

## 6. Regenerative conditions

| Condition | Importance |
|---|---|
| **Habitat quality** | Provides food, shelter, water, breeding conditions, and ecological function |
| **Connectivity** | Enables movement, dispersal, pollination, gene flow, and seasonal adaptation |
| **Structural diversity** | Supports different species through canopy, understorey, ground, aquatic, and edge habitats |
| **Native and locally appropriate species** | Strengthens relationships suited to place and ecological history |
| **Landscape mosaics** | Connects forests, farms, wetlands, gardens, streams, and settlements where appropriate |
| **Monitoring** | Reveals whether species presence corresponds to real ecological improvement |
| **Social compatibility** | Ensures that conservation and cultivation do not create unrecognised burdens for communities |

## 7. Implications for Anavaratha

Anavaratha should cultivate **relationships, not just coverage**. The goal is not simply to increase the number of planted areas, but to strengthen habitat quality, ecological function, species interactions, and continuity across landscapes.

This requires asking what species are already present, what is missing, what habitat they need, whether a proposed intervention connects or isolates ecosystems, how seasonal movement works, and how local people understand or use the landscape. It also requires resisting the temptation to use one indicator—tree count, area, species count, or carbon estimate—as a complete measure of regeneration.

A useful principle is:

> **Regeneration is not the multiplication of individual organisms; it is the renewal of relationships among living beings and their habitats.**

This principle links biodiversity to the rest of the Anavaratha framework. Forests need habitat connectivity; soil and water support species relationships; farms can function as ecological mosaics; wetlands connect urban and coastal systems; and communities shape the continuity of care.

## 8. Open questions

The next research stage should identify Sri Lanka’s priority habitats, endemic and threatened species, ecological corridors, habitat-fragmentation patterns, biodiversity monitoring systems, and available spatial datasets. It should examine how forests, home gardens, farms, tanks, wetlands, rivers, coastal habitats, and urban green spaces interact.

It should also ask how connectivity can be assessed in practice; which species or ecological processes can serve as indicators; how local knowledge can complement remote sensing and species data; how restoration affects both target and non-target species; and how conservation priorities can be reconciled with community access, livelihoods, and local governance.
