# Domain 06 — Agroecology, agroforestry, seeds, and living food systems

## 1. Definition and scope

This domain examines food production as part of living ecological systems. It includes agroecology, agroforestry, homegardens, forest gardens, seed diversity, traditional cultivation, soil and water relationships, biodiversity, food security, livelihoods, nutrition, farmer knowledge, and the cultural meaning of food.

The central question is:

> **How can Sri Lankan food and cultivation systems nourish people while increasing ecological diversity, soil health, resilience, and local capacity?**

## 2. What the evidence shows

A review of homegarden agroforestry systems identifies homegardens as small-scale, often multi-layered systems that combine trees, crops, and sometimes animals around homes. It reports that Sri Lankan homegardening covers approximately 1.05 million hectares, while also emphasising that homegardens vary substantially according to soil, topography, species, culture, and local practice [1]. Because the figure comes from a cited 2020 source, Anavaratha should verify its current scope before using it as a present national estimate.

The review connects homegardens with food, livelihoods, biodiversity, ecological infrastructure, institutions, and sustainable-development outcomes [1]. It also describes homegardens as systems that can accommodate high plant diversity and may require relatively limited financial investment to begin. These characteristics make them important for studying how cultivation can start simply while remaining connected to wider ecological and social functions.

Sri Lanka’s biodiversity profile identifies rich agro-biodiversity shaped by long-standing hydraulic systems, farmer selection, and adaptation to varied ecological conditions [2]. This suggests that regenerative cultivation should investigate existing agricultural knowledge and diversity rather than assume that regeneration must be introduced from outside.

## 3. Agroecology as relationship

Agroecology is not a single technique. It is a way of understanding agriculture through ecological relationships, including diversity, soil life, water, crops, trees, animals, pollinators, pests, climate, labour, markets, knowledge, and culture.

Agroforestry and homegardens are especially relevant because they can combine multiple vertical layers and functions. A tree may provide food, shade, habitat, soil protection, leaf litter, fuel, medicine, or income. Crops may support nutrition and livelihood. Domestic animals may be integrated into nutrient cycles. The value of the system lies in how its elements interact.

Anavaratha should avoid romanticising all traditional or small-scale systems. It should study what works, for whom, under which conditions, and with what labour and resource requirements. It should also examine how market pressures, land fragmentation, climate change, migration, tenure, and changing diets affect continuity.

## 4. Seeds and living food

Seeds are not only agricultural inputs. They carry genetic diversity, adaptation, memory, cultural identity, farmer knowledge, and future possibility. Seed saving and exchange can support local autonomy and make food systems more resilient, but they also require attention to land, labour, storage, selection, governance, and access.

Navdanya presents seed saving and sharing as connected to biological and cultural diversity, living food, and continuity [3]. For Anavaratha, this offers an important conceptual bridge between ecological cultivation and human capacity: communities are not only recipients of restoration but holders and producers of living knowledge.

The research should study Sri Lankan seed systems, locally adapted varieties, farmer selection, homegarden species, community seed networks, seed laws and institutions, and the relationship between agrobiodiversity and nutrition. It should distinguish between documented local practice, advocacy claims, and hypotheses requiring further investigation.

## 5. Soil, water, and livelihood relationships

Agroecological cultivation cannot be separated from soil and water. Homegardens and agroforestry systems may influence shade, erosion, soil moisture, nutrient cycling, microclimate, biodiversity, and household resilience. At the same time, they may demand labour, land, water, and knowledge that are unevenly available.

A regenerative assessment should therefore ask whether an agricultural system improves soil structure and fertility, protects water, supports biodiversity, increases dietary or livelihood diversity, and remains viable for the people who manage it. It should not measure success only through yield or area.

## 6. Regenerative conditions

| Condition | Importance |
|---|---|
| **Agrobiodiversity** | Multiple crops, trees, varieties, and species can support resilience and nutrition |
| **Living soil** | Soil organisms, organic matter, roots, and cover support fertility and water relations |
| **Seed continuity** | Local saving, selection, storage, exchange, and adaptation maintain future capacity |
| **Ecological layering** | Trees, crops, ground cover, and animals can perform complementary functions |
| **Water fit** | Cultivation should match rainfall, irrigation, moisture, drought, and watershed conditions |
| **Livelihood viability** | Ecological practices must be workable for the people whose labour sustains them |
| **Knowledge plurality** | Farmer, local, scientific, and institutional knowledge should be connected without erasing differences |
| **Market and policy support** | Long-term regeneration depends partly on the wider economic and institutional environment |

## 7. Implications for Anavaratha

Agroecology offers Anavaratha a way to understand cultivation as both ecological and social. It provides a possible route by which simple actions can create leverage: a homegarden, seed network, food forest, school garden, or agroforestry plot may support food, biodiversity, soil, knowledge, shade, and community participation simultaneously.

The project should not treat agroecology as a universal solution or use “traditional” as a substitute for evidence. It should study diverse Sri Lankan practices, identify where they are adaptive, understand the labour and land conditions they require, and examine how they interact with institutions and markets.

The core principle is:

> **Cultivate food systems that increase the diversity and future capacity of the landscapes and communities that sustain them.**

## 8. Open questions

The next research stage should map Sri Lankan homegarden and agroforestry systems across ecological regions; examine locally adapted crops and trees; study seed networks and farmer selection; compare soil, water, food, nutrition, biodiversity, and livelihood outcomes; investigate gender and labour; and identify the policies and markets that support or constrain agroecological continuity.

It should also ask how a cultivation initiative can support existing farmers and knowledge holders without appropriating their knowledge, imposing an external model, or turning living practices into a simplified brand.
