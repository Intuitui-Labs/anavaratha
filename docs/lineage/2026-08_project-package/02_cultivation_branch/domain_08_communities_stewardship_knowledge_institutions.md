# Domain 08 — Communities, stewardship, knowledge, and institutions

## 1. Definition and scope

This domain examines the human capacities and relationships that make regenerative cultivation possible over time. It includes local and traditional ecological knowledge, community participation, stewardship, rights, access, livelihoods, institutions, decision-making, gender, labour, learning, conflict, accountability, and intergenerational continuity.

The central question is:

> **Who can care for living systems, under what conditions, with whose knowledge, authority, labour, and long-term support?**

## 2. What the evidence shows

A participatory study of Sri Lanka’s village tank cascade systems integrated local knowledge, expert judgement, and land-use attributes to assess ecosystem services. It identified 24 ecosystem services through community perceptions and concluded that restoration depends on addressing ecological complexity alongside social engagement by communities and stakeholders [1].

Research on community forestry in Sri Lanka’s intermediate zone found that forest outcomes varied with pre-existing conditions, the duration of implementation, local perceptions, local decisions, and government policy [2]. This indicates that community participation is not a simple variable that can be added to a project. Its quality, authority, duration, and relationship to institutions matter.

The World Bank’s account of ecosystem conservation in Sri Lanka connects biodiversity protection, land-use planning, restoration, community engagement, training, and livelihood opportunities [3]. The relationship between ecological restoration and human livelihood is therefore part of the restoration system itself.

## 3. Stewardship as ecological capacity

Stewardship is often treated as a social support function after the ecological design has already been decided. Anavaratha should reverse this assumption. The people who observe, use, maintain, protect, remember, and adapt a landscape are part of its ecological capacity.

A restoration site may fail because the wrong species were selected, but it may also fail because no one has authority to care for it, because maintenance is unpaid, because access is restricted, because responsibilities are unclear, or because the project conflicts with local use. These are ecological outcomes with social causes.

Stewardship should therefore be studied through actual capacities: time, labour, knowledge, land access, rights, tools, finance, leadership, trust, safety, decision-making, and institutional support. A community should not be described as a “partner” unless it can influence the project and share in its benefits and responsibilities.

## 4. Knowledge plurality

Regenerative cultivation requires several forms of knowledge. Scientific ecology can provide species, soil, water, climate, and monitoring knowledge. Farmers and local practitioners may hold detailed knowledge of seasons, seeds, soils, water, animals, and cultivation. Indigenous and cultural traditions may contain relationships, values, practices, and classifications not captured by technical datasets. Institutional knowledge reveals policy, land, planning, and implementation conditions.

These forms of knowledge should be brought into conversation without assuming that one automatically validates or replaces the others. Anavaratha should document the origin, context, authority, and limits of each claim. It should also address consent, attribution, reciprocity, and the risk of extracting knowledge without returning value to the people who hold it.

## 5. Institutions and continuity

Regenerative cultivation often crosses institutional boundaries. Forests, watersheds, agriculture, wetlands, urban planning, land, fisheries, education, and local government may each be governed by different organisations. Community organisations, schools, universities, farmers, businesses, civil-society groups, and customary authorities may operate alongside formal agencies.

The research should map not only which institutions exist but how they interact. Important questions include who has legal authority, who controls land or water, who can approve or prevent action, who funds maintenance, who monitors outcomes, who resolves conflict, and who is accountable if the project creates unintended harm.

## 6. Justice and distribution

Regeneration can produce benefits such as food, shade, water, biodiversity, livelihoods, safety, and cultural value. It can also produce costs such as restricted access, labour burdens, wildlife conflict, displacement, opportunity costs, or unequal control over restored resources.

Jadav Payeng’s forest-restoration example illustrates this tension: ecological recovery and biodiversity gains were accompanied by reported impacts on nearby villagers through animal movement and damage to economic activities [4]. Anavaratha should treat this not as an exception but as a necessary reminder that ecological and social outcomes must be assessed together.

## 7. Regenerative conditions

| Condition | Importance |
|---|---|
| **Meaningful participation** | Communities influence problem definition, design, decisions, and evaluation |
| **Recognised rights and access** | People who depend on landscapes are not displaced or excluded without justification |
| **Knowledge reciprocity** | Local and traditional knowledge is respected, attributed, protected, and returned value |
| **Institutional coordination** | Relevant agencies and groups can align responsibilities across ecological scales |
| **Livelihood compatibility** | Restoration does not ignore the economic conditions that shape land use and care |
| **Gender and labour awareness** | Invisible or unequal work is recognised in design and evaluation |
| **Conflict sensitivity** | Wildlife, access, conservation, development, and livelihood tensions are addressed openly |
| **Long-term accountability** | Responsibility, finance, maintenance, monitoring, and learning continue beyond launch |

## 8. Implications for Anavaratha

Anavaratha should understand positive ecological freedom as a shared capacity rather than an individual achievement. A living landscape becomes more capable when communities can participate meaningfully, institutions can coordinate, knowledge can circulate responsibly, and care can continue across generations.

The project should avoid the language of “empowering” communities if the project itself retains all authority. A better question is: **what capacity already exists, what capacity is constrained, and what relationships would allow it to grow?**

The core principle is:

> **Regeneration is durable only when ecological capacity and social capacity grow together.**

This principle also protects Anavaratha from becoming a purely technical or heroic project. The film, Jadav Payeng, biomimicry, Navdanya, and Sri Lankan restoration precedents all become more useful when read through relationships, accountability, and continuity rather than isolated inspiration.

## 9. Open questions

The next research stage should map the institutions and communities connected to Sri Lankan forests, watersheds, farms, wetlands, coasts, and urban ecosystems. It should study community forestry, participatory ecosystem assessment, traditional ecological knowledge, farmer and seed networks, women’s ecological labour, livelihood relationships, land and water rights, conflict, and financing for long-term care.

It should also develop an ethical protocol for future engagement: how communities are approached, how consent is obtained, how knowledge is attributed, how benefits are shared, how disagreement is handled, and how Anavaratha remains accountable after public attention moves elsewhere.
