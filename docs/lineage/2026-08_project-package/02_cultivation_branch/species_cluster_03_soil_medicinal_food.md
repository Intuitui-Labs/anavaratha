# Species cluster 03 — Soil life, medicinal plants, and food plants

**Anavaratha living-sector atlas — Sri Lanka, working research note, Version 0.1**

## 1. Research question

How do less-visible soil organisms and culturally significant medicinal and food plants contribute to the continuity of Sri Lankan living systems, and what forms of care protect those relationships?

This cluster combines two related but distinct inquiries. The first follows roots, fungi, microorganisms, decomposers, and soil conditions. The second follows plants through food, medicine, knowledge, cultivation, seed, culture, and conservation. They are connected through soil, water, household landscapes, and the continuity of care, but they should not be collapsed into one category.

## 2. Soil-life relation

A peer-reviewed study of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi in Sri Lankan rice rhizospheres reports that fungal colonisation depended on the growth stage of the host plant and interacted with fertilizer treatment and soil condition [1]. It found that colonisation increased over the sampling period and differed between sterilised and non-sterilised soil conditions [1].

The study supports a careful interpretation of root–fungus relationships. Soil life is not a static input that produces the same effect in every field. Its relationship with plants changes across growth stage, soil community, management, and environmental conditions.

### Soil-life record S-01

| Field | Preliminary entry |
|---|---|
| **Organisms** | Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi and rice roots; wider soil community to be studied |
| **Relationship** | Root–fungus association linked to colonisation, nutrient relations, and plant development |
| **Conditions** | Host growth stage, soil community, soil type, management, moisture, and season |
| **Evidence status** | Sri Lankan rice study documents time- and soil-dependent colonisation; wider field implications require care |
| **Regenerative question** | How can cultivation support soil biological relationships rather than replace them with uniform inputs? |
| **Next research** | Compare soil-life evidence across rice, homegardens, forest gardens, and restoration sites |

Anavaratha should also include earthworms, termites, decomposers, bacteria, fungi, roots, litter, and soil fauna where evidence is available. The aim is not to create an exhaustive microbial catalogue but to understand which soil relationships are relevant to regeneration and how they can be observed responsibly.

## 3. Medicinal plants and Osu Uyana

An AGRIS/FAO record for the World Bank publication *Medicinal Plants: Conservation and Sustainable Use in Sri Lanka* describes long-standing use of medicinal plants in Ayurvedic and traditional healthcare and records a large number of plant species associated with medicinal use, including endemic and threatened species [2]. The figures in the record belong to a historical source and must be cited with their date and limitations rather than treated as current national totals.

The same record notes that medicinal plants are also used in ritual, cultural, and religious practices [2]. This makes medicinal plants a biocultural category, not only a pharmacological category.

A prior Sri Lankan community-conservation study identified the establishment of demonstration plots known as Osu Uyana for medicinal plants in villages and schools and the promotion of medicinal plants in homegardens [3]. This provides a useful lead for understanding Osu Uyana as a practical and educational medicinal-garden context, while the exact local meanings, institutional arrangements, and language use require further verification.

### Medicinal-plant record M-01

| Field | Preliminary entry |
|---|---|
| **Organisms** | Medicinal plants selected through verified Sri Lankan sources and knowledge holders |
| **Relationship** | Plant ↔ health, cultivation, knowledge, culture, household, seed, and conservation |
| **Conditions** | Correct identity, propagation, habitat, sustainable harvest, knowledge continuity, and consent |
| **Evidence status** | Historical World Bank/FAO record and Sri Lankan community-conservation lead; species-level set not yet final |
| **Regenerative question** | How can medicinal-plant cultivation conserve biodiversity and knowledge without increasing extraction or making unsafe claims? |
| **Next research** | Verify Osu Uyana contexts, species, Sinhala/Tamil terms, cultivation practices, and knowledge protocols |

The atlas must distinguish documented medicinal use from clinical efficacy. It must also distinguish a plant’s cultural importance from permission to commercialise or harvest it.

## 4. Food plants and homegarden relations

A University of Jaffna study of homegarden agroforestry in the Jaffna Peninsula recorded diverse flora, fruit production, medicinal plants, shade, soil and water relations, and household and ecosystem-service dimensions [4]. This supports a food-plant record that includes nutrition, biodiversity, canopy, seed, household practice, and livelihood rather than treating a food crop as an isolated yield unit.

Kandyan homegardens are also documented as long-standing Sri Lankan landscapes in which perennial trees and fruit-tree diversity are shaped by climate, soil, rainfall, and culture [5]. These systems can inform the study of how food plants and structural trees coexist across layers and seasons.

## 5. Shared regenerative conditions

| Condition | Soil-life implication | Medicinal and food implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Identity** | Correctly identify organisms and associations | Correctly identify plants, varieties, names, and uses |
| **Continuity** | Protect soil communities, roots, litter, and seasonal processes | Maintain seed, propagation, knowledge, and cultivation continuity |
| **Diversity** | Support diverse soil organisms and plant communities | Preserve crop, medicinal, genetic, and dietary diversity |
| **Low disturbance** | Avoid unnecessary disruption of soil structure and biology | Avoid overharvesting, monoculture, or loss of habitat |
| **Reciprocity** | Soil life supports plants; plants supply habitat and organic matter | Plants support health, food, culture, and biodiversity; people provide care |
| **Evidence** | Match claims to soil method, growth stage, and conditions | Separate documented use, cultural meaning, conservation status, and clinical claim |
| **Stewardship** | Maintain soil and root conditions over time | Protect knowledge, rights, access, propagation, and responsible use |

## 6. Implications for Anavaratha

This cluster makes the living-sector descent more complete. Regeneration is not only visible in trees, flowers, birds, or butterflies. It is also present in root–fungus relations, soil organisms, seed continuity, medicinal knowledge, food diversity, and household landscapes.

The project should treat Osu Uyana as a possible bridge between medicinal plants, biocultural knowledge, education, conservation, and community cultivation. It should not assume that a medicinal garden is automatically regenerative; its species, soil, propagation, harvesting, knowledge governance, and ecological relationships must be studied.

The core principle is:

> **Cultivate the continuity of living knowledge and living matter together.**

## 7. Open questions

The next research stage should verify Sri Lankan medicinal-plant lists and conservation status; identify Osu Uyana examples and their institutional contexts; map edible and medicinal plants in homegardens; study seed saving and exchange; compare soil-life conditions across farming and restoration systems; and develop ethical protocols for recording traditional knowledge.

It should also investigate how food and medicinal systems can support biodiversity without shifting excessive labour or extraction burdens onto households, farmers, healers, or communities.
