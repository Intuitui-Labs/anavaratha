# Domain 05 — Wetlands, mangroves, coasts, and blue ecosystems

## 1. Definition and scope

This domain examines the living systems at and around Sri Lanka’s interface between land and sea. It includes inland wetlands, rivers, tanks, marshes, lagoons, estuaries, mangroves, seagrass-linked systems, salt marshes, coastal forests, beaches, dunes, fisheries, coastal settlements, and the communities whose livelihoods and safety depend on them.

The central question is:

> **What conditions allow Sri Lanka’s wetland and coastal systems to regenerate while sustaining biodiversity, climate resilience, livelihoods, and cultural relationships?**

## 2. What the evidence shows

Sri Lanka’s national biodiversity profile identifies extensive wetlands, rivers, irrigation tanks, coastal ecosystems, mangroves, seagrass beds, salt marshes, sand dunes, beaches, coral reefs, and marine biodiversity [1]. These systems are connected through water, sediment, nutrients, species movement, fisheries, seasonal cycles, and human use.

The United Nations Environment Programme describes Sri Lanka’s mangrove-regeneration programme as a shift from mass planting toward protecting existing mangroves and creating the conditions for natural recovery [2]. The programme uses site assessment, including soil and water quality, to identify appropriate restoration conditions and species. This is a direct lesson for Anavaratha: ecological cultivation should begin with diagnosis of the living system rather than assuming that planting is the universal first action.

UNEP also connects mangroves with coastal protection, fish nursery habitat, food, medicine, livelihoods, and climate resilience [2]. The benefits of restoration are therefore ecological and social at the same time.

Research on Muthurajawela Marsh and Negombo Lagoon similarly identifies flood attenuation, water purification, carbon sequestration, and fish breeding and nursery functions as ecosystem services of an important Sri Lankan urban wetland [3]. The study demonstrates the value of spatial and temporal monitoring when land-use change affects wetland function.

## 3. Wetlands as connected systems

Wetlands are not empty or marginal spaces waiting to be converted. They are dynamic systems shaped by water level, salinity, sediment, seasonal flooding, vegetation, fish, birds, microorganisms, surrounding land, and human use.

Mangroves occupy a particularly important threshold between land and sea. Their roots influence sediment, shelter, habitat, and water movement. Their regeneration depends on tidal and hydrological conditions, seed availability, soil, salinity, disturbance, and the relationship between existing stands and surrounding landscapes.

Lagoons, estuaries, rivers, tanks, marshes, and coastal vegetation should be studied in relation to one another. An intervention that appears beneficial at one point may alter sediment, access, water flow, or species relationships elsewhere. The relevant unit may be a wetland complex, lagoon system, estuary, river mouth, coastal zone, or connected watershed rather than a small planting plot.

## 4. Restoration versus replanting

The Sri Lankan mangrove experience provides a valuable distinction between **replanting** and **regeneration**. Replanting counts the number of introduced plants. Regeneration asks whether the ecological conditions allow a living system to establish, diversify, reproduce, and continue.

A restoration process may therefore involve protecting surviving stands, reopening or understanding water channels, assessing soil and salinity, reducing disturbance, supporting natural seedling establishment, selecting appropriate species, and working with communities. Planting may be needed in some locations, but it should follow ecological diagnosis.

| Question | Replanting focus | Regeneration focus |
|---|---|---|
| **What is measured?** | Number of plants placed | Survival, recruitment, diversity, function, and continuity |
| **What is assumed?** | Plants can establish if placed in the area | Establishment depends on ecological conditions |
| **How is success understood?** | Visible coverage | Self-renewing habitat and living relationships |
| **What is the role of people?** | Planting labour | Protection, learning, monitoring, adaptation, and stewardship |

## 5. Community and livelihood dimensions

Wetlands and coasts are lived landscapes. Fisheries, agriculture, transport, tourism, food, medicine, cultural practice, safety, and income may depend on their condition. Restoration should therefore identify who uses the system, how benefits and burdens are distributed, whose knowledge is present, and what forms of access or livelihood may change.

UNEP reports that Sri Lanka’s mangrove restoration has been connected with the return of fish catches and coastal livelihood opportunities [2]. Such outcomes should be studied carefully rather than assumed. The relevant question is how restoration changes ecological productivity, access, employment, safety, and long-term community capacity.

## 6. Regenerative conditions

| Condition | Importance |
|---|---|
| **Hydrological fit** | Water movement, tidal exchange, flooding, salinity, and sediment must support the ecosystem |
| **Natural regeneration** | Existing seedlings, propagules, seed sources, and surviving stands should be recognised and supported |
| **Species fit** | Species selection should respond to local conditions and ecological relationships |
| **Habitat continuity** | Wetlands, mangroves, lagoons, rivers, seagrass, and coastal systems should be studied as connected habitats |
| **Community stewardship** | Local knowledge, access, livelihood, monitoring, and maintenance must shape restoration |
| **Long-term monitoring** | Changes in water, soil, vegetation, fisheries, biodiversity, and community outcomes require time |
| **Institutional coordination** | Coastal, fisheries, environment, land, water, and local authorities may need to work together |

## 7. Implications for Anavaratha

The wetlands-and-coasts domain is a strong expression of Anavaratha’s cultivation principle because it demonstrates that the work is about **creating conditions**. The question is not “Where can trees be planted?” but “What is this water–land system trying to do, and what conditions would allow it to renew itself?”

This domain also expands the project’s imagination beyond terrestrial forests. Regenerative cultivation may include protecting a lagoon, restoring a wetland edge, supporting mangrove recovery, reconnecting a river and floodplain, maintaining a tank ecosystem, or strengthening coastal habitat and livelihoods.

The core principle is:

> **Restore the conditions of the ecosystem before attempting to reproduce its visible form.**

For Anavaratha, mangroves, wetlands, and coastal systems should not be treated only as climate infrastructure or carbon assets. They are living communities with ecological, social, cultural, and intergenerational significance.

## 8. Open questions

The next research stage should map Sri Lanka’s wetland, lagoon, mangrove, seagrass, salt-marsh, estuarine, and coastal systems; compare their ecological conditions; study natural regeneration; identify restoration guidelines and monitoring methods; examine community participation and livelihood outcomes; and investigate how upstream and coastal processes are connected.

It should also ask which coastal systems are already recovering, which restoration approaches have failed, what forms of local knowledge are under-recognised, and how climate change and sea-level change may alter the conditions for future cultivation.
