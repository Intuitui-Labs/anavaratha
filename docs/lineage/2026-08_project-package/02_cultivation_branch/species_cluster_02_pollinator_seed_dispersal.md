# Species cluster 02 — Pollinator and seed-dispersal relations

**Anavaratha living-sector atlas — Sri Lanka, working research note, Version 0.1**

## 1. Research question

Which plant–insect–bird–bat–mammal relationships reveal how Sri Lankan landscapes reproduce, move, diversify, and regenerate?

The focus is not on attracting animals as an ornamental outcome. It is on understanding the relationships through which plants reproduce and through which animals obtain food, shelter, and seasonal habitat.

## 2. Pollination as a relationship

Pollination should be studied as a network of flowering plants and flower-visiting organisms. Bees, butterflies, moths, flies, beetles, birds, bats, and other visitors may contribute differently depending on plant morphology, flowering season, habitat, temperature, weather, and nesting or refuge conditions.

A University of Jaffna study of 125 homegardens in the Jaffna Peninsula recorded plant and domestic-fauna diversity, medicinal plants, food production, shade, soil and water relations, and plant–pollinator relationships. Its abstract reports high host-per-pollinator and pollinator-per-host values for bees and mango [1]. This provides a useful Sri Lankan case of a cultivated landscape functioning through multiple plant–animal relationships.

A separate study of flower visitors in a Sri Lankan dry-zone forest patch examined species composition and visiting frequency, showing the value of observing not just whether pollinators are present but which visitors occur, how often they visit, and in what habitat context [2].

## 3. What the pollinator record should capture

| Field | Question |
|---|---|
| **Flowering plant** | What is the plant, where does it grow, and when does it flower? |
| **Visitor** | Which bee, butterfly, moth, fly, beetle, bird, bat, or other organism visits? |
| **Interaction** | Is the visitor a pollinator, nectar-feeder, pollen-collector, predator, or incidental visitor? |
| **Seasonality** | When does flowering and visiting occur? |
| **Habitat** | What soil, shade, water, shelter, and surrounding vegetation are present? |
| **Continuity** | Are flowering resources available across seasons or only briefly? |
| **Reproduction** | Is there evidence of pollen transfer, fruit set, or seed production? |
| **Stewardship** | Who cultivates, observes, protects, or shares knowledge about the relationship? |

The term “pollinator-friendly” should not be used without specifying which organisms are supported, how, and during which season. A flower-rich space may still fail to support pollinators if nesting habitat, water, shelter, or continuity are absent.

## 4. Seed dispersal as a relationship

Seed dispersal connects fruiting plants with birds, bats, mammals, and other animals. It can move seeds away from parent plants, connect habitat patches, create recruitment opportunities, and influence forest composition. The relationship depends on fruiting seasons, animal movement, food availability, shelter, seed viability, deposition, germination, and suitable microsites.

A Sri Lankan rainforest-restoration review identifies animal-mediated seed dispersal as relevant to rainforest restoration and distinguishes restoration pathways according to disturbance, advance regeneration, canopy, site conditions, and succession [3]. The review’s broader conclusion is important for Anavaratha: the presence of a seed-dispersing animal is not enough; the landscape must also provide places where dispersed seeds can establish.

## 5. Relationship chain

The pollinator and seed-dispersal cluster should be represented as a chain rather than a single interaction:

> **Plant → flower or fruit → visitor or disperser → movement or transfer → seed or fruit set → germination or recruitment → future habitat**

A break at any stage may reduce regeneration. For example, a flowering plant may lack pollinators; a fruiting tree may lack dispersers; seeds may be dispersed into unsuitable ground; seedlings may be suppressed; or habitat may be too fragmented for the relationship to persist.

## 6. Candidate relationship records

### P-01 — Mango and bee relationship in cultivated landscapes

The Jaffna homegarden study identifies mango as a plant associated with high pollinator-per-host values for bees [1]. The first record should verify the mango species, bee taxa, flowering period, cultivation conditions, and evidence of pollination or fruit set. The record should not assume that all bees or all mango trees have the same relationship.

### P-02 — Flowering plant and visitor network in a dry-zone forest patch

The dry-zone flower-visitor study provides a case for recording visitor composition and visiting frequency in a forest context [2]. The next step is to obtain the full species table and identify which native or locally established flowering plants support which visitors across the season.

### S-01 — Fruiting plant and animal disperser network

This record should begin with a Sri Lankan fruiting plant for which bird, bat, or mammal dispersal is documented. The research should then follow the complete sequence from fruit availability to movement, deposition, germination, and recruitment. The candidate should be selected only after the source and geographic context are verified.

## 7. Regenerative conditions

| Condition | Pollination implication | Seed-dispersal implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Seasonal continuity** | Flowers available across relevant periods | Fruits available when dispersers depend on them |
| **Habitat structure** | Nesting, shelter, water, and refuge | Perches, roosts, movement routes, and safe habitat |
| **Plant diversity** | Different flower forms and resources | Different fruiting times, sizes, and dispersal relationships |
| **Low disturbance** | Pollinators can forage and reproduce | Dispersers can move and deposit viable seed |
| **Landscape connectivity** | Flower patches remain reachable | Animals can connect fruiting plants and recruitment sites |
| **Regeneration site** | Pollinated plants can produce viable seed | Dispersed seeds encounter suitable soil and moisture |
| **Stewardship** | Care avoids destroying habitat or seasonal resources | Care protects both dispersers and sites of recruitment |

## 8. Implications for Anavaratha

This cluster strengthens the idea that a living being should be selected because of the relationships it reveals. A flowering plant is not only a plant to place in a garden; it may be part of a seasonal network involving insects, birds, food, seed, and household care. A fruiting tree is not only a source of food; it may depend on animal movement to become a source of future forest.

The project should avoid simplistic language such as “attract wildlife” unless it explains whether the aim is nourishment, refuge, pollination, dispersal, habitat connection, or observation. It should also avoid introducing plants only because they attract generalist species if doing so displaces more appropriate native relationships.

> **Cultivate continuity of relationship, not isolated attraction.**

## 9. Open questions

The next research stage should assemble Sri Lankan flowering calendars, bee and butterfly records, flower-visiting studies, fruiting calendars, bird and bat dispersal evidence, seedling recruitment observations, and habitat-connectivity information. It should compare homegardens, forests, dry-zone habitats, wetlands, agroforestry systems, and urban gardens.

It should also investigate how to monitor relationships ethically and practically, how to distinguish visitation from effective pollination, how to distinguish seed removal from successful dispersal, and how seasonality changes under climate and land-use pressures.
