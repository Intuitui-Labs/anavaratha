# Species cluster 01 — Structural trees and shade–habitat relations

**Anavaratha living-sector atlas — Sri Lanka, working research note, Version 0.1**

## 1. Research question

Which Sri Lankan trees can help us understand structural, shade, habitat, soil, water, cultural, and regenerative relationships—and under what ecological conditions?

This is not a recommendation to plant a particular tree. It is a species-level inquiry into the difference between a tree as an isolated object and a tree as a participant in a living system.

## 2. Why structural trees matter

Structural trees can shape canopy, shade, microclimate, root structure, litter, soil, habitat, food, nesting, and future seed sources. Their effects may extend beyond the tree itself, but their suitability depends on ecological region, elevation, soil, water, disturbance, associated species, and stewardship.

Sri Lankan forest structure varies across habitat and ecological conditions. A peer-reviewed study of a Sri Lankan dipterocarp forest examined species distribution, habitat specialisation, environmental heterogeneity, forest structure, and canopy disturbance [1]. This supports a cautious approach: tree selection should follow habitat understanding rather than a national list of universally desirable species.

## 3. Candidate record A — Kumbuk (*Terminalia arjuna*)

| Field | Preliminary finding |
|---|---|
| **Common name** | Kumbuk |
| **Scientific name** | *Terminalia arjuna* |
| **Other recorded name** | Marutu, Tamil [2] |
| **Working ecological context** | Water-linked or riparian tree candidate; exact Sri Lankan habitat relationships require further verification |
| **Potential relationships to study** | Shade, bank and soil relations, water-edge habitat, associated plants and animals, cultural and agricultural heritage |
| **Evidence status** | Identity and Sri Lankan cultural/heritage presence documented by Dilmah Conservation; ecological function requires stronger place-specific sources |
| **Regenerative question** | Under what river, stream, tank, or wetland-edge conditions does Kumbuk support habitat, shade, soil, water, and community relationships? |

Kumbuk is a useful candidate because its name and presence are documented within a Sri Lankan flora and agricultural-heritage resource [2], and because its wider riparian associations provide a clear hypothesis for studying tree–water–soil relationships. Claims about water purification, water retention, or keystone status should not yet be treated as verified Sri Lankan findings without appropriate evidence.

The Kumbuk record should investigate river and tank edges, root and bank conditions, shade, associated plants, fish and insects, seed and recruitment, seasonal water change, local names, uses, and community stewardship.

## 4. Candidate record B — Hora (*Dipterocarpus zeylanicus*)

| Field | Preliminary finding |
|---|---|
| **Common name** | Hora |
| **Scientific name** | *Dipterocarpus zeylanicus* |
| **Working ecological context** | Lowland wet-zone rainforest and dipterocarp forest candidate; detailed habitat and conservation status require source verification |
| **Potential relationships to study** | Canopy structure, shade, forest succession, habitat, seed, soil, endemism, and fragmented rainforest continuity |
| **Evidence status** | Search results identify it as a Sri Lankan endemic wet-zone tree, but the species-level claims require verification through authoritative botanical or conservation sources before final use |
| **Regenerative question** | What forest conditions, canopy relationships, seed processes, and habitat connections are required for Hora to persist and regenerate? |

Hora represents a different hypothesis from Kumbuk. It should not be selected because it is a large or iconic tree. It is valuable for inquiry because it may reveal wet-zone rainforest structure, endemicity, canopy relationships, and the difficulty of restoring habitat-specific forest systems.

The Hora record should investigate distribution, habitat specialisation, reproductive ecology, seed availability, seedling and sapling recruitment, shade tolerance, associated fauna, forest fragmentation, conservation status, and the ethics of disclosing locations of vulnerable populations.

## 5. Comparative hypothesis

| Question | Kumbuk hypothesis | Hora hypothesis |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary landscape relation** | Water-edge or riparian structure | Wet-zone rainforest structure |
| **Main functional entry** | Shade, soil, water edge, bank, habitat | Canopy, succession, rainforest habitat, endemic continuity |
| **Main risk** | Overgeneralising riparian functions from other regions | Treating a habitat-specific endemic tree as a universal planting species |
| **Research priority** | Verify Sri Lankan habitat and water relationships | Verify distribution, conservation status, regeneration, and habitat specificity |
| **Possible care** | Protect and understand existing river or tank-edge systems | Protect habitat and support natural or assisted regeneration only where appropriate |

These are not competing tree choices. They are two different ways of asking how a structural tree participates in a living landscape.

## 6. What to measure

A structural-tree record should measure more than height or survival. It should document canopy position, shade, soil moisture, soil cover, associated plants, insects and animals, litter, flowering, fruiting, seedling recruitment, seasonal change, human use, and stewardship.

| Level | Suggested indicators |
|---|---|
| **Tree** | Identity, health, size, canopy, flowering, fruiting, seed, and age class |
| **Understory** | Seedlings, saplings, herbs, climbers, grasses, and shade response |
| **Soil** | Cover, moisture, litter, roots, organic matter, visible soil life, and erosion signs |
| **Water** | Distance to water, moisture, flooding, flow, seasonality, and water-edge relation |
| **Biodiversity** | Pollinators, birds, mammals, insects, fungi, and associated plants |
| **Community** | Names, uses, knowledge, care, access, labour, and cultural relation |
| **Landscape** | Patch, corridor, edge, watershed, settlement, and land-use context |

## 7. Working conclusion

The first structural-tree inquiry supports a regeneration-first approach. Anavaratha should first ask whether a place is already regenerating, what tree relationships are present, and what conditions are limiting continuity. Protection, observation, natural regeneration, assisted regeneration, or enrichment may be more appropriate than introducing new trees.

> **A structural tree is not selected for what it looks like alone, but for the living relationships its presence can support in a particular place.**
