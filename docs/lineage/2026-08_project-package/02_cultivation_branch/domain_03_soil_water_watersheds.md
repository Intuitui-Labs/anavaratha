# Domain 03 — Soil, water, and watershed regeneration

## 1. Definition and scope

This domain examines soil, water, and watersheds as interconnected living systems. It includes soil structure and organisms, fertility, erosion, infiltration, groundwater, streams, rivers, tanks, reservoirs, wetlands, rainfall, drought, floods, sediment, land use, climate variability, and the communities whose lives depend on these relationships.

The central question is:

> **How can cultivation support the capacity of Sri Lankan soils and watersheds to retain, circulate, filter, and renew the conditions necessary for life?**

## 2. What the evidence shows

The International Water Management Institute describes Sri Lanka’s upper watersheds as vital “water towers” that support irrigation, drinking water, ecosystems, and hydropower. Its current hydrological study links land use, soil erosion, sedimentation, ecosystem services, water security, climate scenarios, livelihoods, and institutional capacity [1]. This makes clear that soil and water regeneration is not a local gardening question alone; it can involve downstream reservoirs, agriculture, energy, and regional resilience.

A UN Convention to Combat Desertification case study on the Gurugoda Oya Basin describes interactions among watershed degradation, soil erosion, sedimentation, irregular streamflow, drought, land scarcity, poverty, fragmented governance, and limited incentives for upstream communities [2]. It presents Payment for Ecosystem Services as one possible mechanism for connecting downstream beneficiaries with upstream conservation, while also showing that financial mechanisms cannot substitute for coordination, local participation, and fair institutional arrangements.

A Sri Lankan watershed rehabilitation study found through the research process describes forest gardens as a way of combining land rehabilitation, soil moisture, erosion reduction, forest recovery, ecosystem services, and livelihood security [3]. Such examples are valuable not because one model can be copied everywhere, but because they show how cultivation can join ecological and human capacity.

## 3. Soil as a living foundation

Soil should be treated as a living community rather than an inert growing medium. It includes mineral structure, organic matter, roots, fungi, bacteria, invertebrates, water, air, nutrients, and the physical conditions that allow these relationships to function.

A regenerative cultivation approach should investigate compaction, erosion, organic matter, infiltration, moisture retention, fertility, soil organisms, slope, ground cover, disturbance, and seasonal change. It should distinguish between improving soil for short-term plant growth and rebuilding soil processes that support diverse and continuing life.

The question is not simply whether a plant can survive in a location. It is whether the soil can support a diverse living system with limited external inputs and whether cultivation improves or depletes the soil’s future capacity.

## 4. Water as movement and relationship

Water should be studied as a moving relationship rather than a fixed resource. Rainfall, runoff, infiltration, groundwater, springs, streams, rivers, tanks, reservoirs, wetlands, irrigation, evaporation, and coastal discharge form connected processes. Interventions in an upper catchment can change conditions downstream; interventions in a lowland can depend on upstream and seasonal conditions.

Sri Lanka’s traditional tank and irrigation landscapes demonstrate that water systems can be ecological, agricultural, social, and cultural at the same time. Anavaratha should investigate both formal hydrological systems and lived knowledge of rainfall, soil moisture, water storage, cropping, shade, drainage, and seasonal timing.

Cultivation should seek to increase the landscape’s ability to receive water, slow it, hold it, filter it, circulate it, and release it safely. The appropriate action may involve vegetation, soil structure, contour, shade, wetland protection, streamside care, or institutional coordination rather than a single technical structure.

## 5. Watershed scale and proportionality

The watershed is a critical scale because it connects places that may be separated administratively or socially. A field, forest patch, village, reservoir, and river may belong to one hydrological system even when different institutions or communities are responsible for them.

The research should therefore map upstream and downstream relationships, slope and elevation, rainfall and runoff, erosion and sediment, land use, water users, ecosystem services, and seasonal stress. It should ask who receives the benefits of healthy water systems and who performs or pays for the work of maintaining them.

| Scale | Soil-and-water questions |
|---|---|
| **Plot or garden** | What is the soil condition, moisture pattern, slope, and ground cover? |
| **Farm or community** | How are water, fertility, shade, crops, trees, and livelihoods connected? |
| **Catchment** | Where do water and sediment move, accumulate, infiltrate, or leave? |
| **Watershed** | Which upstream and downstream communities, institutions, and ecosystems are connected? |
| **Regional and climatic** | How do monsoons, droughts, floods, climate change, and land-use change alter the system? |

## 6. Regenerative conditions

| Condition | Importance |
|---|---|
| **Ground cover and root systems** | Reduce erosion, support infiltration, protect soil life, and improve moisture conditions |
| **Organic matter and soil biology** | Support fertility, structure, water retention, and plant relationships |
| **Hydrological continuity** | Maintain connections among rain, soil, groundwater, streams, tanks, wetlands, and downstream systems |
| **Slope and land-use fit** | Prevent cultivation practices from increasing erosion or destabilising water systems |
| **Seasonal intelligence** | Match cultivation and care to rainfall, drought, flood, heat, and ecological timing |
| **Upstream–downstream coordination** | Align responsibilities and benefits across the watershed |
| **Community capacity and incentives** | Make long-term stewardship possible for the people who live and work in the landscape |

## 7. Implications for Anavaratha

The soil-and-water domain strengthens Anavaratha’s emphasis on creating conditions rather than imposing outputs. Before planting, the project should understand the site’s soil, slope, water movement, hydrological position, seasonal conditions, and relationships with surrounding land.

A tree may be valuable because its roots stabilise soil, its canopy alters moisture and temperature, its litter feeds soil life, and its presence contributes to a broader water and habitat system. However, the same species or planting pattern may be inappropriate in another context. Regenerative cultivation requires ecological fit.

The core principle is:

> **Cultivate the capacity of landscapes to receive, hold, circulate, and renew life.**

This principle also means that soil and water work must be connected to livelihoods and institutions. A technically effective intervention that cannot be maintained, or that shifts burdens to upstream communities without fair recognition, is not fully regenerative.

## 8. Open questions

The next research stage should compare Sri Lankan soil and agro-ecological classifications; map major watersheds and irrigation systems; study erosion and sedimentation pathways; review traditional and contemporary water-management knowledge; examine forest-garden and agroforestry cases; investigate climate and seasonal change; and identify indicators for soil health, infiltration, water retention, streamflow, and downstream effects.

It should also study the institutional and economic arrangements that make watershed care possible. Anavaratha will need to understand not only how to restore soil and water, but how such restoration can be sustained by the people and institutions connected to the landscape.
