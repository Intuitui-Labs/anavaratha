# Domain 02 — Forests, trees, and natural regeneration

## 1. Definition and scope

This domain examines forests as living, self-organising systems rather than as collections of trees. It includes forest types, native and endemic species, succession, seed banks, natural regeneration, assisted natural regeneration, enrichment planting, forest restoration, community forestry, wildlife relationships, and the long-term care of restored landscapes.

The central question is:

> **What conditions allow Sri Lankan forest systems to recover, diversify, and continue, and what forms of human assistance are proportionate to those conditions?**

## 2. What the evidence shows

Sri Lanka’s ecological variation supports multiple forest types, including rainforests, mountain cloud forests, dry-zone monsoon forests, and arid thorn scrub forests [1]. These forests cannot be treated as interchangeable planting environments. Their species, soils, water relations, disturbance histories, climate, and regeneration processes differ.

A Sri Lankan science article describes Assisted Natural Regeneration as a method that supports naturally emerging seedlings by reducing competition from aggressive vegetation. In this approach, seedlings are not selected entirely by people; many emerge from the existing soil seed bank and may therefore be suited to the site [2]. The article presents the NIFS-Popham Arboretum as a local example in which degraded land developed into dense woodland over approximately two decades, supporting diverse plant and animal life [2].

A peer-reviewed study of community forestry in Sri Lanka’s intermediate zone reports increases in seedling and sapling density and reductions in some disturbances and invasive species. It also shows that results vary with pre-existing forest conditions, implementation period, local perceptions, local decisions, and government policy [3]. Restoration is therefore not a universal technique with a uniform outcome.

## 3. A spectrum of cultivation approaches

Anavaratha should distinguish among different levels of intervention.

| Approach | Basic action | When it may be appropriate |
|---|---|---|
| **Protection** | Prevent further disturbance and allow existing systems to continue | When ecological capacity remains and the main need is security or governance |
| **Natural regeneration** | Remove barriers to recovery while intervening minimally | When seed sources, soil life, moisture, and nearby habitat remain viable |
| **Assisted natural regeneration** | Support naturally emerging seedlings through selective care | When regeneration is occurring but competition, disturbance, or lack of protection limits it |
| **Enrichment planting** | Add selected native species to an existing regenerating system | When particular ecological functions or missing species need support |
| **Restoration planting** | Establish a mixed living system where natural recovery is severely limited | When seed sources, soil, structure, or ecological continuity have been substantially lost |
| **Forest landscape restoration** | Connect ecological recovery with land use, livelihoods, governance, and multiple habitats | When the effects and dependencies extend beyond a single site |

The correct question is not “How many trees can be planted?” It is “What level of assistance does this living system require?” In some places, protection may be the most regenerative action. In others, careful removal of competition, water support, seed work, or mixed native planting may be appropriate.

## 4. Living-system processes

Forest regeneration depends on more than planting and rainfall. Relevant processes include seed availability, dispersal, germination, soil conditions, moisture, shade, succession, herbivory, fire, invasive species, pathogens, human disturbance, wildlife movement, and the relationship between forest edges and surrounding land.

A forest should be studied vertically and horizontally. Vertically, it includes canopy, understorey, ground layer, roots, fungi, soil organisms, and litter. Horizontally, it includes patches, edges, corridors, streams, clearings, settlements, farms, and neighbouring habitats. A planting area that is ecologically isolated may have less regenerative value than a smaller intervention that strengthens connectivity.

Time is also fundamental. The early survival of planted seedlings, the development of canopy, the return of animals, the accumulation of soil organic matter, and the emergence of a self-sustaining forest occur on different timelines. Short-term survival counts cannot stand in for long-term forest function.

## 5. Social and institutional dimensions

Forests are inhabited, used, managed, protected, contested, and remembered by people. Community forestry research in Sri Lanka indicates that local understanding and decision-making interact with government policy and pre-existing forest conditions [3]. Anavaratha should therefore map land tenure, customary use, access, forest departments, local authorities, community groups, farmers, researchers, and other relevant actors before proposing restoration.

Jadav Payeng’s story provides a complementary lesson. His long-term forest-making work is inspiring, but the UCL case study also records conflict and economic impacts for nearby communities as wildlife moved through the restored landscape [4]. A forest can increase biodiversity while simultaneously creating local burdens. Regenerative cultivation must include communication, coexistence, access, livelihood, and distribution—not only vegetation and wildlife.

## 6. Regenerative conditions

The forests-and-trees domain suggests the following conditions for meaningful regeneration:

| Condition | Importance |
|---|---|
| **Ecological fit** | Species and interventions must suit local climate, soil, elevation, hydrology, and disturbance history |
| **Existing regeneration** | Natural seedlings and soil seed banks should be understood before intensive planting begins |
| **Native diversity** | A functioning forest requires relationships among multiple species and ecological layers |
| **Protection and care** | Young regeneration needs appropriate protection from fire, grazing, invasive competition, and damaging disturbance |
| **Landscape connection** | Forest patches need relationship with streams, corridors, edges, and surrounding habitats |
| **Long-term stewardship** | Monitoring, maintenance, local participation, and institutional continuity are essential |
| **Social accountability** | Restoration must consider access, livelihoods, wildlife movement, and local burdens |

## 7. Implications for Anavaratha

Anavaratha should adopt a **regeneration-first** position. Before deciding to plant, it should investigate whether a site is already regenerating, which processes are active, what is limiting them, and what minimum assistance could unlock recovery. This position is consistent with the Sri Lankan mangrove lesson of moving from planting alone toward nurturing the conditions for natural regeneration [5].

The tree remains central to Anavaratha, but as an entry into a living system rather than an isolated output. A tree may create shade, habitat, food, root structure, soil conditions, and future seed sources. Its importance lies in the relationships it enables.

The core forest principle is:

> **Do not plant what the landscape can regenerate; assist what the landscape is already trying to become.**

This should not be interpreted rigidly. Some landscapes may genuinely require active restoration planting. The principle means that intervention intensity should follow ecological diagnosis.

## 8. Open questions

The next research stage should compare Sri Lankan forest types and restoration pathways; identify current evidence on natural regeneration and assisted natural regeneration; examine native species selection by ecological region; investigate forest-edge and corridor conditions; study community forestry and local governance; review restoration monitoring methods; and map the difference between short-term planting success and long-term forest recovery.

It should also ask where the language of “forest creation” may conceal existing community-managed landscapes, natural regeneration, or local ecological knowledge. Anavaratha should learn to recognise regeneration before claiming to produce it.
