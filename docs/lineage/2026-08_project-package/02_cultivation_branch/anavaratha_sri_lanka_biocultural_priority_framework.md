# Anavaratha

## Sri Lankan biocultural priority framework

**Living-sector descent — Working framework, Version 0.1**

## 1. Purpose

This framework identifies how Anavaratha can explore living beings in Sri Lanka without reducing them to a list of species or a hierarchy of human usefulness. It connects ecological identity with habitat, relationship, cultural meaning, medicinal and food knowledge, historical memory, conservation status, and regenerative possibility.

Sri Lanka’s biological richness is associated with ecological, climatic, soil, and topographical variation, and the country contains forest, agricultural, aquatic, marine, coastal, and wetland systems [1]. The living-sector exploration must therefore remain geographically and ecologically specific.

## 2. The priority question

The question is not simply:

> **Which plants, animals, or insects should Anavaratha select?**

It is:

> **Which living beings and relationships reveal the conditions, capacities, histories, and possibilities of regeneration in a particular Sri Lankan landscape?**

A priority may be selected because it is ecologically foundational, highly connected, endangered, endemic, historically significant, medicinally important, culturally related, locally recovering, or capable of revealing a larger system.

## 3. Biocultural priority clusters

### 3.1 Endemic, endangered, and irreplaceable beings

Sri Lanka contains high levels of endemism, and IUCN Sri Lanka’s work includes conservation of endemic freshwater fish, priority-species assessment, habitat surveys, and the protection of coastal species and habitats [1] [2]. These beings should be studied through habitat and relationship rather than through status labels alone.

The research record should ask where the species occurs, what conditions it requires, how its population is changing, what other beings and habitats support it, what threats it faces, and whether the appropriate response is protection, habitat restoration, connectivity, propagation, monitoring, or another form of care.

### 3.2 Structural and shade-bearing trees

Trees can create canopy, shade, cooler microclimates, root structure, soil protection, habitat, food, nesting space, litter, and future seed sources. A shade tree should not be selected only for speed of growth or visual appearance. It should be investigated through local ecological fit, water requirements, associated species, natural regeneration, cultural meaning, and long-term maintenance.

The relevant unit is often the tree-in-community: tree, soil, fungi, insects, birds, shade, water, people, and future seedlings.

### 3.3 Water-preserving and soil-building beings

Plants, roots, fungi, microorganisms, earthworms, insects, and other organisms may contribute to infiltration, soil structure, organic matter, decomposition, nutrient cycling, erosion reduction, and water retention. These functions should be investigated in relation to slope, rainfall, soil, watershed position, and season.

“Water-preserving” should not become a vague label. The research should specify whether the claimed relationship concerns shade, infiltration, soil cover, root structure, groundwater recharge, stream protection, wetland function, or another mechanism.

### 3.4 Pollinators, seed dispersers, and attractor plants

Flowering plants, fruiting trees, insects, birds, bats, and other animals may form relationships of pollination and seed dispersal. An attractor plant is meaningful only in relation to the beings it supports, the season in which it flowers or fruits, and the habitat conditions around it.

Anavaratha should therefore map attraction as a network: which plants support which pollinators or dispersers, when the relationship occurs, what shelter and water are available, and whether the resulting seed or fruit cycle contributes to regeneration.

### 3.5 Medicinal plants and Osu Uyana

Medicinal plants connect biodiversity with health, local knowledge, cultivation, household practice, livelihoods, and cultural memory. The phrase **Osu Uyana** should be investigated carefully as a Sri Lankan reference to medicinal-plant gardens or herbal-garden contexts. Its precise meanings, institutional uses, Sinhala and Tamil terms, local variations, and relationship to indigenous and Ayurvedic knowledge should be verified through Sri Lankan sources and knowledge holders.

Medicinal value creates responsibilities. The atlas should record identity, cultivation conditions, parts used, preparation traditions, sustainability, propagation, access, knowledge ownership, consent, and evidence. It should not encourage harvesting of rare plants or present traditional use as a medical guarantee.

### 3.6 Food plants and living nutrition

Food plants include cultivated crops, homegarden species, wild foods, fruit trees, leafy plants, seeds, tubers, spices, and other organisms connected to nutrition and household resilience. They should be studied through diversity, seasonality, seed continuity, labour, taste, culture, markets, soil, water, and access.

A food plant may simultaneously support pollinators, soil, shade, seed exchange, household income, and cultural identity. The research should preserve this multiplicity rather than assign it one function.

### 3.7 Historically and culturally related beings

Some plants and animals are connected to stories, ritual, place, medicine, agriculture, religion, memory, craft, or identity. These relationships may reveal why a community protects or cultivates a being, how knowledge is transmitted, and how ecological change is experienced.

Such knowledge must be documented with consent, attribution, context, and reciprocity. A cultural relationship should not be extracted and repackaged as project branding without returning recognition or value.

### 3.8 Soil life and less-visible beings

Fungi, bacteria, earthworms, termites, mites, nematodes, and other soil organisms are essential to the living sector even when they are not visually prominent. Their work includes decomposition, nutrient cycling, soil aggregation, root association, and the creation of conditions for plant growth.

The atlas should include methods suitable for less-visible life, including soil observation, laboratory evidence where available, local knowledge, indicator plants, and changes in soil structure and function.

## 4. The priority-selection matrix

| Priority dimension | Low | Medium | High |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ecological importance** | Limited known role | Supports one or more functions | Supports many relationships or foundational conditions |
| **Irreplaceability** | Widespread or replaceable | Locally important | Endemic, restricted, rare, or difficult to replace |
| **Vulnerability** | Stable or adaptable | Sensitive to some pressures | Threatened, declining, or highly specialised |
| **Biocultural significance** | Limited documented relationship | Local use or memory | Strong medicinal, food, historical, ritual, or identity relationship |
| **Regenerative leverage** | Isolated benefit | Supports a small network | Strengthens habitat, soil, water, species, and community capacity together |
| **Evidence quality** | Unverified | Partial or dated | Strong, current, and place-specific |
| **Stewardship feasibility** | No clear responsible caretaker | Possible caretaker with constraints | Rights, knowledge, care, and long-term support are present |

The matrix is not a numerical ranking of life. It is a way to explain why a being or relationship has been selected for deeper inquiry.

## 5. What must not be simplified

An endangered species is not automatically a suitable planting target. A medicinal plant is not automatically safe to harvest. A fast-growing tree is not automatically ecological. An insect described as harmful may be part of a wider food web. An introduced species may have different effects in different habitats. A culturally valued species may require protection rather than commercialisation.

Each priority must therefore carry its evidence, context, uncertainty, and ethical conditions.

## 6. Proposed Sri Lankan atlas layers

| Atlas layer | Content |
|---|---|
| **Identity layer** | Names, taxonomy, synonyms, Sinhala/Tamil terminology, and confidence |
| **Habitat layer** | Ecological region, habitat, elevation, soil, water, and range |
| **Status layer** | Endemicity, threat, protection, population, and date of assessment |
| **Function layer** | Shade, soil, water, pollination, dispersal, habitat, food, medicine, or other role |
| **Relationship layer** | Associated plants, animals, insects, fungi, people, and institutions |
| **Biocultural layer** | Stories, history, ritual, food, medicine, livelihood, and place |
| **Seasonal layer** | Flowering, fruiting, breeding, migration, dormancy, harvest, and care periods |
| **Stewardship layer** | Existing caretakers, practices, rights, knowledge holders, and institutions |
| **Regeneration layer** | Natural recovery, propagation, protection, habitat creation, and monitoring options |
| **Uncertainty layer** | Evidence gaps, contested claims, missing data, and unresolved ethical questions |

## 7. First research sequence

The first pass should not attempt to catalogue every living being in Sri Lanka. It should select a small number of representative relationship clusters across ecological regions and study them deeply.

A suitable sequence would begin with a structural tree or plant, then map its shade, soil, water, pollinator, seed-disperser, medicinal or food, cultural, and stewardship relations. A second sequence could begin with an endangered or endemic species and work outward into its habitat and human context. A third could begin with an Osu Uyana or medicinal-plant context and examine biodiversity, knowledge, propagation, and ethical use.

This cluster-based method is more likely to reveal regenerative relationships than a flat inventory of names.

## 8. Working definition

> **A Sri Lankan biocultural priority is a living being or relationship whose ecological role, vulnerability, cultural meaning, medicinal or food significance, historical association, or regenerative potential makes it important for understanding and supporting the continuity of life in a particular place.**
