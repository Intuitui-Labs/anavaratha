# Domain 07 — Urban and peri-urban ecological cultivation

## 1. Definition and scope

This domain examines ecological cultivation within and around Sri Lankan settlements. It includes urban trees, home gardens, school grounds, community gardens, wetlands, waterways, public landscapes, habitat patches, food-growing spaces, pollinator habitats, shade, recreation, ecological education, and the relationships between built environments and living systems.

The central question is:

> **How can urban and peri-urban spaces become more hospitable to biodiversity, food, wellbeing, water, shade, and long-term ecological care?**

## 2. What the evidence shows

An IWMI feature on Colombo describes urban wetlands as integral parts of the city, with relationships to wellbeing, flood regulation, water, air, recreation, biodiversity, and everyday life [1]. It presents a planning vision in which green spaces are connected through wetlands and waterways, and in which wetland parks can support both ecological function and social connection.

An IUCN case study of the Metta Garden south of Colombo describes a small urban/peri-urban site combining organic food production, an urban forest, and habitat for butterflies, bees, birds, and other insects [2]. The case reports ecological observation before intervention, subsequent increases in recorded butterfly diversity, food production, seed and plant sharing, training, and community learning. The significance of the case is not that every urban space should reproduce the same design. It is that a small site can become a multifunctional living space when cultivation is designed for both human and non-human life.

Research on Muthurajawela Marsh and Negombo Lagoon further shows how urban wetland systems can provide flood attenuation, water purification, carbon sequestration, fish nursery functions, and other ecosystem services while facing pressure from land-use change [3].

## 3. Urban cultivation as ecological infrastructure

Urban cultivation should be understood as ecological infrastructure, not merely beautification. Trees can provide shade, habitat, food, cooling, and social space. Wetlands can support water regulation and biodiversity. Gardens can provide food, pollinator habitat, soil life, learning, and community connection. Streams and canals can become ecological corridors when their edges are protected and cultivated appropriately.

At the same time, urban systems are constrained by land prices, fragmented ownership, infrastructure, safety, access, maintenance, heat, water availability, and competing uses. A regenerative urban intervention must understand these constraints rather than assume that unused-looking land is available for cultivation.

## 4. Small sites and larger networks

Small urban sites can act as leverage points, but their value depends on relationships beyond the site. A garden may provide habitat and seed sources. A school may transmit ecological knowledge across families. A wetland edge may connect water, birds, people, and public space. A line of trees may support shade and movement while linking larger green areas.

Anavaratha should therefore study urban cultivation at several levels:

| Scale | Questions |
|---|---|
| **Household or plot** | Can the space support food, shade, soil life, habitat, and care? |
| **Institution** | Can schools, workplaces, temples, or community facilities sustain cultivation and learning? |
| **Neighbourhood** | Are there linked gardens, wetlands, waterways, trees, and shared stewardship? |
| **City** | How do green spaces, flood systems, wetlands, transport, heat, and development interact? |
| **Peri-urban region** | How are farms, forests, wetlands, settlements, and watersheds connected? |

## 5. Human and non-human access

The idea of an urban garden “for all” should be interpreted literally. People need access for food, movement, exercise, learning, rest, and social connection. Other species need food, water, shelter, nesting, flowering plants, soil, and seasonal continuity.

These needs can reinforce one another, but they may also conflict. Public access can disturb habitat. A planting scheme designed for people may reduce native diversity. A wetland park may protect an edge while altering local access. Anavaratha should make these trade-offs visible and design with them rather than assuming that every green intervention benefits all beings equally.

## 6. Regenerative conditions

| Condition | Importance |
|---|---|
| **Ecological fit** | Plants and habitat must suit the urban microclimate, soil, water, and local species |
| **Multifunctionality** | Cultivation should support ecological, social, food, educational, and wellbeing functions where appropriate |
| **Connectivity** | Small sites should be considered in relation to wetlands, waterways, gardens, trees, and wider habitat networks |
| **Accessibility** | People should be able to use and learn from the space without degrading its ecological function |
| **Maintenance** | Care responsibilities, funding, water, tools, and knowledge must be realistic |
| **Baseline observation** | Existing species, soil, water, shade, heat, and use should be understood before intervention |
| **Long-term continuity** | The site should be able to survive changes in leadership, funding, ownership, and use |

## 7. Implications for Anavaratha

Urban and peri-urban cultivation may be one of the most accessible ways for Anavaratha to explore regenerative capacity without reducing the project to a symbolic planting event. It can make ecological relationships visible in everyday life and connect food, habitat, shade, wellbeing, learning, and stewardship.

The project should especially study the difference between a **green object** and a **living urban system**. The former is installed; the latter is observed, tended, adapted, inhabited, and connected. The former can be measured by area or number of plants; the latter requires attention to species, soil, water, use, care, and time.

The core principle is:

> **Cultivate urban spaces as shared habitats, not as decorative surfaces.**

## 8. Open questions

The next research stage should map Colombo and other Sri Lankan urban areas through wetlands, waterways, homegardens, public green spaces, school grounds, urban heat, food-growing spaces, and biodiversity patches. It should examine land tenure, planning policy, maintenance, access, safety, public use, and existing community initiatives.

It should also compare small gardens, wetland parks, school cultivation, urban forests, homegardens, and peri-urban agroforestry as different forms of ecological infrastructure. The important question is not which type is universally best, but which type fits which urban condition.
