# ANAVARATHA — Evaluation Framework
## Knowing Whether Something Actually Changed

> **The governing commitment:** An honest negative result is more valuable than a dishonest positive one. Anavaratha's evaluation framework exists to tell the truth about what happened — not to justify what was done. If the intervention didn't work, the evaluation is the most important output.

---

## 0. Why Evaluation Is Hard Here

Standard evaluation frameworks ask: did the intervention achieve its stated outputs? Did it reach the target population? Was the budget spent correctly?

These are the wrong questions for Anavaratha.

Anavaratha intervenes in dissipative, autopoietic, holarchic systems. That means:
- The system continuously reorganises around interventions in unpredictable ways
- Cause and effect are separated in time, space, and scale — attribution is genuinely hard
- The things that matter most (reservoir exergy, cultural memory, attentional capacity) are hardest to measure
- A "successful" intervention can displace costs rather than eliminate them
- The system may appear unchanged while undergoing fundamental structural shift

Anavaratha's evaluation framework is built for this reality — not for a counterfactual experimental world where systems sit still while you measure them.

## 1. What Evaluation Asks in Anavaratha

Four questions, in order of importance:

### Question 1 — Did the reservoir condition change?
The ultimate test. Not: did we do what we planned to do? But: did the pressure reservoir we were trying to restore actually improve in measurable ways?

Reservoir condition = capacity to absorb dissipation before collapse.

This is measured differently for each reservoir type:
- **Ecological reservoir:** Species diversity, SOC, water quality, acoustic complexity
- **Cultural reservoir:** Ritual participation, knowledge transmission, governance activity
- **Sensory reservoir:** Soundscape ACI, visual greenness index, quiet hour availability
- **Human exergy reservoir:** Attention quality, care network density, nature contact frequency

### Question 2 — Did the dissipation slow, or just relocate?
This is the displacement test. An intervention that cleans up one river by pushing pollutants to another river has not reduced dissipation — it has moved it. An intervention that restores a tank by drawing labour away from a community's own food production has not increased exergy — it has redistributed it.

Track: where did the cost go after the intervention? Who bears it now that didn't before?

### Question 3 — Was our theory of change correct?
Every intervention design includes a theory of change: *if we do X, we expect Y because Z*. Evaluation tests whether Z was true — whether the causal mechanism we assumed actually operated.

When the theory of change fails (Y didn't follow from X), this is the most valuable evaluation finding — it updates our model of how these systems work, which improves every future intervention.

### Question 4 — What did the system teach us that we didn't know?
This is the open question. What did the intervention reveal about the system that the diagnostic didn't show? What was surprising? What confirmed existing understanding? What contradicted it?

This question is what transforms evaluation from accountability to learning.

## 2. Evaluation Design Principles

### Baseline before intervention
You cannot know what changed if you don't know where you started. The baseline measurement is part of the intervention design — not an afterthought. If a baseline isn't possible, document why and what the implications are for the evaluation's validity.

### Multiple indicators, multiple methods
No single measure captures reservoir condition or dissipation rate. Triangulate:
- Quantitative data (water levels, species counts, SOC measurements)
- Qualitative data (community perception, narrative change, governance activity)
- Sensory data (soundscape recordings, visual landscape assessment)
- Relational data (who is talking to whom, what knowledge is flowing)

### Temporal honesty
Different things change at different rates. Water quality can change in weeks. SOC takes years. Cultural knowledge transmission takes generations. The evaluation timeline must be honest about what can and cannot be assessed within it.

Design evaluation at three temporal scales:
- **Short-term (0–2 years):** Process outputs, immediate physical changes, early relationship indicators
- **Medium-term (2–10 years):** Reservoir condition trends, governance capacity, cultural practice participation
- **Long-term (10+ years):** Structural change in dissipation rates, intergenerational knowledge transmission

Anavaratha evaluations should be explicit about which timescale they operate in and what they cannot assess.

### Participatory assessment
The people in the system are the most sensitive instruments for detecting change. Community-held perception of whether their reservoir is recovering is data — not anecdote. Combine with quantitative measurement, don't replace it.

Community participatory evaluation also functions as a governance feedback loop — it gives communities a formal mechanism to assess whether the intervention served them.

### Honest uncertainty
Evaluation in complex systems produces probabilistic knowledge, not certainty. Report findings with appropriate confidence levels. Distinguish between "we observed X" and "X was caused by the intervention" and "X will persist."

## 3. Indicator Framework

### Layer 1 — Ecological Reservoir Indicators

| Reservoir | Primary Indicator | Secondary Indicators | Measurement Method |
|---|---|---|---|
| Water / cascade | Tank exergy-corrected storage | Water quality (BOD, DO, turbidity), cascade connectivity | Irrigation Dept data + CEA water quality + field survey |
| Soil | Soil organic carbon (SOC) | Microbial biomass, aggregate stability, earthworm density | Laboratory analysis (University of Peradeniya) |
| Biosphere | Species diversity index (Shannon H) | Threatened species trend, habitat connectivity | Field surveys, eBird, IUCN Red List |
| Soundscape | Acoustic Complexity Index (ACI) | Biophonic/technophonic ratio, dawn chorus richness | AudioMoth recordings + librosa analysis |
| Visual ecology | Green View Index | NDVI, visual clutter index, horizon integrity | Satellite (GEE) + street-level photography |

### Layer 2 — Cultural Reservoir Indicators

| Reservoir | Primary Indicator | Secondary Indicators | Measurement Method |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ritual practice | Participation rate in maintenance rituals | Frequency of ritual, intergenerational participation | Community survey + observation |
| Ecological knowledge | Knowledge retention score | Breadth of knowledge, transmission rate to younger generation | Structured knowledge assessment |
| Governance capacity | Active collective decisions per year | Attendance at governance meetings, conflict resolution rate | Governance activity log |
| Craft knowledge | Practitioners per 1000 community members | Age distribution of practitioners, apprentice rate | Community survey |

### Layer 3 — Human Exergy Indicators

| Domain | Primary Indicator | Secondary Indicators | Measurement Method |
|---|---|---|---|
| Attentional capacity | Self-reported attention quality score | Hours of silence experienced daily, nature contact frequency | Weekly micro-survey (KoboToolbox) |
| Care network | Care network density | Reciprocal care acts per week, care network reach | Social network survey |
| Somatic capacity | Self-reported body ease score | Sleep quality, physical pain, energy availability | Weekly micro-survey |
| Collective efficacy | Belief that collective action can change conditions | Recent collective action history, trust in community governance | Community survey |

### Layer 4 — Asymmetry Indicators

| Dimension | Indicator | Measurement Method |
|---|---|---|
| Cost distribution | Who bears ecological cost (by income quintile, community type) | HIES data + environmental exposure mapping |
| Benefit distribution | Who receives ecological benefit (by income quintile, community type) | Payments, access rights, ecosystem service use survey |
| Visibility change | Has the cost bearer's experience become more visible to decision-makers? | Policy document analysis, media analysis, stakeholder survey |
| Power shift | Has the decision-making authority over resources moved toward cost bearers? | Governance analysis, legal analysis |

## 4. The Displacement Test

This deserves its own section because it is the most commonly missed evaluation failure.

After every intervention, systematically ask:

**Where did the cost go?**
- Did ecological cost move to a different geographic location?
- Did it move to a different community (one less visible or less powerful)?
- Did it move forward in time (a fix that works now but produces worse problems later)?
- Did it move to a different domain (fixing water quality by increasing air pollution)?

**Method:**
1. Map the system boundary used in the intervention design
2. Expand the boundary by one level (holarchic principle)
3. Look for condition changes in the expanded boundary during the intervention period
4. Ask community members in the expanded boundary what they have observed

If costs have relocated rather than reduced, the intervention's success metric is false. Document this honestly. It is one of the most important findings an Anavaratha evaluation can produce.

## 5. The Theory of Change Test

For each intervention, the theory of change was: *if we do X, we expect Y because Z.*

Evaluation tests each component:

**Did X actually happen?**
Was the intervention implemented as designed? If not, why not? What changed in implementation and what were the implications?

**Did Y follow?**
Did the expected outcome occur? At what magnitude? At what timeline? In what form?

**Was Z true?**
Was the causal mechanism we assumed actually operating? This is the hardest question and the most important. A failed theory of change that reveals a false causal assumption is a contribution to knowledge — it improves future intervention design for everyone.

**Document the theory of change test as a distinct section of every evaluation report.** Not buried in findings. Prominent. Honest.

## 6. Community Participatory Evaluation Protocol

### Before the intervention begins
- Facilitated community session: what does success look like to you? What would you need to see to know this worked?
- Document community-defined success indicators alongside Anavaratha-defined indicators
- Establish community evaluation committee with authority to assess and report

### At mid-point
- Community evaluation committee meets
- Reviews observations against community-defined success indicators
- Documents concerns, unexpected changes, things Anavaratha hasn't noticed
- This input feeds the adaptive management process — the intervention can change based on this

### At completion
- Full community evaluation session
- Community presents their assessment before Anavaratha presents ours
- Explicit discussion of divergence between community and Anavaratha assessments
- Community evaluation is published alongside Anavaratha's evaluation — not summarised by us

### Community evaluation questions
1. Has your relationship to this ecosystem changed? How?
2. What has improved? What has gotten worse?
3. Has the cost of ecological degradation shifted — toward you or away from you?
4. Do you have more or less capacity to make decisions about this landscape?
5. What do you wish Anavaratha had done differently?
6. What do you wish Anavaratha had not done?
7. Should Anavaratha continue here? Do something different? Leave?

## 7. Reporting Standards

### What every Anavaratha evaluation report contains

**Section 1 — What we set out to do**
The intervention design, theory of change, and intended outcomes — as designed before implementation.

**Section 2 — What we actually did**
Honest account of implementation — what happened as planned, what didn't, why not.

**Section 3 — What changed**
Indicator data before and after. Quantitative where possible, qualitative always. Uncertainty quantified.

**Section 4 — The displacement test**
What happened at and beyond the system boundary. Where did costs go?

**Section 5 — Theory of change assessment**
Was X implemented? Did Y follow? Was Z true? Honest verdict on each.

**Section 6 — Community assessment**
Community evaluation committee findings, verbatim where possible, unfiltered.

**Section 7 — What we learned**
What the system taught us that we didn't know before. What we would do differently. What this changes about how Anavaratha understands these systems.

**Section 8 — What comes next**
Re-entry decision. Recommended next interventions. What should be left alone.

### What evaluation reports do NOT do
- Claim success not supported by indicator data
- Bury negative findings in appendices
- Present community concerns as footnotes to Anavaratha's conclusions
- Omit the displacement test
- Present probabilistic findings as certainties

## 8. The Learning Loop

Evaluation feeds back into every layer of Anavaratha:

**Into the diagnostic tools** — new understanding of how these systems behave improves future diagnostics

**Into the leverage point methodology** — false theories of change update the leverage point framework

**Into the intervention typology** — interventions that didn't produce their intended effects refine when each type is appropriate

**Into the data infrastructure** — evaluation reveals which indicators were most sensitive, which data sources were most useful, which gaps mattered most

**Into the concept doc** — fundamental surprises about how life buys time under pressure update the framework's core understanding

The evaluation is not the end of the intervention. It is the beginning of the next one.
