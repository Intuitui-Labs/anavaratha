# ANAVARATHA — Intervention Typology
## The Forms That Intervention Takes

> **What this document is:** A catalogue of the specific types of intervention Anavaratha designs and executes. Each type has a distinct mechanism, a distinct appropriate context, distinct requirements, and distinct risks. Choosing the wrong type for the right leverage point is as ineffective as choosing the right type for the wrong leverage point.

---

## The Organising Logic

Anavaratha's intervention types are organised by their primary mechanism of action — *how* they slow dissipation or restore reservoir capacity, not *what domain* they operate in. The same mechanism can operate across water, soil, sound, cultural, or human exergy domains.

**Five primary mechanisms:**

| Mechanism | What it does | Intervention types |
|---|---|---|
| **Slowing** | Extends the time a flow takes to move through the system | Flow Retardation, Pause Architecture |
| **Coupling** | Reconnects a symbol or signal to its material consequence | Feedback Restoration, Visibility Design |
| **Storing** | Rebuilds the capacity of a pressure reservoir | Reservoir Restoration, Knowledge Archiving |
| **Aligning** | Re-attunes human systems to ecological rhythm | Ritual Reinstatement, Seasonal Re-coupling |
| **Redistributing** | Shifts who bears cost and who receives benefit | Asymmetry Correction, Care Amplification |

## Type 1 — Flow Retardation Interventions

### What it is
Physical, institutional, or cultural interventions that slow the rate at which a resource or quality flows out of a system. Entropy accelerates gradients — slowing the flow buys time.

### Mechanism
Increases residence time of a valuable flow. Water stays longer in the landscape. Knowledge stays longer in the community. Attention stays longer with the ecological reality.

### Forms this takes

**Physical slowing:**
- Check dams, bunds, contour trenches that slow water movement across a degraded catchment
- Windbreaks and living fences that slow soil movement
- Acoustic barriers that slow noise propagation into quiet zones

**Institutional slowing:**
- Moratoriums on extraction while a reservoir recovers
- Deliberation processes that slow decision-making to the pace of ecological consequence
- Tenure arrangements that create cost for rapid land-use change

**Cultural slowing:**
- Reviving seasonal rhythms that pace human activity to ecological cycles
- Craft practices that slow consumption by embedding attention in production
- Contemplative practices that slow attentional throughput

### When appropriate
When the primary problem is velocity — the flow is not inherently wrong, it is moving too fast for the system to adapt. The tank cascade is designed as a flow retardation system at landscape scale. Most ecological restoration contains flow retardation as a component.

### Requirements
- Understanding of the flow's natural pace vs. current pace
- Political/social permission to slow what economic actors want to accelerate
- Maintenance capacity — most physical slowing structures require ongoing care

### Risks
- Slowing one flow can accelerate another (slow water in one place → flash flooding elsewhere)
- Excessive slowing reduces the adaptive capacity of the system (the central paradox)
- Creates new dependencies if maintenance isn't embedded in local capacity

## Type 2 — Feedback Restoration Interventions

### What it is
Interventions that restore the connection between an action and its consequence — when that connection has been broken by distance, time delay, institutional abstraction, or deliberate obscuration.

### Mechanism
Shortens or clarifies the feedback loop. The person whose action causes dissipation can perceive the cost. The institution whose policy degrades a reservoir can see the degradation. The market that externalises ecological cost must internalise some signal of it.

### Forms this takes

**Perceptual feedback:**
- Placing real-time water quality data in the public view of communities downstream of polluters
- Making visible the sonic environment before and after a development project
- Showing farmers the SOC trend in their soil over time

**Institutional feedback:**
- Requiring environmental impact to be presented in exergy terms, not just monetary value
- Linking institutional budgets to ecological condition of the territories they govern
- Publishing real-time reservoir condition dashboards accessible to decision-makers

**Economic feedback:**
- Payments for ecosystem services that make visible what was previously free to degrade
- Liability structures that connect polluter to cost bearer
- Procurement policies that embed ecological cost in price

### When appropriate
When the primary problem is information — the people making decisions that cause dissipation genuinely cannot perceive the cost, because it lands elsewhere in space or time. Most visibility gradient problems call for feedback restoration.

### Requirements
- Credible, accessible measurement system — feedback only works if the signal is trusted
- Political will to surface information that powerful actors may prefer invisible
- Communication design that makes the feedback legible across the visibility gradient

### Risks
- Feedback without agency produces despair, not behaviour change
- Corrupted feedback signals (corporate-funded "environmental monitoring") can make things worse
- Feedback loops have their own delays — design must account for the lag

## Type 3 — Reservoir Restoration Interventions

### What it is
Direct investment in rebuilding the capacity of a degraded pressure reservoir — ecological, sensory, cultural, or human.

### Mechanism
Increases the system's buffer capacity. A restored reservoir can absorb more dissipation before collapse, giving the system more time to adapt.

### Forms this takes

**Ecological reservoir restoration:**
- Tank desilting and cascade reconnection
- Wetland and mangrove rehabilitation
- Soil organic carbon rebuilding through cover cropping, biochar, reduced tillage
- Soundscape restoration through quiet zone designation and enforcement

**Cultural reservoir restoration:**
- Reviving maintenance practices tied to ecological systems (tank festivals, forest rituals)
- Documenting and transmitting ecological knowledge held by elders
- Restoring craft practices that embed ecological relationship in daily life
- Rebuilding community governance capacity for resource management

**Human exergy restoration:**
- Creating conditions for attention recovery — silence, nature contact, reduced cognitive load
- Rebuilding care networks that have been eroded by economic pressure
- Supporting contemplative and somatic practices that restore nervous system capacity

### When appropriate
When the reservoir is measurably degraded and the rate of erosion exceeds the system's self-recovery capacity. When a reservoir crosses the threshold from declining to critically low. When the early warning function shows proximity to irreversibility.

### Requirements
- Baseline measurement of current reservoir condition — you need to know where you're starting
- Understanding of the reservoir's natural recovery dynamics — how fast does it restore under what conditions?
- Sustained commitment — reservoir restoration takes longer than reservoir degradation
- Community ownership of the restoration — external restoration without internal maintenance fails

### Risks
- Restoration of one reservoir can mask continued degradation of others
- Ecological restoration without governance restoration produces temporary gains
- Creating dependency on Anavaratha for ongoing maintenance defeats the purpose

## Type 4 — Visibility Design Interventions

### What it is
Designing the symbolic, visual, and narrative infrastructure that makes previously invisible flows visible — creating the categories and representations through which a community can name what is being taken from them.

### Mechanism
Shifts what can be perceived. New perception creates new possibility for response. The symbolic layer comes before the political layer — you cannot fight for what you cannot name.

### Forms this takes

**Symbolic infrastructure:**
- Developing local language for ecological concepts that don't yet have names in the community's vocabulary
- Creating visual representations of flows and reservoirs that are legible to multiple audiences simultaneously
- Building participatory mapping processes that make community ecological knowledge visible to the community itself

**Narrative infrastructure:**
- Documenting and telling the story of a system's historical trajectory — what it was, what changed, what the cost has been
- Creating comparison frames — this river vs. that river, this generation vs. the last
- Making the accounting identity visible: here is what was taken, here is what was kept, here is the gap

**Data infrastructure:**
- Building community-held monitoring systems that don't depend on external institutions
- Creating dashboards that surface the data that matters to the people bearing the cost
- Publishing gap documentation — making the absence of data itself visible as information

### When appropriate
When the primary problem is invisibility — the dissipation is real but unperceived, the theft is happening but unnamed. Often the first intervention needed, before any other type is possible.

### Requirements
- Deep listening before design — you cannot design visibility for someone else's experience
- Trust that the community's perception is valid data, not anecdote
- Communication design capacity — symbols, narratives, and visualisations that actually work across the visibility gradient

### Risks
- Visibility without agency produces trauma, not transformation — always pair with capacity for response
- Imposing external categories onto local experience is a form of epistemic extraction
- Over-representation (flood of data and visuals) can produce new cognitive overload — a form of attentional entropy

## Type 5 — Governance Activation Interventions

### What it is
Interventions that rebuild or reactivate the collective decision-making capacity of a community over its own resources and ecological conditions.

### Mechanism
Restores the operational closure of the community's autopoietic system — its ability to reproduce its own governance without external dependence.

### Forms this takes

**Formal governance:**
- Supporting communities to establish or reclaim legal rights over resources they manage
- Building community-level monitoring and enforcement capacity
- Creating institutional linkages between community governance and formal government systems

**Informal governance:**
- Reviving traditional resource management arrangements that have been disrupted
- Rebuilding the social trust and shared norms that governance depends on
- Creating forums for collective deliberation that didn't previously exist or had collapsed

**Knowledge governance:**
- Establishing community protocols for who holds what knowledge and how it is transmitted
- Creating community data sovereignty — communities own and control data about their own territory
- Building capacity for communities to evaluate and respond to external claims about their environment

### When appropriate
When the system is governed badly or not governed — when the autopoietic capacity of the community to maintain its own ecological relationships has been damaged by colonial disruption, economic pressure, or institutional neglect.

### Requirements
- Long timeline — governance capacity is not rebuilt quickly
- Genuine respect for community authority — Anavaratha must be genuinely willing to be told what to do (and not do) by community governance structures
- Understanding of the historical disruptions that damaged governance capacity in the first place

### Risks
- Creating governance structures that are externally designed and therefore fragile
- Formalising governance in ways that undermine informal practices that were working
- Providing governance capacity that serves Anavaratha's agenda rather than the community's

## Type 6 — Care Amplification Interventions

### What it is
Small, precisely placed actions that amplify existing acts of care within a system — working with the negentropic efforts already present rather than introducing entirely new ones.

### Mechanism
Identifies existing negentropic practices — individuals or communities already doing the work of slowing dissipation and maintaining reservoirs — and reduces friction, increases resources, or increases visibility around those practices.

### Forms this takes

**Material amplification:**
- Providing tools, materials, or resources to communities already engaged in ecological stewardship
- Removing barriers (financial, regulatory, informational) that limit existing care practices
- Creating market or recognition mechanisms that make existing care economically viable

**Social amplification:**
- Connecting practitioners across sites so they can learn from and support each other
- Making existing care practices visible to broader audiences — documentation, storytelling
- Building networks that allow knowledge to flow between practitioners

**Institutional amplification:**
- Advocating for policies that recognise and support existing community ecological management
- Creating certification or recognition mechanisms for traditional ecological practices
- Linking community stewards to research institutions who can provide technical support

### When appropriate
When the system already contains people doing the right things — and the problem is that their work is invisible, under-resourced, or isolated. Almost always the right first move after the diagnostic, because it builds trust and produces early wins.

### Requirements
- Careful listening to identify what care already exists — don't assume the system is entirely broken
- Respect for the autonomy of existing practitioners — amplify their practice, not yours
- Restraint — the temptation is to transform what you amplify. Don't.

### Risks
- Amplifying the wrong practices (those that appear caring but are actually extractive)
- Creating dependency on Anavaratha's amplification rather than building independent capacity
- Making care visible in ways that expose practitioners to political risk

## Combining Types

Most real interventions combine multiple types. Common combinations:

**Reservoir restoration + Governance activation**
Restore the physical reservoir while simultaneously rebuilding the governance capacity to maintain it. One without the other fails — restored tanks silt again without maintained governance; governance without restored tanks has nothing to govern.

**Visibility design + Care amplification**
Make existing care practices visible, then amplify them. Visibility first — so the community sees what they already have — then amplification that builds on genuine existing capacity.

**Feedback restoration + Flow retardation**
Restore the feedback signal (people can see what their actions cause) and simultaneously slow the flow (so the system has time to respond to the feedback). Feedback without time to respond produces helplessness.

## Type Selection Guide

| Primary problem | First intervention type | Supporting types |
|---|---|---|
| System moving too fast to adapt | Flow Retardation | Governance Activation |
| People can't see cost of their actions | Feedback Restoration | Visibility Design |
| Reservoir critically degraded | Reservoir Restoration | Governance Activation |
| Cost is invisible, unnamed | Visibility Design | Care Amplification |
| Governance capacity collapsed | Governance Activation | Visibility Design |
| Care exists but unsupported | Care Amplification | Feedback Restoration |
