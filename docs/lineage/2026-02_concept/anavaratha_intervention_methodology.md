# ANAVARATHA — Intervention Design Methodology
## From Diagnostic Insight to Designed Action

> **The governing principle:** Anavaratha intervenes where the diagnostic shows a small action has a large negentropic effect. Not everywhere. Not randomly. Not because the intervention is beautiful in theory. Because the light showed us exactly where to put our hands.

---

## 0. What This Document Is

This is the operational methodology for Anavaratha as an actor — not a witness, not an analyst, but a designing, intervening presence in a system.

It assumes the diagnostic work is done or underway. The flows are named. The reservoirs are mapped. The asymmetries are visible. Now the question is: **what do we do?**

This methodology is not a linear pipeline. It is a set of phases that fold back on each other — understanding deepens through action, action is redesigned through understanding. The five modes (See, Name, Map, Measure, Touch) remain active throughout. Touching changes what can be seen.

---

## 1. The Intervention Arc

Every Anavaratha intervention moves through six phases. They are not always sequential. They are always present.

```
ENTER → DIAGNOSE → IDENTIFY → DESIGN → ACT → EVALUATE → (re-enter)
```

Each phase has a distinct posture, distinct questions, distinct outputs. None can be skipped. Skipping diagnosis produces well-intentioned harm. Skipping evaluation produces invisible outcomes — which is exactly what Anavaratha exists to counter.

---

## 2. Phase 1 — Enter

### What this phase is
The moment of arrival in a system. How Anavaratha enters determines everything that follows. A wrong entry — too fast, too certain, too extractive — damages the trust that intervention depends on and accelerates the very dissipation we came to slow.

### The posture
**Witness before actor.** Enter as someone who does not yet know what needs doing, even if the data suggests otherwise. The data is always incomplete. The people in the system know things no sensor captures.

### Key questions
- What is the history of interventions in this system? What was promised? What was taken? What remains?
- Who has standing to invite us in, and who doesn't? Whose permission matters and whose is being assumed?
- What does our presence cost the system? What does it consume — attention, time, trust, resources?
- What are we bringing in that we might not be aware of? (Assumptions, frameworks, aesthetics, power)

### Outputs
- Entry relationship map — who we are in relation to, what we owe, what was agreed
- Initial system narrative — what the people in the system say about it, before we say anything
- Honest position statement — who Anavaratha is, what we intend, what we don't know, what we won't do

### What entry is NOT
Entry is not a needs assessment conducted on a community. It is not extraction of information in exchange for a vague promise of future benefit. It is not presenting our framework before listening to theirs.

## 3. Phase 2 — Diagnose

### What this phase is
Deploying the full analytical apparatus — data, analysis, visualisation — to understand the system's flows, reservoirs, asymmetries, and pressure points. This phase draws on the ML Guide, Algorithms Guide, and the Sri Lanka Data Sources.

### The posture
**Rigorous humility.** The diagnostic is always incomplete. It is always a partial model of a dissipative, autopoietic, holarchic reality. The moment we mistake the diagnostic for the territory, we are lost.

### Key questions
- Where is dissipation accelerating? Who pays for it?
- Where are the pressure reservoirs? What is their current capacity? What is their trajectory?
- What is the visibility gradient — who sees the benefit, who absorbs the cost, which senses are excluded?
- Where is entropy exploiting delay? What thresholds are approaching that aren't yet visible?
- What is the exergy quality of this system, and in which direction is it moving?

### Diagnostic layers

**Thermodynamic layer**
Material and energy flows. Input-output accounting. Where the official ledger ends and the unaccounted dissipation begins.

**Operational layer**
How the institutions and ecological processes are actually functioning. Not how they say they function. Field observation, not document review.

**Structural layer**
The nested wholes. How damage at one level is propagating to others. What is absorbing pressure that shouldn't have to.

**Sensory layer**
What the official data cannot see — the soundscape, the visual ecology, the olfactory environment, the attentional load. Often the earliest signal of reservoir erosion.

**Human exergy layer**
The quality of human capacity in this system — attention, care, cultural memory, community governance. Is it being consumed faster than it regenerates?

### Outputs
- Diagnostic map — visual rendering of flows, reservoirs, and asymmetries
- Reservoir condition assessment — current status and trajectory of each pressure reservoir
- Visibility gradient analysis — who sees what, who pays what
- Threshold proximity assessment — how close to irreversibility?
- Gap documentation — what the diagnostic cannot see and why

## 4. Phase 3 — Identify Leverage Points

### What this phase is
The analytical bridge between diagnosis and design. Reading the diagnostic output to find where a small, well-placed action has a large negentropic effect. This is not intuition — it is structured analysis. See the Leverage Point Identification Guide for the full methodology.

### The posture
**Selective, not comprehensive.** The diagnostic will reveal more problems than any intervention can address. The discipline is in choosing — and being honest about why we're choosing what we're choosing.

### Key questions
- Where in this system does a small slowing of flow preserve the most reservoir capacity?
- Where does reconnecting a symbol to its material consequence change the most behaviour?
- Where is the coupling between human exergy and ecological exergy weakest — and most recoverable?
- What is the minimum effective intervention? What is the maximum harm a poorly designed intervention could cause?

### The leverage point hierarchy (adapted from Meadows)
In descending order of power — highest leverage first:

1. **The goal of the system** — what is this system optimising for? If it can be reoriented, everything else shifts. Hardest to change. Most powerful if changed.
2. **The rules** — formal and informal governance, tenure arrangements, cultural norms about who can do what
3. **Information flows** — who gets what feedback, when, in what form. Making the invisible visible IS intervention.
4. **Delays** — slowing or shortening the lag between action and consequence. Entropy exploits delay; intervention shortens it.
5. **Negative feedback loops** — strengthening the mechanisms that self-correct. Restoring governance capacity, reactivating ritual maintenance cycles.
6. **Material stocks and flows** — the physical infrastructure. Slowest to change but sometimes most necessary.

### Outputs
- Ranked leverage point map — where in this system is intervention most powerful?
- Intervention opportunity brief — 3–5 specific opportunities with preliminary feasibility assessment
- Non-intervention list — what we explicitly will not touch and why

## 5. Phase 4 — Design

### What this phase is
Turning a leverage point opportunity into a specific, actionable, evaluable intervention design. This is where the typology (see Intervention Typology document) is applied — choosing the right form for the right leverage point.

### The posture
**Minimum footprint, maximum effect.** Anavaratha is not trying to rebuild the system. It is trying to slow the dissipation enough for the system's own autopoietic capacity to recover. The intervention should do less than it's tempted to do.

### Design principles

**Reversibility first**
Design interventions that can be undone if they cause unintended harm. The holarchic nature of systems means an action at one level has unpredictable effects at others. Build in the ability to reverse before committing.

**Work with existing flows, not against them**
Find the places where the system is already trying to self-correct and amplify those, rather than imposing a new direction entirely. Anavaratha is not smarter than the system.

**Distribute the exergy gain**
If the intervention restores reservoir capacity, who benefits? Design explicitly so that benefit flows toward those who have been absorbing cost — not toward those who have been extracting benefit.

**Embed evaluation from the start**
The intervention design includes its own evaluation methodology before action begins. Not retrofitted afterward. The baseline measurement happens before the intervention, not after.

**Document assumptions explicitly**
Every design rests on assumptions about how the system works. Name them. They will be tested by reality. When reality contradicts an assumption, that is information — receive it.

### The design document structure
For each intervention, produce:

1. **Leverage point** — which specific opportunity this addresses
2. **Intervention type** — from the typology
3. **Theory of change** — if we do X, we expect Y because Z. Explicit causal logic.
4. **Actors** — who does what, with what authority, with what accountability
5. **Timeline** — realistic, with decision points where the intervention can be adjusted or stopped
6. **Resources** — what this consumes (time, money, attention, trust)
7. **Baseline measures** — what we will measure before, during, and after
8. **Risk assessment** — what could go wrong, what would we do if it does
9. **Exit conditions** — what does responsible completion or stopping look like?

## 6. Phase 5 — Act

### What this phase is
The intervention itself. Anavaratha in the world, doing the thing.

### The posture
**Present and adaptive.** No intervention survives contact with reality unchanged. The design is a hypothesis. Reality tests it. Adaptation is not failure — rigid adherence to a design when reality contradicts it is failure.

### During the intervention

**Maintain the witnessing layer**
Even while acting, keep seeing. Document what is happening — not just outputs but the quality of what's changing. Are reservoirs recovering? Is human exergy being restored or consumed? Is the visibility gradient shifting?

**Track unintended effects**
Every intervention causes things it didn't intend. Some are positive — unexpected negentropic gains. Some are negative — new costs displaced onto someone not in the original design. Track both.

**Hold decision points**
At pre-agreed milestones, pause and ask: is the theory of change holding? Is the system responding as expected? Are the assumptions proving correct? A decision point is not bureaucracy — it is the feedback loop that prevents compounding harm.

**Communicate openly**
With the people in the system. What we expected, what we're seeing, what we're not sure about. Transparency is itself a negentropic act — it reconnects symbol to material reality.

## 7. Phase 6 — Evaluate

### What this phase is
Honest assessment of what changed, what didn't, why, and what comes next. See the Evaluation Framework document for the full methodology.

### The posture
**Radical honesty over institutional face-saving.** Anavaratha's credibility rests on its willingness to say clearly when something didn't work. An honest negative result is more valuable than a dishonest positive one — it contributes to the collective understanding of how these systems actually behave.

### Key questions
- Did the reservoir capacity change in the direction we expected?
- Did the dissipation slow, accelerate, or displace to a different location?
- Who benefited? Who bore unexpected costs?
- Was our theory of change correct? If not, where was it wrong and why?
- What does the system look like now compared to when we entered?
- What should the next intervention be, if any?

### The re-entry decision
Evaluation doesn't end the relationship. It informs the decision about whether and how to re-enter. Some systems need time to integrate what happened before further intervention is helpful. Some need immediate follow-up. Some need Anavaratha to leave and not return. All three are legitimate outcomes.

## 8. Cross-Cutting Principles

These apply across all six phases:

### The minimum effective dose
The smallest intervention that produces the needed negentropic effect. Bigger is not better. More is not more. Anavaratha does not have an interest in its own expansion — only in the system's recovery.

### The exit is designed before entry
Before entering any system, know what responsible completion looks like. What we will have done. What we will leave behind. What we will take with us. What we will not extract.

### Power flows with the work
Every intervention transfers something — knowledge, capacity, tools, frameworks. Design explicitly so that what's transferred stays in the system and builds local capacity, rather than creating dependency on Anavaratha's continued presence.

### Incompleteness is honesty
No intervention fully succeeds. The accounting identity never fully closes. The evaluation will always reveal things that didn't work, things that were missed, things that remain. This is not failure — it is the honest nature of intervening in dissipative, autopoietic, holarchic reality.

---

## 9. What Anavaratha Does Not Do in Intervention

- Does not enter without invitation from those bearing the cost (not just those holding authority)
- Does not design interventions for communities without their participation in the design
- Does not promise outcomes it cannot control
- Does not stay beyond its welcome or its usefulness
- Does not extract data, stories, or knowledge without explicit agreement about use
- Does not claim credit for recovery that the system did itself
- Does not treat failure as something to hide
