# ANAVARATHA — Leverage Point Identification Guide
## Reading the Diagnostic to Find Where to Put Your Hands

> **The discipline:** The diagnostic will always reveal more problems than any intervention can address. The leverage point guide is about the discipline of choosing — finding the place where a small, well-designed action produces a disproportionately large negentropic effect. Everything else is the courage to not intervene everywhere.

---

## 0. What a Leverage Point Is

A leverage point is a place in a system where a small shift produces large changes in the system's overall behaviour.

In Anavaratha's terms: a leverage point is where the minimum intervention buys the maximum time.

Not all leverage points are equal. Some produce large effects quickly but don't last. Some produce smaller effects that compound over time. Some look powerful but only move symptoms, not the underlying dynamic. The discipline is in reading which is which.

**What a leverage point is NOT:**
- The most visible problem (visibility ≠ leverage)
- The most urgent crisis (urgency ≠ leverage)
- The place that's easiest to access (accessibility ≠ leverage)
- The place where the most money is going (resource intensity ≠ leverage)

## 1. The Leverage Point Hierarchy

Adapted from Donella Meadows, reframed for Anavaratha's dissipative-autopoietic-holarchic ontology.

Listed from **most powerful** to **least powerful**. Counter-intuitive: the most powerful leverage points are the hardest to identify and change. The least powerful are the most obvious and commonly targeted.

### Level 1 — The Goal of the System (Most Powerful)
**What:** What is this system optimising for? What does it consider success?

**Why it's most powerful:** If you change what the system is trying to do, every other element reorganises around the new goal. GDP as the goal of Sri Lanka's economic system means ecological cost is structurally invisible. Change the goal — even partially — and the information flows, incentives, and behaviours all shift.

**How to identify:** Ask: what does this system reward? What does it punish? What gets measured and celebrated? The answers reveal the actual goal, regardless of the stated mission.

**How to intervene:** Introduce alternative success metrics. Create constituencies for the alternative goal. Make the current goal's costs visible enough that it loses legitimacy.

**Example:** A tank cascade system governed by the goal "maximise paddy output" treats siltation as maintenance cost. The same system governed by the goal "maintain cascade exergy" treats siltation as existential threat. Same tanks, different goal, different governance.

---

### Level 2 — The Rules of the System
**What:** Who has power to do what. Formal law, informal norm, cultural expectation, tenure arrangements, access rights.

**Why it's powerful:** Rules determine who can change the system and how. Rules that exclude the people bearing ecological cost from decisions about ecological management are self-reinforcing — they produce outcomes that justify their own continuation.

**How to identify:** Ask: who has the right to do what to this landscape? Who doesn't? What happens when those without rights try to exercise them? What happens when those with rights exceed them?

**How to intervene:** Community rights recognition. Regulatory reform. Norm shifting through visibility design and narrative. Creating new forums where different rules apply.

**Example:** Vaddha community's traditional relationship to the forest is a rule system — informal, ancestral, but real. Colonial law replaced it with a different rule system. Both are intervention points.

---

### Level 3 — Information Flows
**What:** Who gets what information, when, in what form.

**Why it's powerful:** Systems cannot self-correct what they cannot perceive. Missing information is a structural problem, not an accident — information flows are often deliberately designed to serve powerful actors' interests.

**How to identify:** Ask: who knows what about this system? Who doesn't know what they need to know? Where does the feedback loop have the longest delay? What information is collected but not shared? What information nobody collects?

**How to intervene:** This is where Anavaratha's entire data infrastructure layer lives. Visibility design, feedback restoration, community monitoring, dashboard creation. Making the invisible visible IS a leverage point intervention.

**Example:** Estate workers don't know the soil organic carbon trend under their feet. The plantation company does, and doesn't share it. Giving estate workers access to that data changes the information flow — which changes what's possible politically.

---

### Level 4 — The Strength of Negative Feedback Loops
**What:** The mechanisms that keep a system in balance — the self-correcting dynamics.

**Why it's powerful:** Weakening these is how systems get into trouble. Strengthening them is how systems recover. Negative feedback loops are the autopoietic capacity of the system — its ability to maintain itself.

**How to identify:** Ask: what used to regulate this? What self-correcting mechanisms existed before? Why did they weaken? Is there residual capacity to strengthen them?

**How to intervene:** Governance activation — restoring the community capacity that maintained the cascade, the forest, the fishery. Ritual reinstatement — the festival that used to pace the extraction is the cultural form of a feedback loop. Reservoir restoration that rebuilds the physical capacity to absorb variance.

**Example:** The traditional tank festival (*vel vidane*) was not just ceremony — it was the feedback loop that mobilised collective maintenance of the cascade before siltation became critical. Reviving it is a feedback loop intervention.

---

### Level 5 — The Length of Delays
**What:** The time between action and consequence.

**Why it's powerful:** Entropy exploits delay. The longer the delay between a damaging action and its visible consequence, the more damage accumulates before correction is possible. Shortening delays is among the most tractable interventions.

**How to identify:** Ask: how long between the action that causes damage and the damage becoming visible? How long between damage becoming visible and institutional response? How long between response and measurable change?

**How to intervene:** Early warning systems. Real-time monitoring. Predictive indicators that signal future conditions now. Making threshold proximity visible before the threshold is crossed.

**Example:** Soil organic carbon loss takes 30–100 years to become visible as crop failure. By the time the farmer can see the problem, the reservoir is gone. Monitoring SOC annually shortens the delay between action (tillage, agrochemical use) and consequence visibility.

---

### Level 6 — The Structure of Material Stocks and Flows (Least Powerful — Most Commonly Targeted)
**What:** The physical infrastructure — how much is stored where, how fast it moves.

**Why it's least powerful:** Stocks and flows are the physical reality of a system. They change slowly. They respond to all the higher-level leverage points. Changing them directly (building a dam, planting trees, cleaning a river) can be valuable but rarely changes the system dynamics that created the problem.

**How to identify:** The most visible element of most ecological problems. The degraded tank. The eroded hillside. The polluted river.

**How to intervene:** Physical restoration — but only as part of a package that also addresses higher-level leverage points. Physical intervention without governance, information, and goal change produces temporary gains.

**The warning:** Most external interventions target Level 6. Most money goes to Level 6. Most visible results are at Level 6. And most gains at Level 6 are reversed within a generation because Levels 1–5 are unchanged.

## 2. Reading the Diagnostic for Leverage

### Step 1 — Identify the active failure modes
From the diagnostic outputs, list every place where dissipation is accelerating, reservoirs are eroding, or thresholds are approaching.

Don't filter yet. Just list.

### Step 2 — Map failure modes to their mechanism
For each failure mode, ask: *why is this happening at a systemic level?*

Not: "the tank is silting because sediment washes in."
But: "the tank is silting because the upslope catchment has lost its vegetative cover, because land tenure changes made long-term investment in soil conservation unviable, because the feedback loop that connected upstream farmers to downstream tank condition was severed when communal governance was replaced by individual title."

The answer to "why at a systemic level" points to a leverage point level.

### Step 3 — Score leverage points

For each identified leverage opportunity, score on three dimensions:

| Dimension | Question | Score 1–5 |
|---|---|---|
| **Power** | How much does change here shift the overall system? | 5 = fundamental shift |
| **Tractability** | How accessible is this leverage point to Anavaratha? | 5 = directly actionable |
| **Reversibility** | If our intervention fails, how recoverable is the system? | 5 = easily recoverable |

**Combined score = Power × (Tractability + Reversibility)**

This formula weights power heavily but requires that tractability and reversibility are both present. A powerful but completely inaccessible leverage point scores low. A tractable but low-power intervention also scores low.

### Step 4 — Select the intervention portfolio

Choose 2–3 leverage points that form a coherent portfolio:
- At least one higher-level leverage point (Levels 1–3)
- At least one lower-level leverage point (Levels 4–6) that produces early visible results
- Together, they address the same underlying system dynamic from multiple angles

The higher-level intervention does the lasting work. The lower-level intervention builds trust and demonstrates competence while the higher-level work matures.

### Step 5 — Document what you're NOT intervening in and why

This is not optional. The discipline of choosing requires the discipline of naming what you're leaving. Every system has more problems than you can address. Naming what you're not touching — and why — is part of intellectual honesty and prevents scope creep that dilutes every intervention.

## 3. Leverage Point Red Flags

**Signs you may be targeting the wrong leverage point:**

🚩 **The intervention feels obvious and uncontroversial**
Real leverage points disturb something. If everyone agrees the intervention is a good idea, ask what it's leaving undisturbed.

🚩 **The intervention is primarily physical**
Physical interventions at Level 6 are often the most visible and the least systemic. Ask what Level 3–5 problem is producing the Level 6 symptom.

🚩 **The primary beneficiary is not the person bearing the cost**
Leverage point selection often unconsciously serves the most powerful or most visible stakeholders. Check: who benefits if this intervention succeeds?

🚩 **The timeline is shorter than the system's natural timescale**
If the leverage point requires results within one project cycle (1–3 years) but the system dynamic operates on decadal timescales, you're probably targeting symptoms not causes.

🚩 **The intervention requires the community to change but not the extractors**
Leverage points that place all the adaptive burden on the communities bearing cost while leaving the sources of dissipation unchanged are not leverage points — they are cost displacement.

## 4. The Minimum Effective Intervention Principle

For every leverage point identified, ask: what is the **minimum** intervention that produces the needed effect?

This is not about being cheap. It is about:
- **Respecting the system's own capacity** — systems have autopoietic intelligence. The minimum effective intervention works *with* that intelligence rather than replacing it.
- **Limiting unintended consequences** — smaller interventions disturb fewer dimensions of the system simultaneously, making unintended effects more traceable.
- **Building trust before scale** — a small intervention that works builds the relationship and credibility for larger work. A large intervention that fails damages both.
- **Leaving capacity in the system** — an intervention that consumes more community attention and energy than it restores is net negative even if its specific goal succeeds.

**Test:** Could this intervention be half the size and still produce the needed effect? If yes, make it half the size. Test again.
