# Anavaratha: Learning, Leverage, and Action in a Constrained Waste System

**Date:** 22 August 2026. **Focus:** Sri Lanka’s material and waste cycles. **Question:** If public authorities are struggling under resource and institutional constraints, what can Anavaratha learn, do, and leverage from outside the formal system?

## 1. The central conclusion

Authorities’ struggles do not make Anavaratha irrelevant. They define the problem more clearly. The project should not begin by asking how to replace the entire waste-management system or how to make Sri Lanka resemble a high-income country. It should ask where a relatively small improvement in **knowledge, coordination, visibility, trust, material quality, or burden reduction** can make the existing system more capable.

The key distinction is between **capacity substitution** and **capacity augmentation**. Anavaratha is unlikely to substitute for local authorities’ legal mandates, collection fleets, disposal land, or public budgets. It can augment the system by making hidden flows visible, connecting fragmented actors, improving evidence quality, reducing contamination at a critical point, strengthening accountability, and testing low-cost interventions that are compatible with local conditions.

> The leverage point is not necessarily the largest facility. It may be the smallest missing connection between a material flow, an operational decision, an ecological receiver, and an accountable actor.

## 2. What we can learn from the current evidence

| Evidence pattern | System lesson | Anavaratha learning question |
|---|---|---|
| Western Province has a formal master plan and a 49-LA database initiative, but the raw data are not publicly available | A system can possess planning architecture without shared operational visibility | What minimum data must be public and reconciled for a facility to be governable? |
| The WMA plan records 28 composting facilities but also contamination, odour, leachate, weak maturation, space constraints, and residues | Facility count is a poor proxy for usable capacity | Which bottleneck—feed quality, labour, space, equipment, markets, or residuals—actually limits output? |
| The 22-station study reports 2,391.3 t/month running capacity but only 157 t/month production | Nominal and running capacity can coexist with very low realized transformation | Where does the gap arise in the process, and which intervention changes it most cheaply? |
| Some transfer stations unload waste on the ground before reloading | A transfer point can become a hidden disposal interface | Can ground handling, spillage, and residual movement be measured and reduced without major capital works? |
| Emergency waste was dumped beside a formal compost facility after Meethotamulla | Crisis pathways may be physically adjacent to formal facilities but absent from facility inventories | How can temporary and overflow disposal be captured in the spatial record? |
| 21 open-dump symbols appear on the WMA map, while 22 LA-operated disposal sites are reported elsewhere | Different inventories and map products may not reconcile | Which sites are missing, renamed, closed, transferred, or still active? |
| Rehabilitation, project handover, and public plans coexist with unresolved current status | Project completion is not persistence | What evidence proves that an intervention continues to operate after the donor or project exits? |
| Informal recovery is part of the material system | The formal system is not the whole system | How can informal workers’ contribution and exposure be recognized without criminalizing or displacing them? |

## 3. Where Anavaratha has leverage

### A. Information leverage

The first and most immediate leverage is **making the system legible**. A source-traceable facility register, pathway map, status history, and evidence-quality field can connect records that currently sit in separate institutions. This is not merely documentation. When a facility has no current status, no throughput record, no residual destination, or no inspection history, the missing field identifies a concrete governance failure.

The minimum viable public record is not a large dashboard. It is a small, durable dataset with facility identity, geometry, stream, status, design capacity, actual intake, output, residual destination, last verification date, licence, inspection, maintenance, and evidence source. A reliable small register is more useful than a visually impressive but unvalidated national map.

### B. Measurement leverage

The 22-station evidence shows that the most revealing metric may be the **conversion gap**:

> usable output ÷ accepted input, alongside downtime, contamination, stock, rejects, and residual destination.

Anavaratha can create a common measurement grammar for facilities without pretending to create a national score. The aim is to make different facilities comparable only where the boundary, period, units, and measurement method match.

A practical first protocol would measure one complete month at a selected facility: incoming mass, accepted mass, rejected mass, process losses, product output, closing stock, sales, operating days, equipment downtime, staff attendance, and residual destination. This would reveal more than a nominal capacity claim.

### C. Coordination leverage

The Western Province’s challenge is not only lack of resources; it is fragmentation. Local authorities, WMA, CEA, SLLRDC, provincial councils, operators, informal workers, markets, and communities hold different parts of the system. Anavaratha can provide a neutral **shared evidence language** in which each actor’s role and constraint is visible.

The leverage is to translate between institutions: a map for planners, a flow ledger for facility managers, a burden map for communities, a source register for researchers, and a status-and-duty table for regulators. These are different views of one system, not separate projects.

### D. Material-quality leverage

Many capacity failures begin before the facility. Contamination, mixed waste, poor source separation, and inconsistent feedstock reduce the value of composting and recycling. Therefore, a small intervention at a generator or collection interface can increase effective capacity more than a larger downstream machine.

The relevant question is not “Can we build another facility?” but “Can the existing facility receive a cleaner, more predictable stream?” Candidate interventions include narrowly targeted source separation, market-specific collection, contamination feedback, simple weighing, or a route-level feedback loop. These should be tested as small controlled pilots, not assumed to work universally.

### E. Residual and ecological leverage

A system is not successful merely because it diverts material from a dump. Residues, leachate, ash, rejects, and unsold products can transfer burden elsewhere. Anavaratha can create leverage by following the material beyond the facility label and identifying the final receiver.

This is where the project connects Freedom from Waste to Freedom to Flourish. The immediate objective is to reduce exposure, leakage, accumulation, unsafe work, and system failure. The longer-term objective is to protect the ecological conditions that allow communities and non-human life to flourish. Restoration should not be promised before the burden pathway is understood.

### F. Accountability leverage

The law–capacity–reality chain provides another leverage point:

> duty → capacity → detection → enforcement → remedy → persistence.

A rule without capacity may be unenforceable. Capacity without detection is invisible. Detection without enforcement produces no change. Enforcement without remedy leaves the burden. Remedy without persistence allows recurrence. Anavaratha can identify which link is weak at each site rather than treating “compliance” as a single binary variable.

## 4. What we can do from the desk

| Desk output | Immediate use | Later leverage |
|---|---|---|
| Current facility-status register | Separates operating, idle, planned, historical, closed, conflicted, and unknown | Targets data requests and field verification |
| Hidden-dumping and pathway register | Captures temporary dumping, ground transfer, residuals, and overflow | Reveals burden-transfer mechanisms |
| Capacity conversion-gap table | Distinguishes design, running, intake, output, and residuals | Identifies low-cost bottlenecks |
| Source and evidence catalogue | Makes every claim auditable | Builds institutional trust |
| 21-symbol open-dump map lead layer | Preserves historical spatial evidence without false precision | Enables georeferencing and currentness checks |
| Official data-request pack | Seeks raw database, permits, inspections, and logs | Tests whether information capacity can be opened |
| Satellite-screening protocol | Screens visible site change after polygons are obtained | Prioritizes field visits |
| System typology | Compares dense, distributed, centralized, outsourced, and transition systems | Prevents one locality from being treated as representative |

## 5. What to test locally later

A local pilot should not try to solve all waste. It should test one mechanism in a bounded flow. Good pilot questions include:

1. Can a facility’s monthly mass balance be made reliable enough to expose its true bottleneck?
2. Can cleaner feedstock increase actual compost output without increasing labour or capital beyond local means?
3. Can a transfer station reduce open-ground handling through a simple operational redesign?
4. Can a residual route be traced from a treatment facility to its final receiving environment?
5. Can informal workers be included in measurement and safety improvement without losing their livelihood?
6. Can a closure or rehabilitation intervention be shown to persist after the project ends?
7. Can a community complaint, facility log, and regulatory inspection be joined into one verifiable case record?

Each pilot should have a baseline, intervention, mechanism, outcome, unintended effect, and persistence check. A pilot that produces a positive result but cannot explain why, for whom, or for how long is weak evidence.

## 6. What not to do

Anavaratha should not begin by building a composite national risk score from incomplete data. It should not treat OSM points, map symbols, public allegations, or facility labels as proof of illegal dumping. It should not assume that high-income-country infrastructure is automatically appropriate. It should not romanticize informal adaptation or accept ecological burden as the unavoidable price of poverty. It should not measure success only by tonnes diverted, because diversion can become relocation.

It should also avoid becoming a parallel bureaucracy that asks local authorities to produce data they cannot maintain. The data design must be modest enough to survive: a small number of definitions, a clear weighing rule, a monthly reconciliation, and an evidence trail.

## 7. Practical leverage strategy

The recommended sequence is:

| Stage | Action | Success condition |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Make visible | Publish the source-traceable facility and pathway register | Every claim has a source, period, boundary, and uncertainty status |
| 2. Find the break | Compare accepted input, output, rejects, stock, downtime, labour, and residuals | The bottleneck is named rather than guessed |
| 3. Connect actors | Present the same flow to facility staff, authorities, regulators, workers, and communities | Each actor can see its role and constraint |
| 4. Test modestly | Run one bounded operational or source-separation intervention | Change is measurable without synthetic data or false precision |
| 5. Follow the burden | Track residuals, leachate, rejects, exposure, and ecological receivers | No “diversion” claim is accepted without downstream accounting |
| 6. Check persistence | Re-measure after the intervention or project period | The result survives beyond the initial effort |
| 7. Replicate selectively | Apply only to systems with matching conditions | Replication is based on mechanism, not enthusiasm |

## 8. Anavaratha’s distinctive contribution

The project’s distinctive contribution need not be owning a truck, constructing a landfill, or issuing a national policy. It can be a rigorous **material-cycle intelligence practice** that combines:

- material-flow accounting;
- facility-capacity reality;
- spatial pathway mapping;
- law, enforcement, and institutional duty;
- ecological receiving environments;
- worker and community exposure;
- evidence quality and uncertainty;
- and persistence after intervention.

This combination can reveal where apparently different problems are actually connected. A low compost output may be a collection problem. A collection problem may be a vehicle-maintenance problem. Maintenance may be a procurement or finance problem. The resulting residual may be a hidden dumping problem. The dump may be located near a wetland because land scarcity and political power shaped the choice. The project should follow that chain instead of stopping at the facility gate.

## Final answer

Authorities’ constraints are not the end of Anavaratha’s relevance. They are the terrain in which leverage must operate. The project can:

1. **make hidden flows visible;**
2. **separate nominal capacity from usable capacity;**
3. **identify the smallest binding bottlenecks;**
4. **connect fragmented institutions and affected communities;**
5. **follow residual burdens to ecological receivers;**
6. **test modest, context-compatible interventions;**
7. **protect informal and vulnerable actors from being erased;**
8. **and measure whether improvements persist.**

The most important strategic shift is from asking, “How do we fix Sri Lanka’s waste problem?” to asking:

> **Where, in this particular material cycle, can better knowledge, coordination, or practice reduce the greatest burden with the least additional resource—and how can we prove that the benefit persists?**

That is a realistic and powerful form of leverage.
