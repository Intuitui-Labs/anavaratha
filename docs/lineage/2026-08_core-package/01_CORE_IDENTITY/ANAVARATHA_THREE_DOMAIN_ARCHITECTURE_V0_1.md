# Anavaratha: Three-Domain Architecture

**Version:** 0.1  
**Date:** 25 August 2026  
**Geographic focus:** Sri Lanka  
**Author:** Manus AI

## 1. Purpose

Anavaratha is a Sri Lanka–focused research and intelligence project about the afterlives of materials, the burdens produced when material cycles fail, and the conditions under which human and ecological systems can regenerate and flourish.

The project has three distinct domains:

1. **Freedom from Waste**;
2. **Material Afterlives**;
3. **Ecological Flourishing**.

They are connected, but they are not interchangeable. Waste reduction is not the same as reuse. Reuse is not the same as ecological restoration. A facility can improve one domain while worsening another. The project must therefore keep separate ledgers, questions, evidence, and ethical tests for each domain.

## 2. Master formulation

> **Anavaratha studies how materials move from use through disuse, circulation, transformation, residuality, and ecological relation; how institutions and infrastructures preserve or dissipate material potential; how burdens are distributed across people and places; and where practical forms of knowledge, coordination, repair, care, and restoration can expand freedom and flourishing in Sri Lanka.**

The project is grounded in material-flow analysis and spatial evidence, informed by thermodynamic humility, ecological metabolism, industrial ecology, political ecology, environmental justice, resilience, information, and the capability approach. These frames are held together without reducing social values to physical variables.

## 3. The three domains

### 3.1 Domain I — Freedom from Waste

**Freedom from Waste** examines the negative burdens imposed by unmanaged or poorly managed material residues. It asks how people, workers, communities, and ecosystems may be exposed to accumulation, leakage, open dumping, open burning, odour, leachate, unsafe work, nuisance, forced proximity, uncertain responsibility, and repeated institutional failure.

The domain does not assume that waste can disappear or that every non-recovered material is a policy failure. It asks which burdens are unavoidable, which are reducible, which are produced by institutional choices, and who or what receives them.

**Primary question:**

> **How can involuntary exposure to destructive residual burdens be reduced without simply transferring those burdens to less visible people, places, or ecosystems?**

**Objects of study include:** waste generation and collection; transfer; treatment; residuals; disposal; hidden or temporary dumping; open burning; leachate; unsafe work; compliance–reality gaps; and burden transfer.

**Core measures include:** accepted and rejected material; uncollected material; residual quantities; stock accumulation; downtime; exposure; receiving environments; complaints; inspections; remedies; and persistence.

### 3.2 Domain II — Material Afterlives

**Material Afterlives** examines things that are no longer needed by their current holder but may still have use-value, exchange-value, repair value, component value, cultural value, or recoverable material value. The domain begins before an object becomes waste.

The preferred public label is **Material Afterlives** because it is broad enough to include reuse, repair, resale, donation, exchange, sharing, remanufacture, refurbishment, component recovery, salvage, and eventual material recycling or disposal. **Latent utility** is a central concept within the domain, not the whole domain name.

**Primary question:**

> **When does “no longer needed by me” become “available for another,” and what allows or prevents that value from circulating?**

**Objects of study include:** used goods; repairable objects; surplus institutional assets; construction materials; clothing; furniture; appliances; electronics; tools; books; vehicles; spare parts; reusable packaging; informal exchange; repair economies; reuse depots; libraries of things; material banks; and matching infrastructures.

**Core measures include:** time out of use; condition; repairability; compatibility; location; ownership; trust; information completeness; demand; transaction cost; safety; transport; repair labour; price; reuse duration; and end-of-afterlife residuals.

Material Afterlives is not automatically virtuous. Reuse may involve unsafe goods, exploitative labour, long transport, energy-intensive repair, unequal access, or eventual residuals. Every afterlife must therefore be evaluated for material, social, economic, and ecological consequences.

### 3.3 Domain III — Ecological Flourishing

**Ecological Flourishing** examines the conditions under which ecosystems and more-than-human communities can regenerate, maintain function, support biodiversity, sustain soil and water relations, and expand future possibilities for life. It includes trees, forests, wetlands, waterways, soils, habitats, and ecological restoration, but it is not reducible to planting trees.

The domain is deliberately explored as a distinct axis. It should not be treated merely as an endpoint for waste management or as a justification for material interventions.

**Primary question:**

> **What material, institutional, and social conditions allow ecological systems to regenerate, adapt, and sustain the capabilities of living communities?**

**Objects of study include:** forests; wetlands; waterways; soils; habitats; ecological corridors; restoration sites; tree systems; pollution pathways; biodiversity; ecological memory; environmental stewardship; and the social capabilities needed to care for ecosystems.

**Core measures include:** ecological function; habitat condition; connectivity; water and soil quality; species or structural indicators; regeneration; disturbance; restoration persistence; protection; local stewardship; and unequal access to ecological benefits.

Ecological Flourishing must not be inferred from the existence of a park, a tree-planting project, or a waste-diversion claim. It requires evidence of ecological condition, trajectory, function, and persistence.

## 4. The relationship among the domains

The domains form a **three-domain field**, not a simple linear ladder.

```text
                         ECOLOGICAL FLOURISHING
                    regeneration / living capability
                                  ▲
                                  │
                    care / restoration / recovery
                                  │
        MATERIAL AFTERLIVES ◄──► FREEDOM FROM WASTE
       reuse / repair / exchange     residual burdens / exposure
            before disposal          and failed containment
```

Materials may move between domains in many ways. A reusable object may remain in Material Afterlives, become waste, or be repaired into a new afterlife. Composting may reduce a waste burden while creating a possible nutrient relation with ecological systems. A landfill may contain waste while creating long-term ecological obligations. Restoration may require materials, labour, finance, and maintenance that themselves have afterlives and residuals.

The bridge between the domains is therefore not “circularity” alone. It is **accountable transition**:

> **A material transition is beneficial only when it preserves or creates use, reduces burdens, protects ecological capability, and does not conceal or export new residuals.**

## 5. Shared conceptual grammar

All three domains use a common grammar, but each domain gives the terms a different emphasis.

| Shared term | Meaning in the project |
|---|---|
| **Material** | Matter with a source, composition, quality, location, history, and possible use or burden |
| **Flow** | Movement of material, energy, money, labour, information, responsibility, or ecological effect |
| **Stock** | Material, equipment, value, burden, knowledge, or ecological condition accumulated over time |
| **Transformation** | A change in composition, quality, form, function, ownership, or relation |
| **Boundary** | The spatial, temporal, institutional, and material limits of an analysis |
| **Capacity** | What a functioning arrangement can actually receive, transform, preserve, contain, monitor, or sustain under stated conditions |
| **Dissipation** | Specified loss of material quality, useful potential, information, organization, or burden containment; never a single universal score |
| **Afterlife** | A subsequent phase of use, repair, exchange, recovery, transformation, or residuality |
| **Receiver** | A person, community, worker, institution, ecosystem, or future generation that experiences a flow or burden |
| **Persistence** | Whether a condition, intervention, or ecological function continues through time |
| **Evidence status** | Observed, measured, estimated, reported, planned, historical, disputed, inaccessible, or unknown |

## 6. Thermodynamic alignment

Anavaratha studies **organized cycles under unavoidable dissipation**. Human settlements and facilities are open systems: they take in materials, energy, labour, information, money, and ecological services; they transform them; and they export heat, degraded materials, residuals, emissions, burdens, and information gaps.

Thermodynamics contributes discipline in five areas: conservation, boundary definition, quality, irreversibility, and unavoidable throughput. It does not supply a single social theory of goodness.

- In **Freedom from Waste**, the relevant concern is uncontrolled or harmful dissipation: mixing, leakage, degradation, residual formation, exposure, and burden export.
- In **Material Afterlives**, the relevant concern is preservation of function, separability, information, repairability, and recoverability before quality is dissipated.
- In **Ecological Flourishing**, the relevant concern is the maintenance and regeneration of living organization under material and energy flows.

“Negentropy” may be used only as a cautious metaphor for maintained differentiation, information, memory, feedback, repair, and organization. It is not a literal substance in a database, object, institution, or forest.

The project must distinguish thermodynamic entropy, information-theoretic entropy, material dispersal, organizational dissipation, and social injustice. Their relations may be studied, but they are not identical quantities.

## 7. Evidence and method by domain

| Domain | Primary evidence | Main analytical unit | Main danger |
|---|---|---|---|
| Freedom from Waste | Facility records, waste-flow data, licences, inspections, complaints, environmental observations, GIS pathways | Facility, route, receiver, local authority, burden chain | Treating removal from a visible place as resolution |
| Material Afterlives | Asset records, condition, ownership, repair, exchange, demand, transaction and reuse histories | Object, household, institution, repair network, exchange system | Calling any resale or recycling circular and beneficial |
| Ecological Flourishing | Ecological condition, land-cover/time series, hydrology, biodiversity, soil/water indicators, restoration records | Habitat, catchment, corridor, restoration site, community–ecosystem relation | Equating activity or planting with flourishing |

Each domain must maintain separate material, capability, burden, knowledge, time, and persistence ledgers. A combined model may connect them only through explicit flows and documented evidence.

## 8. Shared desk-first method

The project descends from an eagle-view conceptual model to local validation only after each higher layer is understood.

```text
Ontology and philosophy
          ↓
Three-domain material-cycle model
          ↓
Sri Lankan national and institutional architecture
          ↓
Domain-specific evidence and stream cycles
          ↓
Capacity, power, time, space, and ecological receivers
          ↓
Integrated system typology
          ↓
Controlled case selection
          ↓
Remote and documentary verification
          ↓
Field validation and bounded intervention
```

A named locality is not selected because it is interesting, visible, or anecdotal. It is selected because it tests a prior system type or hypothesis, has sufficient documentary and spatial evidence, and can be validated without converting uncertainty into accusation.

## 9. Three-domain research questions

### Freedom from Waste

- Where are material burdens produced, contained, transferred, accumulated, or made invisible?
- Which facilities possess nominal assets but lack usable capability?
- What happens to rejects, residues, leachate, ash, stock, and temporary overflow?
- Which groups and ecosystems receive burdens when systems adapt under constraint?
- Which interventions reduce exposure without displacing it?

### Material Afterlives

- Which objects leave first use while retaining meaningful utility?
- What information is lost when objects become surplus, obsolete, or abandoned?
- Which infrastructures connect holders, repairers, buyers, borrowers, and recipients?
- When does reuse preserve value, and when does it externalize labour, risk, or pollution?
- How long do second lives persist, and what happens at the end of them?

### Ecological Flourishing

- What conditions allow ecosystems to regenerate rather than merely absorb disturbance?
- How do material and waste pathways alter ecological capability over time?
- Which restoration or stewardship practices persist after projects end?
- Who receives ecological benefits and who bears conservation restrictions or costs?
- What forms of knowledge and care support living systems without reducing them to services?

## 10. Practical contribution

Anavaratha is not intended to replace public authorities, build every facility, or solve all material problems. Its practical leverage is to make hidden relations legible, identify binding constraints, preserve information, connect actors, trace burden transfer, support repair and reuse, and test modest interventions whose persistence can be measured.

The common sequence is:

> **make visible → define the boundary → distinguish capacity from outcome → trace the middle and the residual → identify power and receivers → test a modest leverage point → verify persistence.**

## 11. Final identity

> **Anavaratha is a Sri Lanka–focused material-cycle intelligence and burden-justice project with three linked domains: Freedom from Waste, Material Afterlives, and Ecological Flourishing. It studies how materials, institutions, people, and ecosystems preserve or lose capability through cycles of use, disuse, circulation, transformation, residuality, and regeneration.**
